# Gregory the Decapolite

Gregory was born in one of the cities of the Decapolis, the one called Irenopolis. His father Sergius was held by the passions of the body and took no care at all for his own salvation; his mother Maria was devout, a lover of God and of her children, and doxologia's life calls her the good root from which this great Gregory grew. Set to his letters at eight years old, he learned what he needed and then ran always to church, doing with his hands whatever profitable word he heard there.

He would eat nothing rich, only plain food and little of it, though his parents pressed him to eat and be merry with them; he wore poor clothes when they wanted to dress him in fine ones, remembering that those who wear soft clothing are in kings' houses. He was skilled at many kinds of handwork and lived off them, giving what was left over to the poor. When he came of age and his parents moved to marry him against his will, he slipped away from the world, from them and from all his kindred, and went to a monastery.

The abbot there was a bishop who had lately left his own see rather than be forced by the iconoclasts out of the true dogmas, and who was living in the mountains and the caves. Seeing the young man burning in spirit, he confirmed him in his purpose and sent him to monks who lived in a very quiet and hidden place, so that he might learn the monastic labours better. Not long after, Gregory's father died, and his mother searched every monastery, skete and wilderness until she found her son.

She did not grieve or hinder him, but praised him and told him to be great-souled and bold in the labours of monks. She asked one thing only: that he go to a community nearby where a brother of his was, so that the two might struggle together. Gregory went — and found that its abbot loved the iconoclasts and was one of them. He rebuked him before the whole brotherhood, saying he was not worthy to shepherd so many reasoning sheep; the abbot, filled with anger, had him beaten without mercy, and he received the blows as a heavenly dew and thanked God.

So he left, carrying the wounds on his body, and came to another monastery whose abbot Symeon was a kinsman of his mother and archimandrite over all the monasteries of two cities. From there his life became a long journeying: Ephesus, a winter in Asia, the island of Proconnesus when he had meant to go to Constantinople to denounce the iconoclasts and was hindered, Thessalonica and its abbot Mark, Corinth, and a passage to Rhegium in a ship whose sailors feared the Arab pirates until he told them to take courage.

At Rome he kept a small cell three months and let nobody know him, until a man he freed from a demon made him known and he fled the honour; then Syracuse in Sicily, where he shut himself in a tower of the city and the demons burnt the mat he slept on and came at him as a crowd of great mice, and were driven off by his prayer. He came to Thessalonica a second time and lived in the monastery of Saint Menas with no food or covering but the one garment he wore day and night.

God gave him foreknowledge. A monk named Athanasius, who told the story himself, came to be blessed for a journey to Constantinople and was told what he would do before he did it. His disciple [John](/saints/john-disciple-of-gregory-the-decapolite), a reverent man, told how he lost the saint on a road and could not find him anywhere, and then saw him coming behind. A count under sentence of death from the governor of Thessalonica was delivered from the prison by Gregory's pleading, though nobody had asked him and he had heard nothing of the case.

In the town of Avlon, near Thessalonica, he fell into a hard sickness. His uncle and elder Symeon, whom Leo the Armenian had shut in prison for confessing the veneration of the icons and who was then free, wrote begging to see him once more in this life. Old, worn out by his labours and by the illness, Gregory set out and came slowly to Constantinople; the two met and comforted each other, and Symeon went back to his monastery while Gregory stayed in the city.

He bore the sickness a whole year and sought no medicine, being ready for death. Then he told the brethren who were with him to carry him to the guest-house, for his end would come after twelve days — and after twelve days, on the twentieth day of the month of November, he gave up his soul to God, come to deep old age. As they carried him to burial a man bent double was straightened by touching the bier; the brethren, judging it wrong to leave such a treasure in the earth, took the coffin up again.

Healings did not stop at his relics: a man with a cancer in his leg and another with an old wound were anointed with what flowed from the coffin; a woman three days in labour was anointed with oil from his lamp and bore her child without pain; a monk Peter, taken captive and condemned with many others, was told in a dream *Do not fear, Peter, you will not die*, and all the prisoners were delivered. The relics stayed long in the monastery where he died.

Then, doxologia's life says, the pious ban Barbu Craioveanul, of the line of the Basarabs who filled the Romanian Land with monasteries, having built the Monastery of Bistrița in Vâlcea on the water of the Bistrița in honour of the Dormition of the Most Holy Theotokos, brought the relics there at great cost and laid them in the right-hand part of the church. There they remain, whole and uncorrupt, giving healings of every sickness to those who come with undoubting faith.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 20 November, and fills the Romanian line out. saint.gr makes him a ninth-century man of Irenopolis in the Decapolis, and says he owed his Christian upbringing first of all to his mother Maria, who brought her son up by the commands of the Gospel out of her own living faith. He became a monk and fought hard for moral perfection, and what marked him out was the cultivation of self-restraint, which he held necessary for a clean mind and for mastery over the flesh; asked why he laid such weight on that one virtue, he would answer with Saint Paul, that every man who contends is temperate in all things, they for a corruptible crown and we for an incorruptible. He did not stop at the monastic life either: he was close in the hard struggles against the iconoclast emperors, made many journeys, settled at last at Thessalonica in the monastery of Saint Menas, gave himself to writing, and died of a heavy illness at Constantinople in 816.
