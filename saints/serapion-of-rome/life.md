# Serapion of Rome

Serapion was one of three Roman martyrs — [Hermas](/saints/hermas-of-rome),
Serapion and [Polyaenus](/saints/polyaenus-of-rome) — who suffered for
Christ in the second century. They were known, the Orthodox Church in
America’s life says, for their zeal in spreading the faith and answering the
pagans’ arguments; arrested and brought before the authorities, they held to
their confession, were beaten without mercy and thrown into a dark and foul
prison, where hunger and hardship did nothing to shake them. Taken out and
bound with ropes, they were dragged by the mob over rough ground full of
sharp stones, and died of it — striking against the rocks and ledges, the
Sretensky calendar’s life says, in narrow and impassable places — their
flesh torn and their heads covered in wounds, and their souls untouched. The
Russian calendar keeps the three on 18 August (Julian).

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 18 August with his two companions, and doxologia.ro prints one life for all three. It says [Polyaenus](/saints/polyaenus-of-rome), Serapion and [Hermas](/saints/hermas-of-rome) came from the city of Rome and were unyielding in confessing and preaching the right faith against the error of idolatry. Denounced to the chief men of the city and refusing to deny Christ, they were cruelly beaten and thrown into a dark and foul prison; and when they remained unmoved in their hope, they were brought out and dragged by the crowd, bound with ropes, over ground full of sharp stones, until their bodies were broken, and their souls rose to Christ the eternal Bridegroom.

The Greek calendar keeps the three on the same day, 18 August, and says plainly where they were from and how they died. saint.gr calls them children of Rome, notes the variant Ερμής for the name of the first, and says they were marked out by their zeal in spreading the faith and in standing against the polemic of the idolaters.

They were denounced to the authorities and stayed firm in the confession of their faith. A series of savage tortures waited for them: first they were beaten without pity, then thrown into a dark and foul prison and worn down there with many deprivations and hardships, and nothing managed to shake them. Then they were brought out, bound tight with ropes, and dragged by the mobs over broken ground full of many sharp stones, so that their flesh was torn to pieces and their heads were filled with wounds and blood; but their souls, untouched, flew to Christ the Saviour, to rest with him for ever.

The page gives each of the three a synaxarion distich of his own, all three built on the same stones: of Serapion it says «Τὸν ἐκ πέτρας πάνδεινον ἑλκυσμὸν φέρει, Καὶ Σεραπίων, ὁ πλέον στερρὸς πέτρας» — Serapion too bears the all-dreadful dragging over rock, he who is firmer than the rock itself. It prints no hymn for them.
