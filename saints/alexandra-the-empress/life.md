# Alexandra the Empress

Alexandra, the martyr of Christ God, was the wife of the emperor Diocletian. Seeing that Saint George was tormented toward death in every manner and yet remained, by a wonder, alive and whole, she came forward before the emperor while he was sacrificing to the idols and confessed herself a Christian; and for that she was put into prison.

Sentence was then given that the two of them, George and Alexandra, should be beheaded. When she learned the emperor's sentence, she prayed to God there in the prison and gave up her soul, and so did not come to the sword.

Apollo, Isaacius and Codratus, who were of the empress's household servants, had seen her despise a temporal and corruptible empire and the emperor who tormented her, and believe in Christ and die for him; and they believed also. They came forward boldly and reproached the emperor, calling him a breaker of the law and a savage, who had not pitied even his own wife, with whom he had had children.

He was angered at it and had them shut up, and thought all that night by what death he would destroy them. In the morning he had Codratus beheaded, and sent Apollo and Isaacius back to prison to be killed with hunger; and after some days, worn out by it, they gave their souls into the hands of the Lord. The calendar keeps only Alexandra by name on this day, and gives her no year and no troparion.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, and saint.gr's account gives her the whole scene. Alexandra was the wife of the emperor Diocletian, and altogether unlike him: where he was harsh in his feelings and a friend of violence and of blood, she was marked by a calm disposition, by compassion and by a charitable life. During the martyrdom of [George the Trophy-bearer](/saints/george-the-trophy-bearer) the divine mercy counted her worthy to feel within her the breath and the rush of faith in Christ. Turning to the emperor, she begged him to order the tortures stopped; he supposed that the empress, unused to such spectacles, had been seized by an unthinking pity, and told her to withdraw — and got for answer, loudly, that such a scene was inhuman and unworthy of the crown.

When the emperor reviled the Name of Christ she declared in a manly voice that she denounced the persecutors of the Christians before the true God and confessed her own faith in Jesus Christ. He tried to interpret her declaration as a disturbance of mind; she protested and repeated the confession; and Diocletian was enraged — while he sought to wipe out the Christians, their conquest had entered the palace itself, and the queen was openly of their faith and their advocate. So he ordered her carried off and imprisoned.

In the prison she passed the night praying for herself and beseeching the Lord for His Church, which was so violently shaken. For her own life she took no thought at all; she even desired a martyr's death, but hesitated — knowing that for her husband there was no mercy before God the just judge, she did not want his guilt for the crime burdened with her own murder as well, and she prayed to the Most High to take her soul out of that prison and to be merciful to her for having watched the persecutions of the Christians so long unmoved at the persecutor's side. Her prayer was heard: two days before the death of George, in the year 303, she gave up her last breath in the prison. Three of her followers, [Apollos](/saints/apollos-companion-of-alexandra), [Isaacius](/saints/isaacius-companion-of-alexandra) and [Codratus](/saints/codratus-companion-of-alexandra), followed her example, and the Church keeps their memory on her own day.
