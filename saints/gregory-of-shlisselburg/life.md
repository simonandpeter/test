# Gregory (Lebedev)

Gregory was born in the ancient town of Kolomna in Moscow province on
12 (24) November 1878; his father, the archpriest Alexis Mikhailovich
Lebedev (1843–1914), served at the Dormition convent in Kolomna, and his
mother Maria was the daughter of the local priest Theodore Ostroumov. The
infant was named Alexander; the family was large — four brothers and
three sisters — and the good and devout mother planted in the boy’s soul
the seeds of faith and prayer, but died early of pneumonia, and his
upbringing fell wholly to his father, who strove to instil the fear of God
and the wish to keep the commandments, and took the boy with him to the
convent, where he spent whole days under the nuns’ eye and gladly attended
the long monastic services. He finished the Kolomna church school with
distinction, winning prizes every year for learning and conduct, and at
the local seminary the rector made him typicon-master and canonist to the
end of his course, which drew him close to the grace of the services;
serious, collected, and yet sociable and attentive, he was loved and
respected by his fellows, and loved solitude too, walking every summer
from Moscow to the Trinity-Sergius Lavra and from Kolomna to Golutvin and
the Saviour monastery of the Ryazan diocese. Finishing the seminary in
1898, he prepared for the Moscow Academy as a novice at the Bobrenev
monastery, but judging himself unready broke off the entrance examination
and went instead to Kazan, where as a novice of the Transfiguration
monastery he audited and then, from 1899, studied at the Kazan Academy,
finishing in 1903 in the first rank. The Synod made him teacher of
homiletics and liturgics at the Simbirsk seminary, where he taught four
years; in Moscow he taught at the Cadet Corps, the third gymnasium and the
Nicholas Orphan Institute, which in 1918 became the 165th Moscow labour
school, which he for a time headed, and in 1919 he ran the postal section
of the Chief Forestry Committee. Feeling another calling he left Moscow,
and in the Nativity fast was tonsured Gregory by Bishop Bartholomew
(Remov) at the Zosima hermitage of the Vladimir diocese, his first months
under the elder Mitrophan; passing to the Danilov monastery in Moscow he
came under Bishop, later Archbishop, Theodore (Pozdeevsky), and rose there
from hierodeacon to archimandrite. On 19 November (2 December) 1923
Patriarch Tikhon consecrated him bishop; at his nomination he said his one
task was to stay in the channel of God’s providence, to give himself to
God wholly and without reasoning, going forward without looking back,
with one word on his tongue, the word of silence. Tikhon sent him to
Petrograd as bishop of Shlisselburg, vicar of the metropolis — I am
sending you a pearl, he told them — and superior of the Alexander Nevsky
Lavra, at the height of the Renovationists’ assault, against which he
stood as a firm defender of Orthodoxy. As superior he cared above all for
the traditions of monastic piety, and drew close to the hieromonk Barnabas
(Muravyov), the future Seraphim of Vyritsa, with whom his communion grew
into a true spiritual friendship; at the services his face kept an
unearthly stillness while he burned in prayer. His sermons of 1924–1927
survive: on the parable of the wedding feast he begged his hearers to make
their choice while conscience still spoke and they could still tell light
from darkness, and hurry to secure a place at God’s supper; on the
talents, looking at our sad earth where God’s gifts are forgotten and life
ends in the bankruptcy of illusions, he could only cry, woe to us; and on
the Gadarene demoniac he told them they stood on the shore of life by
God’s almighty power, and that whoever bore the storm of evil and kept the
divine seed in himself would make evil powerless. The FSB archive records
two arrests in Petrograd: in December 1924, as superior, for non-payment
of taxes and the sale of the Lavra’s vestry, on which he bore himself
bravely, served a short term and was freed on 17 April 1925; and on 31
March 1927 in the group case of the pastoral school, under article 58-10,
for supposedly founding a circle of “zealots of true Orthodoxy” to
organise protests at church closures — dropped for want of compromising
material. He came out on 19 November 1927 into the growing dispute over
Metropolitan Sergius’s declaration of July 1927, which he did not accept,
commemorating at the services only the locum tenens Metropolitan Peter;
holding to the principle of not following personalities, he shared
Metropolitan Joseph’s position in general without joining his group, and
tried to keep normal relations with Sergius, to whom on 28 February 1928
he wrote a sincere letter asking to be relieved of the Lavra and of the
Shlisselburg and Detskoye Selo vicariate and retired: four and a half
years of almost unbroken suffering had taught him to walk only by his
conscience before God, and now his conscience was at peace — let the Lord
judge me; I leave the Lavra to which I gave my soul, where so many tears
were shed and so much grief lived through, and where the merciful hand of
God was felt; let them forgive me, and understand that I can do nothing
else. He went home to Kolomna and worked at theology, above all his
“Gospel of St Mark: spiritual reflections”, which shows every Gospel
truth addressed to each soul, and that only God can change human
nothingness at the root and breathe in new life by the cleansing,
creative power of the Spirit. Some months of 1932–1933 he spent at
Zhavoronki outside Moscow until pressure on his landlady cost him the
room; in the spring of 1933 he settled at the old town of Kashin, in
theological work and the care of the spiritual children who came to him,
whom his letters show him guiding as a tender, loving and wise father
along the way of the Christian struggle: the way after Christ is a cross,
an unbroken labour, a pitiless violence on oneself — do not look to life
for roses, rest or complacent enjoyment; love the saving sorrow, the
unavoidable road to God; whoever goes to Christ is under his hand and need
not fear being forgotten. In what was happening to him he saw God’s will,
and took it as only wise and merciful. At Kashin he went to the cathedral
of the princess Anna but seems to have served at home — his episcopal
vestments and a great many candles were seized at his arrest, on 16 April
1937, under articles 58-10 and 11, as leader of a fascist-monarchist
organisation in the town. He rejected the charge at the first
interrogation and admitted no guilt; questioned three times in April and
May he named no one and gave the investigators no handle, and after the
last interrogation on 10 May, convinced of his firmness, they did not call
him again. On 13 September 1937 the troika of the Kashin NKVD sentenced him
with fifty others to be shot, and four days later, on 17 September 1937 —
4 September of the old style, the day the Russian calendar keeps him — he
received the martyr’s crown. He was glorified in 2000, and is also kept in
the synaxes of the saints of St Petersburg, Tver and Moscow.
