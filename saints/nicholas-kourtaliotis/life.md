# Nicholas Kourtaliotis

Nicholas, gifted with an exceptional grace of wonderworking, lived the
ascetic life in southern and central Crete in the middle or second half of
the seventeenth century, and his death is placed around 1670. What is known
of him survives only in the island’s oral tradition, above all in the
province of Agios Vasileios in the prefecture of Rethymno, where the
Kourtaliotiko gorge runs and the river Kourtaliotis flows through it to the
Libyan Sea. In the middle of the gorge, near its floor, stands his small
chapel, reached down a stair of many stone steps, and near it, tradition
says, is his grave, not yet uncovered.

The people’s own synaxarion of him has the first signs of his holiness in
childhood, in almsgiving: he gave the poor the stores of his father’s house
— beans and oil are named — and yet, for all his father’s worry, the jars
were always found full, so that his father blessed him to follow the
ascetic life if he wished. He went off to the mountains, forests and gorges
of Crete as a boy or a youth, and living as a hermit reached great heights
of holiness; part of his life he spent on the plain of Messara in the
prefecture of Heraklion. Shortly before his death he came back to the
province of Agios Vasileios, where he had been born — probably at the
village of Asomatos, in today’s municipality of Phoinikas — bringing with
him a man of Messara who, in one version of the story, had wounded him
mortally by mistake while hunting with his bow, and had carried him there
at the saint’s own wish. Near the spot where the chapel now stands the man
grew thirsty, and the saint, stretching out his hand, worked a great sign
from God: five springs of abundant water, in the order and proportion of
the fingers of a hand, burst from the wall of the gorge, and flow to this
day. The chapel itself, the tradition says, was built in the Turkish time by
an Ottoman who had mocked the saint’s memory, was punished by him to teach
him, and was restored by a miracle; it is one of the best-loved shrines of
the district, under the parish of Asomatos. Though the saint has never been
recognised by a formal act of the Ecumenical Patriarchate, he is in the
Cretan calendar of saints and his memory is kept with great splendour on
1 September; a service to him was printed at Athens in 1879.
