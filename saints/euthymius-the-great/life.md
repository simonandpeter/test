# Euthymius the Great

Euthymius was the son of Paul and Dionysia, people of good family and adorned with every virtue after God, whose home was Melitene, the famous metropolis of the Armenians. Dionysia was barren, and the two of them prayed long for a child at the church of the martyr Polyeuctus near the city, until one night a vision told them to be glad, for God had given them a son named for gladness, and through his birth would give gladness to his Churches. So when the child was born they called him Euthymius, which is interpreted *gladness*, and vowed to dedicate him to God; he was born, the life says, in the month of August, in the fourth year of the rule of Gratian.

The vision was true, the life adds, for the holy Churches had been forty years in grief under the Arian emperors, and Euthymius, whose name is gladness, turned the troubles of the Churches into gladness. His father died three years after his birth, and his mother's brother Eudoxius, a scholastic and a counsellor to Otreius, who then governed the Church of Melitene and shone at the holy council in Constantinople, brought the child to the bishop and dedicated him to God, as Hannah once brought Samuel. Otreius, hearing of the vision given to his parents and of the vow made before his conception, marvelled and said: «Truly the Spirit of God has rested upon this young man.»

He came in time into Palestine, and his name was soon heard through the whole province and the eparchies about it for the healings God worked through him; and because he loved stillness he drew away from the crowds who pressed on him, so that his settlements grew between one monastery and another as men followed him into the desert. The life gives his part in the defence of the Council of Chalcedon, when a certain Theodosius had misled the empress Eudocia and many of the monks; in his eighty-second year the young Sabbas came and asked to stay with him, and in his ninetieth his fellow-labourer Theoctistus died and was buried by him.

Among the other gifts given to the great Euthymius was this, the life says: to know beforehand the day of his falling asleep and what would happen to his place. He told his brethren that God had willed the lavra to become a coenobium, and appointed where the buildings should stand; then he dismissed them all but Dometian, and, staying in the vestry through the night of the Sabbath, fell asleep and went to his fathers, old and full of days. A countless multitude of monks and laymen gathered, and Archbishop Anastasius came down to the lavra with clergy, laity and soldiers; the patriarch sent the stone for the tomb and what else was needed, and came himself to lay the body in it. Dometian, his disciple, who had served him more than fifty years, would not leave the coffin for six days, and in the week after Euthymius appeared to him by night.
