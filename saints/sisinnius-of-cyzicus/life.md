# Sisinnius of Cyzicus

saint.gr's calendar for 23 Νοεμβρίου names «Όσιος Σισίνιος ο Ομολογητής» and gives him a short life. He was distinguished in the persecution against the Christians when Diocletian was emperor, in 300 by the page's reckoning. He came from Cyzicus and preached the Christian truth with courage and daring, and for that work he suffered many imprisonments and merciless beatings.

When Constantine the Great became emperor, Sisinnius was set free and went on with his pious struggle, well-pleasing to God. But when the heresy of Arius broke out he took part in the fight as a champion of the Orthodox doctrine; and so, contending bravely, he finished a life devoted with his whole soul to Christ. The couplet set over the line turns on the word for force: having endured many violences first in his life, Sisinnius passed out of the violence of life.

The page gives him no year of birth or of death, no see, no relics and no hymn; the two emperors and the heresy are the whole of its dating.

**The Russian calendar keeps him on the same day and gives him a different persecution, a named persecutor and a manner of death.** The Сретенский monastery’s calendar names him fifth of the twelve of the Julian 23 November, «Сщмч. Сисиния, еп. Кизического (III),», the third century, and hangs behind his name page a notice of two sentences: «Святой мученик Сисиний, епископ Кизический, пострадал за Христа в царствование Диоклитиана (284–305) от правителя города Кизика Александра. После многих и тяжелых пыток мученик был обезглавлен» — he suffered for Christ in the reign of Diocletian at the hands of Alexander the governor of the city of Cyzicus, and after many and heavy tortures was beheaded.

**That is not the account this folder was made on.** saint.gr’s notice for the same day has him live well-pleasing to God and then, when the heresy of Arius broke out, take part in the fight as a champion of the Orthodox doctrine and so finish a life devoted wholly to Christ — a fourth-century death and no beheading. The Russian gives a third-century reign, a named governor and an execution. The two sources agree on the man’s name, his see and his day and on nothing else about his end; both stand here, each attributed, and the folder’s `floruit` of about 300 is left where the Greek reading put it, being the one point the two accounts can both be read against.
