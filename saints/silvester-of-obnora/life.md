# Silvester of Obnora

Silvester of Obnora was born in the fifteenth century in Russia, and from a young age had the longing to be a monk within him. So he left the world and took refuge in the monastery of the Holy Trinity, where he was a novice and the subordinate of [Sergius of Radonezh](/saints/sergius-of-radonezh); and having stayed there a while and got on in obedience, he received the blessing to remain alone in the wilderness and to carry on his ascetic struggle there.

In the thick forest, beside the river Obnora which runs on into the Kostroma, he set up a cross at the spot he had chosen and began his spiritual struggle from there. For a long time nobody knew anything of the holy hermit. His cell was discovered by accident by a peasant who had lost his way, and who told the disturbed hermit that people had seen bright rays and a layer of cloud above his dwelling. Silvester broke into bitter tears that the place of his solitude had been found out; and when the pilgrim pressed him to speak about himself, he said that he had lived there a long while eating the bark of trees and roots, and that at first he was weak without bread, and once fell to the ground from weakness — and then an angel in the form of a wonderful man appeared before him and touched him on the hand, and from that moment he never felt weakness again.

Another time the peasant came to him again and brought him bread and flour, so that he should have provisions; and that one meeting was enough for the saint's spiritual achievements to be widely known. Soon peasants began to come to him from the settlements round about, and Silvester allowed them to build cells near his own. When enough brethren had gathered in the skete he went to Moscow and asked the blessing of [Alexis](/saints/alexis-of-moscow) to build a church dedicated to the Resurrection of Christ; and the holy hierarch gave him an antimension for the celebration of the liturgy and made him abbot of the new monastery.

With the building of the church the number of the brethren grew quickly, and the saint very often withdrew into the nearby forest so as to be alone and pray. That place got the name of the forbidden grove, because Silvester ordered that no tree be cut there; in it he dug three wells, and a fourth on the side of a hill by the river Obnora. When he came back from his solitude many people would be waiting for him at the monastery, each wanting to receive his blessing and hear his counsel.

One day he fell ill of an incurable sickness, and the brethren of the monastery — who had been sorry when he went off into solitude — were sorrier still at the thought of his coming death. Do not be distressed at this, brethren, he said to comfort them, for everything is according to the will of God; follow the commandments of the Lord and do not be afraid to suffer trials in this life, and so you will receive your reward in heaven. If I find boldness before the Lord, and if my life has pleased Him, then this holy place will not be broken up after my going. Pray to the man-befriending God and to His all-pure Mother, that you may be freed from temptation. He died in the year 1479, and was buried on the right-hand side of the church of the Resurrection.

The Russian calendar keeps him on this same day and tells the life in almost the same words, but it carries one thing further. A record of his wonders survives from the year 1645 in which twenty-three of them are described, and above all healings of possession — twelve such — and of derangement of the mind and of diseases of the eyes, six of those. One wonder of that same year is set down for the lesson in it. A hieromonk of the monastery, «Иеромонах обители Иов», ordered the peasants to cut the forbidden grove for firewood and was struck blind for it; four weeks later he understood his sin, repented, and promised to act no more by his own will but to do everything by the counsel of the brethren. He had a service of intercession sung in the church, venerated the reliquary, and received his sight.
