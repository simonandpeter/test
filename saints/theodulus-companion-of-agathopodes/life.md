# Theodulus, companion of Agathopodes

Theodulus was a reader of the church at Thessalonica in the reign of Diocletian and Maximian, young and fair of face, living in purity and without blame, the son of honourable Christian parents; his brothers in the flesh were Capito, Metrodorus and Philostorgius, all of them right-believing. The deacon [Agathopodes](/saints/agathopodes-the-martyr), an old man adorned with grey hairs and very wise, was the other of the pair whom the calendar keeps together on this day.

Before the contest Theodulus was given a sign of the crown he would receive. Resting one night, he seemed in a dream to take something into his hand from an honourable person; and waking at once he found in his hands a very beautiful ring, worked of an unknown substance, whose seal bore the figure of the Holy Cross — the mark of suffering for the Lord Jesus, who suffered on the Cross for us. With that ring the young man healed every sickness among men: it was enough that Theodulus should meet someone who was ill, and the illness left him and health was given; and for this many of the Greeks turned to Christ.

When the persecution came to Thessalonica the two did not flee or hide, but stayed in the house of God, praying day and night for the Church. The soldiers seized them and threw them into prison, and Faustinus the governor tried to take the younger man by himself. He sent the others away, called Theodulus close and said in a friendly voice: hear me, young man, leave off this new Christian deceit and come to the old laws, lest you lose your life badly. Theodulus answered him with a cheerful face that he had long since escaped every deceit and error, and that he feared very much for the governor, who had loved vanity, lest he fall into the eternal death.

Threats of torture, he said, could not frighten him at all, nor bend him a little to their will; and when Faustinus asked whether an honourable life were not better than a cruel death, he answered that he knew it well, and had made up his mind to overlook this mortal life of few days on earth in order to share the deathless life and the eternal goods of heaven — torture me, then, he said, with fire and with beatings, and you will learn how the body under torture is a stranger to the soul. Marked with the Cross from his infancy, he would sooner be parted from his body by the governor's torments than from the Cross of Christ.

He was stripped for torture while the crier called on him to sacrifice, and he said that they had taken the clothes from his body but could never take his faith. Lapsed Christians were made to sacrifice in front of him, and it grieved his heart. Ordered to bring the Christian books to the court, he answered that if he had thought the governor would throw off the emptiness of idol-worship he would have brought him the books of the prophets and the apostles; but knowing his mind was treacherous, he would not put the divine gifts into his hands, nor give the holy books to the heathen to be mocked, though his body was broken and thrown to the beasts. Led out to the place of execution to frighten him, he saw the sword over him and cried: glory to thee, O God, Father of my Lord, who was well pleased to suffer for us.

At the third hearing the two answered nothing but that they were Christians and would suffer all things for the name of Christ, and Faustinus sentenced them to be drowned in the sea. The night before, they had both been shown the same dream — that they went aboard a ship full of people — and had understood that the martyrdom of the sea was given to them both, and that where many sink they would pass through delivered.

Their hands were bound behind them and heavy stones tied to their necks, and a crowd of friends and neighbours gathered on the shore; and when the acquaintances wept, Theodulus smiled and asked why they wept for men who were running towards better things instead of weeping for their own dangers. The governor sent a man of note, Fulvius, to tell them that a little incense would save them, and they went on calling on Jesus Christ. Agathopodes was thrown in first, calling that second baptism the washing of all their faults, and Theodulus after him.

The sea gave their bodies back in the same hour, put out on dry land by the waves, loosed from their bonds and from their stones and very bright; and those who knew them buried them with honour. Not long after, Theodulus appeared to them in white garments with a shining face, commanding that his goods be shared out to the destitute, to the orphans and to the widows. The two ended on the fifth day of the month of April.

The Greek calendar keeps the two of them on 4 April, and saint.gr adds the shape of their life together before the trial. It says they came from Thessalonica and lived in the first years of the persecutions; Agathopodes was the elder and Theodulus the younger, and Christian love joined them like brothers. They studied the Scriptures together, worked for the spreading of the Gospel, and wore themselves out in the service of their suffering and destitute brethren.

One night something strange happened: both saw the same dream, that they were travelling in a ship, that a storm came up suddenly and the ship broke in two, and that they were saved out of it and climbed a mountain whose summit reached to heaven. The next day the dream came true. They were arrested, and understood that waves of death were waiting for them; the governor Faustinus called on them to deny Christ, and both confessed him with courage, so they were thrown into the sea. Their corruptible bodies went to the bottom of it, the page says, but their souls went up to the throne of God.
