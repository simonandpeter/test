# Joseph the Hymnographer

Joseph's homeland was Sicily, and he was born of right-believing and virtuous parents, Plotinus and Agatha. He was brought up well in letters and in good habits, and even in his childish ways the man he was to become could be seen: he was gentle and humble, kept apart from children's games, and loved fasting and restraint from his earliest years, seeking no sweet food but eating bread and water only, and that in the evening. He went far in the learning of the divine Scriptures.

While he was still growing, barbarians fell upon part of Sicily and his parents had to flee their country. Joseph went with them first into the Peloponnese, and then, leaving his parents for God's sake, went on to Thessalonica, the metropolis of Macedonia. There he entered a monastery and was tonsured, and trained himself hard: the ground with a skin over it for a bed, a worn-out garment for clothing, a harsh fast, a small piece of bread and water by measure, the whole night in vigil, constant kneeling, singing and work, and the reading of the divine books.

He was among the brothers like an angel of God, and the abbot and the brothers compelled him to take the priesthood; the bishop of Thessalonica ordained him. When the venerable Gregory of Decapolis came to that monastery and saw him, he knew him for a holy man, loved him greatly and made him his friend and fellow-dweller. Afterwards, going to Byzantium to strengthen the faithful whom the heretics were troubling, Gregory asked the abbot and brothers for Joseph, and they gave him up unwillingly, weighing the need of a Church caught in that storm.

At Byzantium the two lodged at the church of the holy martyrs Sergius and Bacchus and wore themselves out in a hard life, Joseph following the older ascetic's example. They put on the breastplate of faith and went about the streets of the city, through the market and through the houses of the faithful, counselling and begging them not to be taken in by the heretics' craft; and their words were like the morning dew on flowers and grass withered by the heat of the day, for they revived hearts that were wilting in the heat of heretical teaching.

Then the monks asked Gregory to send Joseph to old Rome — as a man skilful in words and strong enough in body for the long road — to the orthodox Pope, who was then Leo the third, to tell him and all the West of the persecution at Constantinople, so that the orthodox there might help them and overturn the heretics' dogmas. Gregory told him it was the hour to show his zeal, and to tell the West how the icon of Christ and of his most pure Mother had been spat on and trodden, the Church stripped of its adornment, its good shepherds driven out unjustly and hirelings and wolves sent into the flock in their place.

Joseph, trained to obedience from his youth, said nothing against it, bowed his head, asked his father's prayers and blessing, and took ship. But God's hidden judgement willed otherwise. Those who held with the emperor Leo the Armenian in the heresy had learned that many were leaving the imperial city for other lands, and had set robbers on the sea for it; these fell on the ship, plundered it, took everyone who was with Joseph and sent them to Crete, to the persecutors of the icons.

There he was put in heavy chains and thrown into a prison where a multitude of others already lay bound for the same faith, and he comforted them day and night with words inspired by God, rejoicing for his Lord himself. What could be sweeter, he asked them, than these chains, with which we are bound for the love of Christ and for his holy icon? Paul counted his chains as ornaments of gold; Christ himself suffered for us and left us the example; and God, who could have saved man some other way, willed that the strength which opens the long-locked door of heaven should be born out of labours.

There was in that prison an orthodox bishop, persecuted like the rest for honouring the icons, who could not bear the hardship and was near to consenting to the heresy in order to be freed. Joseph spent words and tears on him, begging him to be ready to die for Christ's icon as for Christ himself; and the bishop was so strengthened that he did not deny his faith even under the worst torments, but stripped off his own clothes and gave himself willingly to the blows, and made a martyr's end. When Joseph heard of it he rejoiced and gave thanks to Christ, who had strengthened a soldier who had slipped a little and then set himself right and beaten the enemy.

He suffered in that prison six years. On the night of the feast of the Nativity of Christ the emperor Leo the Armenian was killed by his own soldiers in church at the singing of Matins, and in that same hour Joseph was told of the tyrant's end in his prison in Crete, and was loosed from his bonds by a wonder. Keeping the whole night without sleep, singing the hymns of the feast and praying, at cockcrow he saw a light shine in the prison and a man stand before him, grey-haired and bright of face, in a bishop's vestments, who said that he had come to him from Myra of Lycia, sent by God.

He gave him a written sheet; Joseph read it, swallowed it with joy and said how sweet those words were to his throat, and was told to sing them; and as he sang the chains and fetters fell from his neck, his hands and his feet. Rise and follow me, said Saint Nicholas; and he rose and went out, the doors opening of themselves, with the prison guards knowing nothing of it, and was carried through the air by an unseen divine power and found himself in a little while on the high road into Constantinople, glorifying God.

When he entered the city he did not find his beloved father Gregory of Decapolis among the living, but only John his disciple; he wept much that he had not been counted worthy to see him alive again, and stayed by his grave with father John. After a long while John too departed, and Joseph buried him near Gregory. Then he moved to a quiet place outside the city, not far from the church of Saint John Chrysostom, built there a church in the name of the hierarch Nicholas, brought the relics of both his fathers to it, founded a monastery and gathered brothers.

Going once into Thessaly, he obtained a good portion of the relics of the apostle [Bartholomew](/saints/bartholomew-the-apostle) and laid them with honour in his monastery. He often saw the apostle in dreams, and longed to adorn his feast with hymns of praise, but did not dare, doubting whether it would please him. He prayed for forty days with fasting and tears that he might be told, and at the Vespers of the feast he saw the apostle standing in the sanctuary in white, the curtain drawn back, calling him.

When Joseph came near, the apostle took the book of the holy Gospel from the divine table and laid it on his breast, saying: may the right hand of Almighty God bless you, and may heavenly waters of wisdom flow from your tongue; may your heart be made a throne of the Holy Spirit, and may the whole world be gladdened by your songs. With that he became invisible, and Joseph, filled with unspeakable joy and feeling the gift of wisdom in him, began from that hour to write church hymns and the odes of canons — for the apostle's feast, for many saints, and above all for the most pure Mother of God and for the hierarch Nicholas, so that he filled the Church with his songs and got from them his name.

Under Theophilus the storm of persecution rose against the icons again, and Joseph, for his bold and open reproof of the heresy, was banished to Cherson, where he lived in bonds and torments until that emperor's death. Then the empress Theodora, who reigned with her son Michael and had held the right faith with the most holy patriarch Methodius, called him back to Constantinople; and when Ignatius took the throne after Methodius, he made Joseph keeper of the vessels of the great Church of the imperial city.

After Bardas was killed by the imperial servants and the emperor Michael likewise lost his life at the hands of his own, Basil the Macedonian took the empire, and Ignatius and Joseph returned to Constantinople, the one to his throne and the other to his former honour. Photius, who followed Ignatius in the patriarchate, held Joseph dear as well, called him a man of God and an angel in the flesh and a father of fathers, entrusted the whole church order to him, and commanded all his clergy to have him as their spiritual father.

When he had lived many years and come to deep old age, his body was worn out with the labours he had kept from his youth — asceticism on the one hand, and on the other exile, bonds and prisons borne for righteousness. Near death he fell ill as the days of the Forty Days' Fast were ending, having been told of his end in sleep, and on Great Friday he wrote out all the vessels and goods of the church entrusted to him and sent them to the patriarch Photius, and made himself ready, thanking God for keeping him in the shadow of his wings all the days of his life.

On the night of Bright Wednesday, going into Bright Thursday, he died. A man who had been praying to a saint for help slept a little during a soul-profiting reading at Matins and saw that saint, who told him: why are you grieved? Joseph the hymnographer was parting from the body and I was beside him; and when he died this night, all of us escorted to the heavens that soul which honoured us with canons and with songs, and it went before the face of God — and that is why I was late in appearing to you.

The page's closing note dates him: he lived under the emperors Nicephorus, Michael Kouropalates, Leo the Armenian, Michael Balbus, Theophilus, and Michael his son who reigned with his mother Theodora, and he died in the reign of Basil the Macedonian, in the year 6391 from the making of the world and 883 from the incarnation, on the fourth day of April. The note also says plainly that there are two saints of this name who wrote hymns: the first is Joseph bishop of Thessalonica, the brother of Theodore the Studite, kept on 26 January, whose canons are headed «Facerea lui chir Iosif»; this Joseph, who was never a bishop but a hieromonk and abbot and keeper of the vessels of the Great Church, is the one whose canons carry no «chir».
