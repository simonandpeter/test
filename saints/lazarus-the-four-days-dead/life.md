# Lazarus the Four-days-dead

Lazarus was the brother of [Martha](/saints/martha-sister-of-lazarus) and [Mary](/saints/mary-sister-of-lazarus) and lived in the village of Bethany, not far from Jerusalem. During his earthly life, the Russian calendar's notice says, the Lord Jesus Christ often visited the house of Lazarus, whom he loved greatly and called his friend (John 11.3, 11.11); and when Lazarus had died and had lain four days in the tomb, the Lord raised him from the dead (John 11.17–44). The raising of Lazarus is remembered by the Church on the Saturday of the sixth week of Great Lent, which is called Lazarus Saturday.

Many of the Jews, hearing of it, came to Bethany, and having satisfied themselves of the reality of that greatest of miracles became followers of Christ; and for this the chief priests wanted to kill Lazarus. He is named in the Gospel once more: six days before the Passover the Lord came again to Bethany, and the raised Lazarus was there (John 12.1–2, 12.9–11). After the raising, the page says, the holy Lazarus lived thirty years more; he was bishop on the island of Cyprus, where he spread Christianity, and there he fell asleep in peace.

The relics of bishop Lazarus were found at Kition. They lay in a marble ark on which was written: «Лазарь Четверодневный, друг Христов» — Lazarus the Four-days-dead, the friend of Christ. The Byzantine emperor Leo the Wise, whose reign the page dates 886 to 911, ordered them brought to Constantinople in 898 and laid in a church of the name of the Righteous Lazarus. That translation is what the calendar keeps on 17 October, and it is the day this folder's two rows carry; his own name page gives him three days, 22 March, 4 May and 17 October.

The Serbian calendar keeps the same translation on the same day, in four lines, and differs from the Russian page on two things. It dates the bringing of the relics to the new church 890 rather than 898 — the emperor is the same Leo the Wise, who built the church at Constantinople for them — and it names his chief feast as 17 March and Lazarus Saturday, where the Russian name page gives 22 March and 4 May. Nothing read here reconciles either pair, so both stand.

Its last line is the inscription, told a little differently. When they dug up the grave of Lazarus in the city of Kition on Cyprus after nearly a thousand years, the Ohrid Prologue says, they found a marble slab on which was written: „Лазар Четвородневни пријатељ Христов.“ The same words, on a marble ark rather than a slab, are on the Russian page, which does not say how long after the burial the finding was.

The Russian calendar prints a troparion and a kontakion for him under this day, and both are hymns of the translation and not of the raising: the one calls him a great treasure and a wealth that cannot be stolen, come to us out of Cyprus at a pious emperor's command, and the other sees his honoured relics rise out of Cyprus like a most radiant star, sanctifying the Imperial City and gladdening the Christ-loving emperor. Neither calendar gives a year for his death, so it stands here as the first century: thirty years after the Passion, and no narrower.
