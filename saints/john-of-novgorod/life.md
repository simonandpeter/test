# John

John was born at Novgorod to Nicholas and Christina, and after their deaths he
and his brother Gabriel spent their inheritance on a small monastery of the
Annunciation, a wooden church first and then a stone one. The stone church
exhausted their means before it was finished; they prayed to the Mother of
God, who told them in a dream that what was needed would be provided, and in
the morning a splendid horse stood at the gate with two sacks of gold on its
back, and vanished when the sacks were taken off. The brothers took the
schema — John as Elias, Gabriel as Gregory — and in 1162 the chronicles
record John made bishop of Novgorod, in 1165 its first archbishop. His first
letter to his clergy survives: do not be too attached to this world, look
first that the people do not give themselves to drink, question those who
come to repentance with mildness and do not impose harsh penances, do not
scorn the reading of books, for the yoke of Christ ought to be light.

In the winter of 1170 Suzdal and its allies besieged the city. On the third
night, praying before an icon of the Saviour, John heard a voice telling him
to fetch the icon of the Mother of God from the church on Ilyina street and
carry it to the walls. The archdeacon sent for it could not move it; when
John and the whole assembly came and sang before it, at the kontakion
“Protectress of Christians” it moved of itself, and he carried it to the
wall with two deacons. When the assault began and the arrows fell like rain
the icon turned its face to the city and wept, John gathering the tears in
his phelonion, and a darkness fell on the Suzdal army, which fled in terror.
That was 25 February 1170, and he made it Novgorod’s own feast of the Sign.
He fed the ruined and the orphans afterwards, went to Vladimir in 1172 to
reconcile Andrew Bogolyubsky with the city, and left some thirty
instructions on baptism, confession and the eucharist, and a guidance for
monks — a monk ought always to be a monk, asleep and awake, keeping the
memory of death and fleshless in the flesh.

The life the Orthodox Church in America prints keeps the story Novgorod told
of him: a demon that splashed in his washbasin at night and, held there by
the sign of the cross, carried him to Jerusalem and back in one night; the
demon’s revenge, a woman seen running from his cell, and the crowd that put
him on a raft on the Volkhov — which sailed against the current to the
St George monastery, until the people followed along the bank weeping and
begged him back, and he prayed, Lord, do not hold this sin against them.
Not long after, he laid down the omophorion, took the schema as John again,
named his brother Gregory his successor, and died on 7 September 1186. His
relics were found incorrupt and unnamed in 1439, when a stone fell and
cracked his tomb in the cathedral of Holy Wisdom, and he told Euthymius in
a dream whose they were. The Greek synaxarion keeps him on 7 September in a
line — archbishop in 1162, asleep in 1186 — and says it has no further
details; the Russian calendar keeps him the same day of its own reckoning.
