# Niphon of Novgorod

saint.gr says Niphon was born in Russia and became a monk in the Lavra of the Caves at Kyiv in the days of the abbot Timothy. His one care was to adorn his soul with the virtues, and so he laboured at fasting, at vigil, at prayer and at a severe ascesis; and the report of a way of life so pleasing to God was not long in passing beyond the bounds of the Lavra. When in the year 1130 the bishop of Novgorod, [John](/saints/john-of-novgorod), resigned his see in order to be at rest in a monastery, Niphon was elected his successor.

As soon as he took up his duties he saw to the raising of churches and gave himself with holy zeal to the preaching of the Gospel; and many times he stepped in to make peace in the quarrels of the lords of that country, who were then in continual strife and civil war, for the good of the people and the profit of their souls. In his time the men of Novgorod rose up and drove out their prince Vsevolod Mstislavich, calling Svyatoslav Olgovich in his place; and when Svyatoslav took the rule he wanted to contract an unlawful marriage. Bishop Niphon not only refused to perform it, but forbade every priest of his diocese to bless the transgression.

The prince did not change his mind. He forced some priests he had brought with him to Novgorod to perform the rite, and even after that Niphon did not cease to rebuke him openly. The page dwells above all on his care that the Russian Church keep its canonical dependence on the Ecumenical Patriarchate. When the metropolitan Michael of Kyiv left for Byzantium for reasons unknown, the prince of Kyiv, Izyaslav Mstislavich, son of the great prince Mstislav Vladimirovich, called a synod of the Russian bishops and proposed that they elect the monk Clement Smolyatich the Philosopher metropolitan without the approval and blessing of the patriarch of Constantinople.

Most of the synod gave way to the strong prince's will. Niphon would not agree in any way, and afterwards refused to recognise Clement's election and consecration; whereupon Izyaslav forbade him to return to his diocese and confined him in the monastery of the Caves. The next year, however, George Monomachos, prince of Suzdal, took Kyiv, drove out Izyaslav and set Niphon free, and the just hierarch went back to Novgorod with honours. A little before his repose he saw in a vision [Theodosius of the Caves](/saints/theodosius-of-the-kyiv-caves); thirteen days later, on Bright Saturday of the year 1156, he fell asleep in peace and was buried in the monastery of the Caves beside the other relics of the fathers who had fallen asleep there.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same day and gives him four sentences, two of them things the Greek page has not. He was marked, it says, by a great zeal in the building and the repairing of the churches of God, and by a great courage in standing up to violent princes. Thirteen days before his death Saint Theodosius appeared to him and told him beforehand of his near passage into the other world; and he reposed in 1156. The thirteen days and the year are the ones already written above, and the Theodosius is the one already named there; the entry gives him no epithet of its own.

**The Russian church calendar keeps him on the Julian 8 April**, which is the Greek and the Serbian day, and its line is «Свт. Нифонта, еп. Новгородского (1156).» — the rank, the forename, the see and the year 1156 this folder already holds. **His name page, 1658, carries no life at all**: a title, two days of memory and four hymns, and nothing else, which is the one entry of this day where the calendar of record has the man and not his story. The title is «Святитель Нифонт Печерский, Новгородский, епископ», and it puts the Caves before Novgorod — the only book read for this folder that names both. The second day it lists is 28 сентября with the mark «(Печер.(Б))», the synaxis of the fathers of the Near Caves, which is a synaxis and not written into the feast.

**The life comes from azbyka.ru, which serves the Caves Paterikon for this day**, and it is the fullest account this folder has had. He was a black-robed monk of the Caves monastery in the days of the abbot the Greek page also names; and when his predecessor, «блаженный Иоанн», left of his own will the see he had laboured in twenty years and went for silence to a monastery, he was chosen by common consent and consecrated at Kyiv by the metropolitan «Михаилом» — «вторым этого имени», the second of that name, an ordinal no other book gives.

Three acts of his episcopate are new here: he laid and finished a stone church of the Most Holy Mother of God in the middle of Novgorod; he adorned the cathedral church of Holy Sophia throughout, narthex and all, with icon-painting, and covered its whole roof with lead; and when the men of Kyiv and the men of Chernigov had drawn up their hosts against each other he went to them with the nobles of Novgorod and made peace between them.

**The council is named bishop by bishop, which no other book does.** Nine sat: «Онуфрий Черниговский, Феодор Белогородский, Евфимий Переяславский, Дамиан Юрьевский, Феодор Владимирский, святой Нифонт Новгородский, Мануил Смоленский, Иоаким Туровский, Козьма Полоцкий»; and five held with him to the end — «Козьма Полоцкий, Иоаким Туровский, Мануил Смоленский, Евфимий Переяславский и Феодор Белогородский» — so the Greek page’s *most of the synod gave way* is here a count of four against six.

The page adds the thing that was used instead of a patriarch’s blessing: the man was blessed «главой святого Климента, папы Римского», with the head of a first-century pope kept as a relic at Kyiv. And it gives the refusal in his own words — that he would not serve with him nor commemorate him, because he did not commemorate the patriarch from whose throne the Russians had the beginning of their blessing.

**And it says why he was at Kyiv when he died**, which the Greek page does not. Hearing that a metropolitan «Константин» was on his way from Constantinople to depose the irregularly consecrated one, he went to Kyiv to take his blessing and to venerate in the Caves, and fell gravely ill there. **The reckoning of the days differs from both books above.** This page puts the vision three days before the illness began and the illness at thirteen days; the Ohrid Prologue puts the vision thirteen days before the death, and the Greek page thirteen days before it too.

The vision itself is given at length: he asked in church to see the good steward of the house, was taken into the sanctuary, fell at his feet, and was raised and blessed with the words that he was welcome and from now on would be with them inseparably; and he was given a scroll which began *Behold I and the children whom the Lord hath given me*. Also here: a letter from the patriarch of Constantinople numbering him with the ancient fathers who stood firm for Orthodoxy.

**The calendar keeps him on the day he died, and this is the book that says so.** «Итак, поболев тринадцать дней, уснул он с миром в Господе, месяца апреля в 8-й день, в субботу Светлой недели» — the eighth day of the month of April, on the Saturday of Bright Week, which is the Julian 8 April this day is. The stored `death` is narrowed from the bare 1156 to **8 April 1156** on the same `traditional` basis, the Greek page’s Bright Saturday of 1156 agreeing with it. **One thing contradicts the calendar’s own filing**: this life lays the body «в пещере преподобного Феодосия», in the cave of the great one among the fathers whose vision is told above, which is the Far caves — while the name page files his synaxis under the Near caves, and azbyka’s own list of his days does the same. Both are recorded and neither is chosen; no `locations` row follows, no page giving a coordinate.

**Four hymns, where the folder had none.** The day prints two troparia and two kontakia for him, in the third, the fourth, the fifth and the eighth tones, all four proper and all four naming him. The second troparion’s heading is printed «Ин тропарь святителя Нифонта, епископа Нивгородского», with the city misspelt, and the misspelling is recorded in that hymn’s note and not carried into the text. The first kontakion is the one that matters to the story above: it says he would not be concelebrant to the man who had seized the throne of the Metropolia of his own will, but was obedient to the one elected to it by all and served with him in a priestly manner — so the hymn has him end by concelebrating with the lawful metropolitan, which is a thing none of the three prose accounts says outright.
