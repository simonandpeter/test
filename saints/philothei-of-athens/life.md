# Philothei of Athens

Philothei was born at Athens in the year 1522. Her parents, Syriga and Angelos Benizelos, were marked out not so much by eminence and wealth as by their faith; and gentle Syriga had begged the Theotokos often for a child. Her prayers were heard, and the daughter born to them was baptised Revula. They brought her up in the right faith and in a deep piety until she was twelve years old, and then gave her in marriage.

Her husband proved an evil and unbelieving man, who beat and tormented her, and she bore it patiently, praying God that he might be mended. After three years he died, and Revula began to grow in the spirit by fasting, by vigil and by prayer. She founded a convent dedicated to the Apostle Andrew the First-Called, and when it was finished she was the first to receive the tonsure in it, under the name Philothei.

In those years Greece was under the Turkish yoke and many Athenians were made slaves by the invaders, and Philothei did everything she could to redeem as many of her brethren from slavery as possible. Once, four women who had escaped from their Turkish masters — who had demanded that they give up the Christian religion — took shelter in her convent. Hearing where the fugitives had hidden, the Turks burst into the saint's cell, beat her, carried her before the governor and imprisoned her; and the next morning they brought her out before a crowd and threatened to cut her in pieces if she would not renounce Christ.

When she had made up her mind to take the martyr's crown, by the Lord's mercy a crowd of Christians gathered as if out of nowhere, made peace with the judges and got her set free. She went back to her convent and went on in abstinence, prayer and vigil, and received the gift of working miracles; and she built another convent at Patisia, a suburb of Athens, where she laboured in the ascetic life with her sisters.

On the eve of the feast of Dionysius the Areopagite, which is kept on 3 October, the Turks took Philothei and tortured her, and left her more dead than alive and covered in blood. Weeping for grief, the sisters of the convent carried the martyr's bloodied body to Kalogreza, and there she died, on 19 February 1589. Soon after her death her relics were brought to the cathedral church at Athens. The page doxologia offers as her troparion is a recording of a sung invocation and prints no hymn text.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and adds a great deal, of which this paragraph takes what the Romanian account has not. saint.gr names her parents Angelos and Syriga Benizelou, says her mother was barren and conceived her after long prayer, and tells the vision that answered it: asleep from the labour of praying in the church of the Mother of God, Syriga saw a strong bright light come out of the icon of the Theomother and enter her womb. Before her tonsure the child was called Rigoula, or Revoula, that is Paraskevoula. At fourteen her parents married her against her will to one of the archons of Athens, and only after her parents and her husband had died did she give herself wholly to Christ, become a nun and take the name Philothei. It was St Andrew the First-called, seen in a vision, who told her to build the women's monastery she then named for him and endowed with dependencies and estates; in a little while it had two hundred sisters, and it became a harbour where the worn-out of the slavery found protection, the sick healing, the hungry food, the old a support and orphans tenderness. Against the Turks' opposition she built hospitals, orphanages and «σχολεῖα διὰ τοὺς παίδας τῶν Ἀθηναίων», schools for the children of the Athenians, and took particular care to save young Greek women from being made Muslims or carried off; and the page cites as undisputed historical confirmation of that work her correspondence with the Senate of Venice in 1583, from which she asked for money.

Once they arrested her, and she answered: I thirst to endure every kind of torture for the name of Christ, whom I worship with all my soul and heart as true God and perfect man, and I shall owe you great gratitude if you can send me to him an hour sooner with the crown of martyrdom. Christians reached the governor almost at the last moment and appeased him, and she went back unbloodied to her monastery; she crossed afterwards to the island of Tzia, Kea, where she had long before built a dependency for sisters who were afraid to stay at Athens. Of her wonders the page tells one, a shepherd boy given to thefts whom Satan had taken, who wandered the mountains naked until she prayed long over him, freed him and brought him into the order of monks. Then, on the night of 2 October 1588, while she and her sisters were keeping an all-night vigil in the little monastery they had built at Patisia — it was the memory of the hieromartyr Dionysius the Areopagite — five Turks climbed the outer wall, broke into the church, seized her and scourged her with fury and brutality and left her half dead outside. The column she was tied to still stands to the right of that church's door. Her nuns carried her to her retreat at Kalogreza, and there she died of her wounds on 19 February 1589.

Twenty days after her falling asleep her grave gave out a fragrance, and when a year later the translation was made her body was found whole and entire and full of fragrant myrrh. It lies today, the page says, in the Metropolitan church of Athens, incorrupt but for the head, which has been lost; a finger of hers is at the monastery of her name at Ekali in Attica. Over her grave these words were found written: under this sign of chaste Philothei is hidden a body, but the High-ruler laid her soul among the blessed. She was proclaimed a saint under the ecumenical patriarch Matthew the Second, 1595 to 1600, after Neophytos metropolitan of Athens had examined her life and her martyrdom and drawn up a report to the Patriarchate with the bishops of Corinth and Thebes and the notables of Athens; the synodal document says that since it was safely shown that the most divine body of the most venerable Philothei is filled with fragrance and pours out myrrh continually, and gives healing to the sick who come to it, it seemed good to write her too into the choir of the venerable and holy women, to be honoured and kept festival yearly. Her service, it adds, was written by a wise and devout man named Hierax, whose encomium says she had David's meekness, Solomon's wisdom, Samson's manliness, Abraham's hospitality, Job's patience and the Forerunner's divine ascesis. Its couplet calls Philothei a shoot of Athens who cast down the enemy with the whole armour of the Cross.
