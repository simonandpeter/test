# Gorazd

Gorazd — Matthias Pavlík in the world — was born on 26 May 1879 at Hrubá
Vrbka in southern Moravia, then in Austria-Hungary, to the educated peasant
Jan Pavlík and his wife Anna, born Bělčíková, Roman Catholics; he took joy
in the services at neighbouring Kuželov, was loved by teachers and
schoolfellows, learned excellently, and read much in his free time,
especially about the life of the first Christians. After the elementary
school he went to the Catholic gymnasium at Kroměříž, where in the seventh
class he began to teach himself Old Slavonic, finished with distinction, and
in 1898 entered the Catholic theological faculty at Olomouc; between his
second and third years, in 1900, he travelled to the Kiev Caves Lavra. He
finished in 1902 in the first class, was ordained priest on 5 July in the
cathedral of St Wenceslas at Olomouc, served the parishes of Karlovice and
then Brumovice, and from 15 September 1906 to 1 May 1920 was chaplain of the
psychiatric hospital at Kroměříž, honoured by the patients and by the
doctors and staff alike. The decisive turn in his religious outlook came
with Austria-Hungary’s attack on Serbia: unlike most of the Catholic clergy
he condemned the aggression of Austria and Germany against Serbia, and began
to publish the journal The Right of the People, with articles calling for
the reformation of the Roman Church. In the autumn of 1918, amid the great
changes of the newly independent Czechoslovakia, he fell gravely ill and
almost lost his sight, lay long in hospital, and was pensioned young. About
half a million Czechs and Slovaks left the Roman Church in those years,
which many felt as the religion of the Austrian conquerors, and on 8 January
1920 the Czechoslovak Church was proclaimed, heir, it said, of the Hussite
and of the Cyrillo-Methodian traditions, under the former priest Karel
Farský; he could not agree with it wholly, lacking as it did a hierarchy and
any bond with the universal Church, but resolved to work in it for the
enlightenment of the people in the spirit of Orthodoxy, and on 11 October
1920 formally left the Roman Church and joined the Czechoslovak Church to
defend the Orthodox direction in it, hard as that was against its strong
liberal wing. From 1 July 1921 he was church administrator for Moravia and
Silesia and began the journal For the Truth, in defence of Orthodox
principles against the radicals who held the leading posts; by his efforts
the Church’s first council accepted Orthodox morals and doctrine, and its
second, on 29 August 1921, resolved that its teaching should be Orthodox,
recognised the decrees of the seven Ecumenical Councils and took the name
Czechoslovak Orthodox Church, with the Serbian exarch Bishop Dositheus of
Niš present, which opened canonical communion. On 21 September 1921 he was
tonsured at the Krušedol lavra on Fruška Gora with the name of Gorazd, the
disciple of Methodius, made igumen at Grgeteg the next morning and
archimandrite at Hopovo that evening by Dositheus, and on 25 September 1921,
in the cathedral of St Michael, consecrated bishop of Moravia and Silesia by
Patriarch Demetrius of Serbia, Metropolitan Anthony of Kiev, Metropolitan
Barnabas, Bishops Dositheus and Joseph, and all the Serbian bishops present.

As an Orthodox bishop he had to fight for Orthodoxy in the Czechoslovak
Church, whose radicals liked the wrecking of Catholicism and not the
building of Orthodoxy — if the Czechoslovak Church chooses not to know true
Christianity, he said, it should say so openly and declare that it needs
neither ordained bishops nor ordained priests; but if bishops and priests
are to be ordained, one cannot be cut off from traditional Christianity; and
it would be the sign of a truly elastic conscience to accept the Nicene
creed and the seven councils and the name Orthodox, and laugh at everything
Orthodox, dogma and rite alike. Sent in July 1922 on a mission to the Czech
colonies in America, he came home to find that Farský’s radicals had issued
a heretical Czechoslovak Catechism — no one God in three Persons but a
“living law of the world”, Christ not the Son of God and Saviour but a
prophet beside Moses, Socrates, Muhammad, Zoroaster, Buddha and Confucius,
the Holy Spirit only God’s inspiration in man, the doctrine of the Mother of
God rejected — and answered that its whole tone matched neither the Gospel,
nor the first Christians, nor the councils, that it looked at Christ
one-sidedly without regard to grace, and that without grace Christianity
ceases and only ethics is left, which cannot sanctify or save. For nearly
two years he fought them, while a separate Czech Orthodox community, after
failed talks with the Serbian Church, went under Constantinople in 1923, a
jurisdictional quarrel fed by a state whose president Masaryk and leading
politicians were against the restoration of Orthodoxy; unable to shepherd
the Orthodox-minded in Bohemia himself, he advised them to join that
community, as most of them, especially in Prague, did. On 5 March 1923 he
resigned his see and all activity in the Czechoslovak Church; the diocesan
council refused the resignation and the Orthodox-minded begged him to stay,
and he stayed so that they should not be without a pastor, and fought
another year; on 10 June 1924 For the Truth appeared for the last time as an
organ of that Church, and on 21 July 1924 he left it with its Orthodox part,
after which it ceased to be Orthodox in anything but name and, adopting the
Catechism as its official teaching at its assembly of 31 August 1924, fell
finally into Protestantism. Under the Serbian patriarch he set about
organising the Czech Orthodox Church as a Serbian diocese, sent the first
theology students to Serbia in September 1924, and built: his aim was a
church and a parish house in every parish, a church a year, with the
archpriest Vsevolod Kolomatsky his great helper, and he not only directed
the building but worked with the parishioners with his own hands and gave
much help in secret — fourteen churches built and consecrated from 1928 to
1942 against a furious propaganda, he reported to Patriarch Barnabas, from
President Masaryk, the political parties, the Czechoslovak Church and the
Roman Catholics. He was a missionary bishop, living months in a parish and
visiting every Orthodox family, and a writer: a Collection of Prayers and
Liturgical Chants of the Orthodox Church, set to the Russian melodies of the
first, third, fourth and sixth tones and of the prokeimena, which he went
round the parishes teaching the people to sing; the Service Book of 1927; a
Bible History for the elementary schools; and, under the pen-name P. Malý,
his outstanding Life of Saints Cyril and Methodius and their relations with
Rome and Constantinople. It took much patience to end the division with the
followers of Constantinople — on 22 November 1925 he was elected head of the
Czech Orthodox community, the state recognised the Serbian jurisdiction as
lawful, in February 1926 the administration passed into his hands, and in
September 1929 the Serbian Church and the government approved a diocesan
organisation in law — and four years more to settle the legal questions and
set Orthodoxy in Czechoslovakia on the footing of two dioceses, the Czech
and the Subcarpathian; the monastery he founded lost its monks for various
reasons; on 28 September 1935 he took part in the consecration of the
cathedral of Cyril and Methodius in Prague.

In 1938 the country fell victim to the Munich agreement and part of it was
occupied; he called the faithful in sermons, letters and articles to their
Christian and patriotic duties, and on 15 March 1939, the day Czechoslovakia
lost its independence altogether, wrote in an article to his brothers and
sisters that injustice would be destroyed if the people did not forsake God,
and that the Orthodox Church was the firmest foundation both of religious
and moral life and of the nation’s; he began writing Lives of the Saints,
blessed the building of a Trinity church at Čelechovice na Hané even under
the occupation, and told the protectorate minister Moravec, we shall obey
every lawful government. Pressed by the protectorate to leave the Serbian
jurisdiction for that of the Berlin archbishop of the Russian Church Abroad,
he was received there provisionally with twenty parishes on 21 November
1941, the war having cut him off from Serbia. On 27 May 1942 the executioner
of the Czech people, the SS Obergruppenführer Heydrich, was killed in
Prague, and the parachutists who did it hid in the crypt of the Orthodox
cathedral of Cyril and Methodius in Resslova street; on 8 June the Germans,
informed by the parachutist Čurda, who had turned traitor, surrounded the
church and stormed it, and arrested the archpriest Václav Čikl, Father
Vladimír Petřek, Jan Sonnevend, Václav Ornest and others. Gorazd was in
Germany at the time — an unconditional alibi in law — and it was not his own
safety that concerned him: he came back at once so that the Church should
not suffer bloody persecution, and in a letter to Moravec took on himself
the responsibility for all that had been done in sheltering the
parachutists, which Moravec sent to the Gestapo. On Thursday 25 June 1942 at
five in the morning the Gestapo arrested him, and from then until 4
September he was in prison under heavy and painful interrogation, the
Germans tearing his thick beard, beating him and tormenting him in other
ways, taking him away at ten at night and bringing him back at five in the
morning, giving him no rest by day, the warder often kicking him away from
his food; those who witnessed his imprisonment and his tortures said that
he, Father Václav and Father Vladimír were very calm through it all, with a
deep peace on their faces at the trial, at the sentence and at the place of
execution. The military tribunal condemned him with the archpriest Václav
Čikl, the priest and doctor of theology Vladimír Petřek and the layman Jan
Sonnevend to death, and on 4 September 1942 they were shot in Prague; the
bodies of Gorazd, Father Vladimír and Sonnevend were burned, and Father
Václav’s given for dissection; soon after, the Czech Orthodox Church was
forbidden altogether by the Reich. His veneration began at once — the first
icon of him was painted in 1944 by the archpriest Kolomatsky — and on Sunday
5 September 1987, in the cathedral of St Gorazd at Olomouc, before a great
crowd and the whole Synod of the Orthodox Church of Czechoslovakia under
Metropolitan Dorotheus, with bishops of Constantinople, Russia and Serbia,
the hieromartyr Gorazd, bishop of Bohemia and Moravia-Silesia, was
glorified; his vestments are kept in the Prague cathedral, and in 1992 the
first liturgy was served in a church dedicated to him in his native Hrubá
Vrbka. The Russian calendar keeps him on 22 August of the old style, 4
September, and the Serbian calendar, which ordained him, the same day.
