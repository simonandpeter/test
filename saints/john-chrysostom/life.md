# John Chrysostom

Trained in rhetoric at Antioch under the pagan orator Libanius, John spent some
years as a hermit before returning to the city, where his preaching earned him
the name by which he is now known — *chrysostomos*, golden-mouthed. His homilies
survive in greater quantity than those of any other Greek father.

Made archbishop of Constantinople in 398, largely against his will, he set about
reducing the expenses of his household, disciplining the clergy and preaching
against the luxury of the court. This was less popular than his preaching in
Antioch had been. He was deposed at the Synod of the Oak in 403 on charges
assembled by Theophilus of Alexandria, recalled after a few days, and exiled
again the following year. He died in 407 being marched to a still more distant
place of banishment.

The liturgy most commonly celebrated in the Byzantine rite bears his name,
though its present form is considerably later than he is.

The Ohrid Prologue gives him the fullest entry of this day and begins with a household: born at Antioch in 354 to a father who was a military commander and to a mother, [Anthousa](/saints/anthousa-mother-of-john-chrysostom). Studying Greek philosophy he came to loathe Greek heathenism and took the Christian faith as the one and whole truth; he was baptised by [Meletius](/saints/meletius-of-antioch), the patriarch of Antioch, and after him his parents received baptism too. After their death he was tonsured and began a strict ascetic labour, and it was then that he wrote his book on the priesthood.

Then three signs, and the book sets them out in order. Two of the apostles appeared to him, foretelling a great service, a great grace and also a great suffering. When he was to be ordained priest an angel of God appeared at the same hour both to him and to the patriarch who had succeeded Meletius; and when that patriarch laid hands on him, everyone saw a white shining dove over his head.

Famous for his wisdom, his asceticism and the power of his word, he was chosen patriarch of Constantinople at the wish of the emperor, and governed the church six years with an incomparable zeal. The entry lists what he did in them: he sent missionaries to the heathen Celts and Scythians; he put down simony, deposing many bishops who had bought their sees; he widened the church's work of mercy; he wrote a particular order of the holy Liturgy; he put the heretics to shame; he rebuked the empress; and he expounded the Scriptures with his golden mind and tongue, leaving the church many precious books of his homilies.

The people glorified him, the envious hated him, and the empress sent him into exile twice. He spent three years in exile and died on the day of the Cross, 14 September 407, at Comana in Armenia — which is the day and the year this folder already records. Before his death the same two apostles appeared again, and a martyr in whose church he received his last communion. Glory to God for all things, the book gives as his last words, and with those words his soul passed over. Of his relics, it says, the head rests in the church of the Dormition in Moscow and the body in the Vatican at Rome.
