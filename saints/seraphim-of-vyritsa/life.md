# Seraphim of Vyritsa

Vasily Muraviev, who was to be Seraphim, was born in 1865 in the town of Cheremovsky in the province of Yaroslavl, the son of peasants, Nicholas and Chiona. His father died when he was ten, and he was left to his sick mother and his sister Olga. A kind neighbour took him to Saint Petersburg and found him work as a shop clerk. He wanted secretly to become a monk, and went one day to the Lavra of Saint Alexander Nevsky to speak of it to one of the elders.

The elder told him to stay in the world and raise a family, and that when their children were grown he and his wife should serve God in the monastic life. Vasily took the words as God's will and lived by them: he went back to the shop, kept sending money home, married at twenty-four a wife also named Olga, set up as a furrier and grew very rich. They had a son, Nicholas, and a daughter, Olga; and after the daughter's death the two agreed to live from then on as brother and sister.

At about thirty he gave part of his fortune away to monasteries, and when their son was grown he and Olga withdrew to monastic houses to serve God. She was tonsured in 1919 with the name Christina, lived at the New Resurrection–Diveyevo convent in Saint Petersburg, was later tonsured to the great schema as Seraphima, and died in 1945. Where Vasily himself was tonsured is not known — some say on Mount Athos — nor what name he was given then.

In 1927 he came to the Lavra of Saint Alexander Nevsky and became the monks' spiritual father, and there he was tonsured to the great schema with the name Seraphim. It soon became plain that he had been given the gifts of foresight and of healing, and many came to him for help and counsel. Bishop Alexei (Simansky) of Novgorod came to him that same year to ask whether he should leave Russia, since bishops and priests were being arrested and shot; before he could say a word, Seraphim told him that many wished to leave but had nothing to fear, that he was needed there, and that he would become Patriarch and reign many years.

Towards the end, his cell-attendant, Father Alexei, who brought him Communion at two in the morning, once overslept and did not come until four; he apologised, and noticed a certain brightness about the elder, who said: Father, do not be troubled, the holy angels have already brought me Communion. Seeing his face, Father Alexei knew it was true. The elder sent him to Moscow to tell Patriarch Alexei I that he would depart to the Lord in a fortnight; and the Patriarch turned to the icons and prayed, and turned back with tears running down his face, saying that he had been patriarch four years and had twenty-one left, for so the holy elder had told him. Patriarch Alexei died in 1970, as Seraphim had foretold.

Seraphim departed to the Lord on 21 March 1949. In the hours before his death he asked that the akathists of the most holy Mother of God, of Saint Seraphim of Sarov and of Saint Nicholas be read. For a week after his blessed falling asleep a sweet fragrance filled Vyritsa. He was buried in the cemetery beside the church of the Kazan Icon there, a great crowd came to the burial, and Vyritsa became a place of pilgrimage. While he lay in the coffin a mother brought her daughter, blind from birth, who began to see as soon as she kissed his hand; many such wonders go on happening at his grave. The Church of Russia canonised him in August 2000.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and tells his life at greater length than doxologia does. saint.gr gives him his name in the world, Vasily Nikolaevich Muraviev, and his parents' names, Nikolai Ivanovich and Chionia Olympievna, devout and God-loving people; he was baptised in 1866 and named for Basil the New. It has him teach himself grammar and then the beginnings of mathematics almost alone, with the Gospel and the Psalter for his first books, and as a boy read the lives of the saints — Paul of Thebes, the great ascetics Antony, Macarius and Pachomius, Mary of Egypt — until the thought of the monastic and angelic form was born in him and kept secret from those around him.

His father died at thirty-nine, his mother was sickly, and at ten the boy went to Saint Petersburg, blessed by his mother with an icon of the Theotokos, and took work as a delivery boy in one of the shops of the Gostiny Dvor, sending nearly all his wages home. At about fourteen he went to the Lavra of St Alexander Nevsky to ask for the monastic life, and an elder there gave him the counsel that proved prophetic: to stay in the world, to make a good and pious family, to bring his children up rightly, and with his wife to give the whole of the rest of their life to monasticism — and, the elder added, a hard road with much grief waited for him, but the hour would come and God would reward him. The page calls the forty years that followed a single feat of obedience.

In his last years, it says, illness came: unbearable pain in his legs, and then failure of the lungs and the heart at sixty-three, at which the doctors advised the country and Vyritsa was chosen, with the blessing of Metropolitan Seraphim Chichagov, who had medical training himself. At Vyritsa he went to no more doctors — for everything there is the will of God; illness is the school of gentleness, when you truly know your own helplessness. He ate nothing on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays and sometimes nothing for several days, communed weekly, and prayed in the garden on a stone before the icon of St Seraphim of Sarov, whom he imitated, doing so daily from the beginning of the Second World War. A day before the end he blessed his relatives and friends with that icon; very early on the last morning of 1949 the Mother of God appeared to him and pointed to heaven with her right hand, and about two o'clock after midnight he made the sign of the cross, whispered «Σώσε, Κύριε, και ελέησε όλον τον κόσμο» — save, O Lord, and have mercy on all the world — and departed. The people came for three days, the page says, and his hands stayed soft and warm as if he were alive.

The Russian calendar's reading, taken from Filimonov's life of him, is much the fullest of the three and answers what the others leave open. It puts his birth on 31 March 1866 at Vakhromeevo in the Arefinskaya district of the Rybinsk uyezd of Yaroslavl province, and his baptism the day after, when he was named Vasily for Basil the New the confessor. His spiritual father from his youth was the Gethsemane elder [Barnabas of Gethsemane](/saints/barnabas-of-gethsemane), who gave him the counsel to stay in the world, blessed his marriage to Olga Ivanovna in 1890, and guided him for about twenty years until his death in 1906.

In 1892 he opened his own house for the buying and selling of furs, which traded into Germany, Austria-Hungary, England and France, and he gave the profits to churches, almshouses and the sick. He did not take his money abroad in 1917. On 13 September 1920 he asked the Spiritual Council of the Lavra of Alexander Nevsky to receive him, gave it forty thousand roubles in gold, and was set to the sacristan's obedience; on 29 October he was tonsured there with the name Varnava, for his elder, and Olga was tonsured the same day as Christina. He was made deacon over the cemetery office, priest on 11 September 1921, and then treasurer and a member of the Council.

At the turn of 1926 and 1927 he was clothed in the great schema as Seraphim and chosen spiritual father of the Lavra. Lung and heart failure sent him to Vyritsa in the summer of 1930, and he saw no doctor again: illness, he said, is the school of humility, where a man truly learns his own weakness. From 1935 he prayed in the garden upon a stone before the icon of the Sarov wonderworker, and from the first day of the war he did it daily. He departed on 3 April 1949, the day the Church keeps the raising of Lazarus. The day page carries seven hymns under his name — four troparia and three kontakia — and all seven are here.
