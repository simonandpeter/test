# Stephanida

In the reign of Antoninus, emperor of Rome, a woman of the people named Stephanida, a Christian and the wife of one of the soldiers, saw two beautiful crowns coming down from heaven, one to the head of the Martyr [Victor](/saints/victor-of-damascus) and the other to her own; and she began with a loud voice to call him blessed. Blessed art thou, Victor, she said, and blessed are thy sufferings for Christ; thy sacrifice is acceptable to God as Abel's was, for thou hast offered thyself to Him with an upright heart.

She went on through the Scriptures: God has received thee as He received Enoch the righteous man, whom He took to paradise that he should not taste death for a time; thou art righteous as Noah was, full of good works and perfect in his generation; thou hast believed as Abraham did, and hast brought thyself as a sacrifice to God as Isaac was brought; thou hast had labours as Jacob had, and hast been made most wise as Joseph was, to whom it was given to tell beforehand the things that were to be; thou hast borne temptations as Job did, who after much suffering overcame the devil; thou hast followed Isaiah, whom Manasseh cut asunder with a saw; the fire has not touched thee, as it did not touch the three young men in the furnace of Nebuchadnezzar; thou hast set thy hope on God as David the son of Jesse did.

For behold, she said, I see two crowns sent from heaven, one greater and fairer and one smaller: the greater is brought to thee by twelve angels, and the smaller to me, since I too am the weaker vessel; but I am ready to enter the contest and to endure manfully for our Lord, and to lay down my soul for Him.

While she was saying this the commander heard her and ordered those standing by to take her and bring her before him; and looking at her proudly he asked who she was, and she answered that she was a Christian. He asked her then about her name and her age; and the Romanian life goes on from there to her end and Victor's.

At the last her holy soul flew away like a bird and found its nest in heaven and the crown prepared for her. So she suffered together with Victor in the city of Damascus, on the eleventh day of the month of November; and now, the life says, they dwell both together in that city which needs neither sun nor moon to light it, for the glory of God lightens it and the Lamb of God is its lamp. The page gives no year; it places her by the reign of Antoninus and by nothing else.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 11 November, and says who she was before. saint.gr makes Stephanida the wife of a soldier in Italy in the year 160, when the emperor was Antoninus; her husband died in the meantime and she was left a widow. A Christian from her forebears, she saw Victor, whom the same day keeps, being tortured beyond measure, and called him blessed for his courage. That one utterance provoked the idolaters, who brought her before the governor; and because she confessed Christ there too with courage, they bound her hands to the tops of two palm trees which they bent down by force, and then let them go, and as the trees sprang back to their place they tore the saint in two, and so she gave her blessed soul into the hands of God. Her distich makes a palm of her: bound to the palm trees, Stephanida flowered like a palm in the midst of the martyrs.

The Russian calendar keeps the two of them on one line, «Мч. Виктора и мц. Стефаниды (II).», and serves one life for both. In it Victor is a soldier in the reign of the emperor Marcus Aurelius the Philosopher, which the page dates 161–180. When the emperor began to persecute the Christians Victor refused to sacrifice to the gods — and the page says what that refusal was, that the obligatory sacrifice was the test of a soldier's loyalty to the gods, to the emperor and to his country.

He was given over to torture and came through all of it unharmed. By the strength of his prayer he overcame a sorcerer, who from then on gave up his spells and became a Christian; at his prayer soldiers who had been struck suddenly blind saw again. Seeing the wonders the Lord worked through him, the young and devout wife of one of his torturers openly glorified Christ, and for that she was put to a savage death: they tied her to two palm trees bent together, which straightened and tore her apart.

Victor was beheaded. The two suffered at Damascus in the second century, the page says, and there their honourable remains were buried. **The day page prints one kontakion for three of its martyrs together** — «Кондак мучеников Виктора, Стефаниды и Викентия», tone 4 — and it is therefore in all three folders. It names none of them in its body, which is why it can serve three; it is not a common of martyrs either, since it is printed under their three names and not under a class. The last clause as the page prints it reads «да омут непобедимую победу», where «омут» stands for «имут», and it is copied as printed: a reading that looks wrong is the source's and not the scrape's.
