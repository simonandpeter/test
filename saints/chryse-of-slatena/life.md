# Chryse of Slatena

saint.gr's calendar for 13 October keeps «Αγία Χρυσή η Νεομάρτυς», and gives her village and her year. She was born at Slatena — the Chryse of today — in the district of Almopia in the prefecture of Pella; her father was poor and had four daughters, and Chryse, the page says, was beautiful in body and in soul.

Once, when she was out in the fields with other women gathering firewood, a Turk carried her off and took her to his house. He tried by flatteries to make her a Muslim and his wife, but Chryse resisted and answered him with force: I know Christ alone for my bridegroom, and I shall not deny him even if you cut me to pieces. Her parents and her kin, under compulsion from the Turks, begged her to accept Mohammedanism and save herself; and the great-souled Chryse answered them that she had for a father her Lord Jesus Christ, for a mother the Lady Theotokos, and for brothers and sisters the men and women saints of our Church.

Faced with her steadfastness, the page says, the Turks answered with horrible tortures, and at last, on 13 October 1795, they cut her body to pieces with knives, and so she received the crown of martyrdom from Christ the Bridegroom, deservedly. The calendar prints an apolytikion and a kontakion for her, both naming her, and a megalynarion that calls her the virgin-martyr of Christ, gilded in body and in soul.

The Russian calendar keeps her twice and names the village saint.gr does not. She was born and lived, its life says, in the Bulgarian settlement of Slatino in the diocese of Moglena, and Bulgaria at that time lay under the Turkish yoke. From her youth she was marked out by an extraordinary strength of character, a firm faith in Christ, chastity and beauty; a local Turk tried again and again to win the girl over and make her accept Islam, and neither persuasion nor threats nor the monstrous tortures that went on for many months broke the spirit of this glorious confessor of Christ. It closes her year as «(+ 1795)», as the Greek does.

The two calendars part over the day. saint.gr puts the cutting of her body with knives on 13 October 1795, and the Russian keeps her repose on 18 October — «Преставление вмц. Златы (Хрисы) (1795) (Болг.)» — while giving 13 October to something else entirely: the remembrance of a miracle of hers at Skopje in 1912, a hundred and seventeen years after her death.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her on the Julian 13 October, with the Greek calendar’s day and not the Russian’s, and calls her great-martyr, which neither of the other two books read for this folder does. She was born, it says, in the village of Slatina in the district of Meglen, of poor village parents who had three more daughters; and she was a meek and devout girl, wise with the wisdom of Christ, and golden not only in her name but in her God-fearing heart.

She had gone out to the water when some shameless Turks seized her and dragged her to their house. One of them offered to make her his wife if she would turn Turk, and she answered him without fear: I believe in Christ, and him alone I know as my Bridegroom; I shall never deny him, though you put me to a thousand torments and cut me to pieces. Then her parents came to her with her sisters, and begged her — have mercy on yourself and on us, deny Christ in appearance only, that you may be happy and we with you, and Christ is merciful and will forgive a sin done under the need of life. The poor parents and the sisters and the kinsfolk wept bitterly.

Her answer to them is the heart of the entry. If you counsel me to deny Christ the true God, she said, you are no longer my parents nor my sisters; I have the Lord Jesus Christ for a father, and the Theotokos for a mother, and for brothers and sisters the saints, men and women.

The Turks threw her into prison then, where she lay three months, and brought her out every day to be scourged until her blood soaked the ground. They hung her head downwards over a fire to choke her with the smoke, and the Lord was with her and gave her strength in her suffering. At the last they hung her on a tree and cut her all into small pieces. Christians carried the pieces of her relics away to their own homes for a blessing. This book closes her year as 1796, where the Greek and the Russian both give 1795, and the interval in this folder is widened to hold both rather than preferring either.
