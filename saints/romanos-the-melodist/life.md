# Romanos the Melodist

The Greek synaxarion says that little is known of Romanos’s life. He lived either in the sixth century, under the emperor Anastasius I, or by another account in the eighth, under Anastasius II. He was born at Emesa in Syria and, an anonymous hymn says, was of Jewish descent. He was first a deacon of the church at Beirut, then went to Constantinople and lived in the cells of a church of the Theotokos. His learning was modest and his gift for verse still unknown, but he had a deep devotion to the Virgin and kept the night vigils at her church at Blachernae. After one of them, at Christmas, he dreamed that the Theotokos gave him a book and told him to eat it, and on waking he composed the Nativity kontakion «Ἡ Παρθένος σήμερον».

From then on the gift never left him, and he became the greatest melodist of the Church. He wrote about a thousand hymns, of which about a tenth survive, on nearly every saint and feast, in plain and vivid language; the synaxarion quotes the Byzantinist Krumbacher calling him the greatest poet of the Byzantine age, and says he brought the kontakion — the prooimion, the oikoi and the refrain, bound by equal syllables and stresses — to its most perfect form, often writing the music as well. It keeps him with two apolytikia, the second the venerable father’s common.

The Romanian life makes him a Syrian brought up at Emesa who lived in virginity from his youth, and first a sacristan of the church at Beirut. In the reign of the emperor Anastasius he came to Constantinople and lived at the church of the Theotokos in Kiri, walking each evening to Blachernae to pray all night and back again, and later served as sacristan at Hagia Sophia. He could not read, but the patriarch Euthymius loved him for his life and gave him a share like the clergy’s, and the clergy resented it. At vespers of the Nativity, with the emperor in the church, they dragged him to the ambo to shame him and told him to sing. He wept, prayed before the icon of the Theotokos, went home without eating and slept, and dreamed that she put a small paper in his mouth and told him to eat it.

He woke with understanding of the Scriptures, and at the vigil went up into the ambo and sang his kontakion «Fecioara astăzi»; the clergy fell at his feet asking forgiveness, the patriarch made him a deacon, and he wrote more than a thousand kontakia for the feasts of the Lord and of the Theotokos and for the great saints. Where the Greek has him a deacon at Beirut before he came to the city and given a book to eat, the Romanian makes him a sacristan there and a deacon only after that Christmas, and the Virgin’s gift a small paper. It gives no year for his death. The Romanian calendar keeps him with a troparion, the venerable father’s common, and a kontakion of his own.

The Serbian church's Ohrid Prologue keeps him third on this day, after the feast of the Protection and the apostle Ananias, and it is the only one of the three calendars that puts a year to anything. It has him a sexton first at Beirut and then at the cathedral church in Constantinople «у време патријарха Јевтимија (490- 504. год.)» — the patriarch the Romanian life names and leaves undated — and it says that as deacon of the Great Church he reposed at Constantinople in 510 and passed over among the choirs of angels. That is the sixth-century reading of him, and it cannot stand with the Greek entry's alternative eighth; both are kept on this folder and neither is preferred.

It agrees that he was of Emesa in Syria, that he was unlettered and unskilled in chanting, and that some learned clerics mocked him for it. Of the dream it tells one thing neither of the others does: that what the Theotokos handed him and told him to swallow was a scroll of paper «звани кондак» — called a kondak. The next day dawned Christmas; he went out on the ambo and sang «Дјева днес» in an angelic voice, and all were astonished at the song and at the singer's voice alike. Having received the gift of song from the Theotokos, the Prologue says, he sang more than a thousand kontakia.
