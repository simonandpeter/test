# Leo the Great

Leo, the great hierarch and shepherd of the Church of God, was of Italy by race, and his father was Quintian. From his youth he was brought up in letters, in the wisdom of the world and in the good works of a Christian. Choosing the spiritual life rather than the worldly one, he was first archdeacon to Pope Sixtus the Third; and after Sixtus died he was chosen by all, for his great wisdom and his purity, to the throne of Rome, and shepherded the rational sheep of Christ well, as a good shepherd does, laying down his soul for them.

When Attila, lord of the Huns and a cruel tyrant, the man called the scourge of God, had beaten many countries and came against Italy to waste it with fire and sword, Leo saw that nobody could stand against him. He prayed to God with fasting and tears, asking His defence and calling the great apostles Peter and Paul to his help; and then he went alone to the tyrant to beg him to be still, ready to die for his sheep. Speaking to him with sweet words breathed from God, he turned him from a wolf into a sheep.

Attila heard the words of God's servant meekly and humbly, wondering at his bishop's vesture and afraid of his honourable and holy face; he did everything as Leo wished, and went out of the borders of Italy to his own place. His nobles and commanders, wondering at so sudden a turn to gentleness, asked why he had feared one Roman who came to him unarmed, and had gone away like a beaten man leaving so much plunder in the land of Italy. You did not see what I saw, he answered them: I saw two men in the likeness of angels, the great apostles Peter and Paul, on either side of the pope, holding swords in their hands and threatening me with death if I would not hear the bishop of God.

In the years of his priesthood, after the heresy of Nestorius, came Eutyches, archimandrite of the monks of Constantinople, and Dioscorus, patriarch of Alexandria, who mixed the two natures of Christ our Lord, the divine and the human, into one, and troubled the Church of God greatly with it. Gathering their own council at Ephesus, they judged [Flavian](/saints/flavian-of-constantinople), patriarch of Constantinople and defender of the faith, unjustly, and killed him, and did much harm to the Orthodox. Then Leo pressed hard for the mending of the troubled Church, writing to the emperors — first to Theodosius, then to Marcian — that a council of the whole world should be called.

So the holy Fourth Ecumenical Council gathered at Chalcedon, six hundred and thirty fathers, in the reign of Marcian and [Pulcheria](/saints/pulcheria-the-empress), against Eutyches and Dioscorus, who said that in Christ the Lord there is one nature and one operation and one will. Leo could not be there himself — the road was long, old age made him weak, and the frequent barbarian raids on Italy made the crossing hard — so he sent the bishops Paschasinus and Lucentius and the presbyters Boniface and Basil in his place.

At the council, when there was quarrelling with the heretics and much doubting, the fathers ordered Leo's letter to Flavian to be read out; and when it had been read they all cried: Peter the Apostle speaks through the mouth of Leo. The life tells how that letter came to be written. Leo laid it on the tomb of the Apostle Peter and prayed there with vigils and fasting, saying: if I have erred in anything, as a man may, do thou, to whom the apostleship was entrusted by the Lord, save me. After forty days the apostle appeared to him at his prayer and said, I have read it and corrected it; and taking the letter up from the tomb Leo found it set right by the apostle's own hand.

He lived to great old age, and the page says he ended in the reign of the emperor who bore his name, Leo the Great. As his end came near he sought assurance that his human weaknesses were forgiven, and spent forty days at the tomb of the Apostle Peter in prayer and fasting, asking the apostle to pray God for him. After the forty days Peter appeared and said: I have prayed for you, and your sins are forgiven you — except the ordinations, for of those you will be asked, whether you ordained a man well and lawfully, or not.

After that warning Leo pressed his prayers, his fasts and his alms the harder, crying out in the breaking of his heart until he had full relief; and so, having prepared himself as he should for his going out, he gave his soul into the hands of God. Doxologia prints no year for him anywhere in the life, and the page it offers as his troparion is a recording in Greek with no text.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and gives him two lives, one after the other, and says so. In the first, saint.gr calls him one of the greatest defenders and champions of the Orthodox faith, sets him in the years of the emperor [Marcian](/saints/marcian-the-emperor) and [Pulcheria](/saints/pulcheria-the-empress), and marks him out for his great theological training, the character of his manners and the purity of his life; he was bishop of elder Rome. His contribution to the work of the Fourth Ecumenical Council was of the greatest importance, when he sent four representatives and a letter in which, with full exactness and on the foundations of the apostolic tradition, he defined the two natures of Christ, and which helped both the carrying on of the discussions and the writing of the council's final terms — to be exact, the page says, the letter had been sent three years before to the patriarch of Constantinople [Flavian](/saints/flavian-of-constantinople), whom the Greek calendar keeps on 16 February, and was read out at the council; it is known as the Tome of Leo. He was besides a most prolific writer, and a number of his letters survive, written with full vividness and with force of speech. He fell asleep in holiness in a very deep old age on 10 November 460, and his synaxis used to be held in the Great Church.

Then the page prints a second version of the life, which it attributes to S. Eustratiades, and it is not a friendly one: according to it Leo was born at Rome at the end of the fourth century, served as deacon to the popes Callistus and Sixtus before going up to the throne on 29 September 440, was one of the most absolutist of the popes and stubbornly upheld the papal primacy, and owes his numbering among the saints to the letter he sent to the Fourth Ecumenical Council against the Monothelites and Monophysites, which was received with enthusiasm by the fathers who were present; he fell asleep on 10 November 460. Both readings are the source's and both are recorded here as the source's; nothing here decides between them. Its couplets say that the divine Leo pours out his soul and casts fear into the phalanxes of the demons, and that on the eighteenth Leo belched forth his spirit.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović has him born in Italy of devout parents, archdeacon first under Pope Sixtus III and raised to the Roman throne after that pope's death against his own will. When Attila came near Rome with the Huns and was already preparing to destroy and burn the city, it says, Leo went out to meet him in his hierarch's vestments, tamed the Hunnish leader's anger and turned the city's ruin aside. As far as Attila let himself be counselled by the holy man, so far he must have been frightened by the vision of the apostles Peter and Paul, who stood beside Leo and threatened him with flaming swords.

And he did not only save Rome, the Prologue goes on, but helped much towards the saving of Orthodoxy from the heresy of Eutyches and Dioscorus — which consisted, it explains, in the running together of Christ's divine and human natures into one and so in the denial of two wills in the person of the Lord and Saviour. For that the fourth ecumenical council was called at Chalcedon, where the epistle of Leo was read out, the one he had written and laid on the tomb of Saint Peter, and which Saint Peter had corrected.

Before his death he spent forty days in fasting and prayer at the apostle's tomb, asking to be told whether his sins were forgiven. The apostle appeared and said all of them were, except his sins in the ordaining of priests — from which, the Prologue remarks, one may see how heavy a sin it is to ordain an unworthy man. The saint fell to prayer again until he was told that those too had been blotted out, and then peacefully gave up his soul to the Lord, in 461.
