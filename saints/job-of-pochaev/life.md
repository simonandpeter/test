# Job of Pochaev

Job — John Zhelezo in the world — was born about 1551 in Pokuttia in Galicia
to pious parents, and had hardly learned to read before he was steeping
himself in the life of the Forerunner whose name he bore, the Ladder of John
Climacus and the lives of Sabbas the Sanctified and John of Damascus, in a
home whose example matched its reading; small in years, his biographer
Dositheus says, he was perfect in understanding. At ten the longing for the
desert life overcame even his love for his parents, and he left them for the
Transfiguration monastery of Ugornik in the Carpathians, where the igumen,
reading the marks of a chosen child in his face, received him gladly; not
content with the obedience the ecclesiarch gave him, he served every monk as
he could, as the young Climacus and the young Damascene had, and at twelve,
for his good conduct, meekness and deep humility, the igumen tonsured him
with the name Job — a name, says Dositheus, that he did not merely resemble
but far outdid, for the old Job’s sorrows were sent him and this Job chose
his own, sparing himself nothing. He lived among the brethren like an angel,
and became a pattern to them so young that all Galicia heard of him, common
people and great lords coming to Ugornik to see him and ask his counsel and
blessing; at thirty, after long refusing, he was ordained priest, and his
fame reached into Poland. The Jesuits had come into Poland and Lithuania in
1566, and the Union was being pressed on the Orthodox with the magnates’
help, churches profaned and the monasteries the one refuge of the faith;
Prince Constantine of Ostrog, its great defender, who wanted his monasteries
to be seedbeds of true monks against the Protestant communities and the
Catholic orders, begged the igumen of Ugornik again and again until he let
Job go to the Exaltation of the Cross monastery on the island at Dubno,
where the brethren soon chose him igumen. There, in a house close to a busy
town and bound to be a lamp to the country round, he restored the Studite
rule the founders had laid down — the gates shut through the Great Fast and
opened only on Lazarus Saturday, silence kept, the igumen himself doing
nothing but the spiritual on those days save Saturdays; the prince, it is
said, came there the first week of Lent, put off his princely dress and put
on a monk’s to prepare for confession and communion — raised the inner life
of the brethren by his own example, and beyond the walls set himself against
the Jesuit preaching, defending leavened bread in the Eucharist against the
unleavened and, still more, warning the Orthodox against the Protestant
sects, so that princes, lords and people flocked to Dubno and to its igumen,
troubling him, as Dositheus says, with honour and praise. That vain glory
disturbed him; his bent was to solitude as a simple monk, and there were
disagreements with the prince, who honoured him but did not always act on
his views; and after twenty years of governing Dubno he withdrew to the
Pochaev hill, to the house now known as the Pochaev Lavra, which was only
beginning to be ordered after the wonder-working icon of the Mother of God
had been brought there from a pious lady’s house, and had no igumen at all.
He came to be a hermit, not an abbot; the monks begged him, his humility
could not refuse, and he hoped that so small a house would leave him time
for the solitary labours of his heart.

He built a stone church for the decayed wooden one by the rock, secured
lands for the house’s keep, dug wells and ponds, planted and grafted with
his own hands the orchard that still stands at the foot of the Lavra, and
dug the lake behind its wall. Then trouble came: when the pious Anna Goyska
died, her heir, the Lutheran Andrew Firley, hated the monastery, took its
lands, forbade the monks even the water they drew in Pochaev, kept pilgrims
from the icon and reviled it, and on 19 June 1623 sent his servant Gregory
Kozensky with Lutheran soldiers to sack the church and carry off the icon
and every vessel, vestment and votive silver in it to his estate at Kozin,
where he dressed his wife in the priestly vestments and put the chalice in
her hands to mock the Mother of God before his guests; the Mother of God
struck the woman with a sickness that did not leave her until the icon was
returned, though the plate stayed with the robber. The wonders began again,
the pilgrims came back, and the house grew. Through all this he kept the
labours of his youth: in the cave where he prayed the marks of his knees
remain, and he would shut himself in it three days or a whole week in prayer
and fasting, fed on tears; he kept so near a silence that little was heard
from him but the prayer always on his lips, Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God,
have mercy on me; his flesh was worn and his legs full of sores, so that
with the apostle he bore the marks of the Lord Jesus in his body; and he was
without malice — coming one night on a man stealing the monastery’s wheat,
who stood frozen and then fell at his feet begging him to tell no one, he
helped him lift the sack onto his shoulder, reminded him of the commandments
and the Judgement, and let him go in peace. In 1628 he sat in the Council of
Kiev that made Meletius Smotrytsky renounce his errors, and signed its
decree with the promise to stand firm in the Orthodox faith, never to go
over to the Union, and to exhort all the Orthodox people to the same; after
1642 he took the great schema with the name John. In 1649, feeling his
strength go, he handed the igumen’s office to Samuel Dobryansky, still
called igumen of Pochaev, and, having lived to a hundred, foretold the day
of his death a week before and died quietly, without any suffering, on 28
October 1651, after fifty years over Pochaev. Seven years later light was
seen coming from his grave, and he appeared three times in a dream to
Metropolitan Dionysius Balaban of Kiev bidding him uncover the relics; on 28
August 1659 the metropolitan, the archimandrite Theophan and the brethren
opened the grave, found the relics incorrupt, and laid them in the porch of
the great church of the Trinity, and healings flowed from them. The house he
built was taken by the Uniates in 1720 and held by Basilian monks until
1831, when it came back to the Orthodox; on 28 August 1833 the relics were
solemnly opened for the veneration of all, and in 1902 the Synod ordered
that on that day they be carried round the Dormition cathedral after the
liturgy. The Lavra keeps him three times a year — 19 May with Job the
Much-suffering, 10 September for the finding of his relics, which is the day
this entry stands under, and 10 November, the day of his death.
