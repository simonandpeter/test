# Basil of Moscow

saint.gr says Basil the fool for Christ was born at Moscow in 1468, in the reign of Ivan III, of poor and honest and pious parents, and that from his first years it was plain he would be a holy man: he refused to suckle from his mother's left breast and never suckled at all on Wednesdays and Fridays. When he grew up his father sent him as an apprentice to a shoemaker, and there he worked and served his master until the day a customer ordered a pair of boots and wanted them particularly strong.

The young Basil smiled, so as to be asked about it by his master after the customer had gone, and answered that the man would not live to wear his boots, for he would die. And indeed he died the next day. That event, the page says, mattered enormously to the saint for the rest of his course: for if that man had known the hour of his death he would have taken thought for his soul instead of busying himself with trifles like ordering strong boots.

So he left the shoemaker and began to go about the streets of Moscow naked and above all unshod, warning everyone of the passing character of this life. He gave alms to beggars, to cripples and to the wretched and hard-pressed of every kind, and he often visited the special prisons where alcoholics were dried out and supported the inmates in spirit. His great ascesis and holiness were covered over by his nakedness, his follies and his loud laugh, which became proverbial.

In 1547, the year of the crowning of Ivan the Terrible, on 23 June, Basil went to the monastery dedicated to the Finding of the Honourable Head of John the Baptist and began to weep bitterly and to pray. People, knowing that the fool did nothing without a reason, wondered what it meant; and they had their answer the next day, when a fire of enormous size broke out in that church and spread over the whole city. Everything burned, and the flames left only embers and ruins in their passing.

Once, while a Persian ship was in danger on the Caspian Sea, a Russian Orthodox sailor called out to Basil for help; and the naked fool appeared, walking upon the waves, came aboard, seized the helm, brought her into safe water and then vanished. Afterwards the Persian merchants came to Moscow on their business and recognised their mad rescuer, who, wishing to escape them, began to play the fool in various ways.

Shortly before he died at the age of eighty-eight the whole royal family visited him, and the fool prophesied to the youngest child, Theodore, that he would inherit the imperial estate. Ivan and the tsarina Anastasia wept both for joy, because they had known a holy man, and for grief, because they were losing him. At the funeral procession the coffin with Basil's body was carried on the shoulders of the tsar himself with the princes, and all Moscow took part in the burial service.

Later a church was raised by the king's command over the saint's grave. That magnificent church, dedicated to Saint Basil the fool for Christ, adorns Red Square to this day and is reckoned the sight of Moscow above all others; and it is worth remembering, the page says, that the atheist communist government of the Soviet Union in all its years not only did not touch the church, as it did in other cases, but respected it.

The saint is also remembered in the life of [Nicodemus of Lake Kozha](/saints/nicodemus-of-lake-kozha). Before Nicodemus became a monk, when he was still called Nicetas, somebody tried once to poison him; they did not manage to kill him, but they did him great harm in the stomach, so that he had unbearable pains and could not work. He decided to leave Moscow for elsewhere until he should find some doctor to cure him, and as he was leaving he met Saint Basil going about the streets in rags.

Nicetas asked him to explain what was happening, and when Basil had been told he said: my child Nicetas, come at six o'clock to the metropolitan church; there you will find me and I will give you something to drink, and the prayers of the Most Holy Theotokos will help you to be cured. Nicetas went to the church at that hour, and Basil gave him a vessel and commanded him to drink it, after first making the sign of the honourable and life-giving Cross. He was well at once.
