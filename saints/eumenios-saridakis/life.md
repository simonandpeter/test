# Eumenios Saridakis

Elder Eumenios came of a large and poor family and was born on 1 January 1930 in the village of Ethia on Crete. His parents, George and Sophia Saridakis, were devout people, and at baptism they gave him the name Constantine. His father died when he was two. He was not like the other children of his age: he would stay a long time in the village church and in those round about, praying, and spent his evenings in vigils, and from childhood he wanted to be a monk.

In the winter of 1944, sitting by the stove eating doughnuts with his mother and his two sisters, he was pierced by a light that filled him with joy, and told his sisters: I shall become a monk. He was fourteen. In 1947 he entered the monastery of Saint Nicetas at Achendria, a small monastery cut into a cave in the south of Crete towards the Libyan gulf, where Father Hierotheos was abbot with two other monks, and the boy served them with much obedience.

Four years later, in 1951, he was tonsured and given the name Sophronius; and he went to do his military service, which was then obligatory even for monks. In the army a fever took hold of him that would not let him go, and the first signs of leprosy appeared. I felt, he said afterwards, as if somebody were driving an iron harrow into my back and pulling the flesh off me. After many examinations a doctor told him that he had caught Hansen's disease, and must be taken to Athens at once.

He asked the doctor whether he was in earnest — am I to carry so great a cross? — and when the doctor said yes, he fell out of bed for joy. Asked long afterwards why, he answered that when God sends you a great illness it means a great cross, and that a great cross, borne with God's strength, brings a great resurrection; and that he had prayed, my God, I am not worthy to carry so great a cross; do You love me so much? only help me to bear it. Asked whether he did not suffer, he said that he suffered in the body, and that his heart rejoiced as much as his body suffered.

He told the writer of this page one thing more. As soon as he had shown so much joy at being a leper the doctors left, and while he waited to be taken to the leper hospital the place around his bed filled with little demons that came at him to frighten him and reached almost to his body; but an unseen force threw them back. What did you do then, father? I rejoiced all the more, he said, and I said, how beautiful, how beautiful — the grace of God is with me, and does not even let Satan come near me. And I took great courage in that moment, and understood how strong our faith is.

In 1957 he was moved to the leper hospital in Athens. Because the remedy for leprosy had been discovered in 1947 and he had been diagnosed in time, he was cured by degrees; and being cured he stayed on in the leper hospital to serve the lepers. When the Venerable Nikephoros came there, and on the written recommendation of Saint Anthimos, the monk Sophronius took him for his elder. They kept their services and prayers apart, and fasted and ate no meat although the doctors told them to; and in time the holy life of the two monks came to the ears of many of the faithful, who, when they visited the lepers, sought out these two as well for the profit of their souls.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and begins with the village. saint.gr says that Ethia, the elder's homeland, is a mountain village in the south of the prefecture of Herakleion in Crete, standing at 740 metres and thirty-eight kilometres from Herakleion, and so barren a place that its people moved down to the lower village of Rotasi. Two churches stand in Ethia: the central one is dedicated to the Panagia and keeps a wonderworking icon of her, and the other is of the Prophet Elijah, with a holy spring near by. It was in the first of them, the page says, that the Mother of God appeared as a woman dressed in black, one day when the venerable Eumenios was a small child lighting the lamps of the church, and said to him: «Εσύ μια μέρα θα γίνεις ιερεύς» — you will become a priest one day. In the churchyard of that same church is the tomb where his body now rests.

He was the child of a large and very poor family, born on the first of January 1931. His parents, George and Sophia Saridakis, were devout and virtuous people who had eight children, and he was the eighth and last of them; at his baptism he was given the name Constantine. The couplet the page sets over his memory calls him a healer of lepers and of those sick with pestilence, and a guide of the faithful.
