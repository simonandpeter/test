# Stephen Štiljanović

He was born into a devout Christian family in the Serbian town of Župa, on the southern side of Zahumlje, at a time when the Turks raided Serbia constantly and laid it waste. He defended his country as a soldier in the army of the Serbian ruler. When famine came he gave his own bread away to the starving, and the calendar makes a point of refusing to separate the patriot from the Christian: he laboured always, it says, in what is good — almsgiving, purity, prayer, the Orthodox faith, and unfeigned love of his neighbours. He went to the Lord on 4 October 1515.

What happened afterwards is why he has a feast. Some time later the Turks saw a light over his grave, and supposing treasure was hidden there they dug it open and found his body incorrupt. Serbian monks bought the relics from the Turkish pasha and carried them to the monastery of Šišatovac on the Fruška mountain.

The Serbian Church, the Russian calendar says, prays to him as a healer: «Слава во бранех, воине Стефане Щиляновичу, великий целебниче приходящим к тебе с верою» — glory in battles, soldier Stephen Štiljanović, great healer of those who come to you in faith. The Russian keeps him with «(Серб.)» beside his name, marking him as one of the Serbian saints it carries, and his own index entry gives 30 August as his Serbian day. **That is a reason to go to the Serbian calendar for him and not a substitute for it**, so the Serbian row here still stands undocumented.

The Serbian church's Ohrid Prologue keeps him second on this day, and it is the first source this folder has in his own church: until now a Serbian despot was recorded here out of the Russian calendar alone. It calls him «Деспот српски», despot of Serbia, and gives his origin as Paštrovići, and says he governed the Serbian people in the hardest of circumstances, fighting bravely against the Turks and the Latins.

A righteous man of God and a lover of his people, the entry says, this admirable prince may be set beside [Alexander Nevsky](/saints/alexander-nevsky) or [King John Vladimir](/saints/john-vladimir) — the Prologue's own comparison, and the reason both stand on his page. A light used to appear over his grave by night, and it was by that light that his holy relics were found. They rested a long while in the Fruška Gora monastery of Šišatovac, and during the Second World War were brought to Belgrade and laid in the Cathedral church beside the body of Prince [Lazar](/saints/lazar-of-serbia).

His wife Jelena, seeing Stephen's incorrupt relics and the wonders worked by them, took the monastic habit and gave herself to the ascetic life until her death. The Prologue names her and does not call her a saint or commemorate her on this day, so she has no folder here. On his year it is less sure than the Russian life is: «Упокојио се почетком XVI века (по некима 1515. год.)», at the beginning of the sixteenth century, by some in 1515 — which is the year the Russian life states outright and the day line prints.
