# Maximus Sandovich of Gorlice

Maximus Sandovich, called of Gorlice, was born on 19 January 1886 — 31
January by the new style — in the village of Żdynia in the Carpathian
foothills, then in Austria-Hungary, in a family of Uniate Lemkos; his father
Timothy had a large farm and was psalmist of the church, his mother
Christina kept the house. After the four-class school at Gorlice nearby he
studied at Jasło and then at the gymnasium at Nowy Sącz, where his deep
faith drew the notice of his fellows in the Russian boarding-house the
Lemkos kept up by their own gifts: he sang the hymns to the Mother of God at
matins and vespers, prayed often, fasted strictly, and surprised them by his
burning wish to be a monk under the strictest rule. After four years at the
gymnasium he spent three months in the Basilian monastery at Krekhiv, was
disappointed in the spiritual life he saw there, and in 1904 entered the
Pochaev lavra just across the Russian border as a novice; Bishop Anthony
(Khrapovitsky) of Zhitomir and Volhynia noticed the able young novice on a
visit and sent him, as a reward for exemplary conduct, to the Volhynian
seminary at Zhitomir. He finished the six years at the head of his class in
1911, married Pelagia Grigoruk, daughter of the priest of Nowe Berezowo near
Hajnówka, and on 17 November 1911 was ordained priest by Anthony, now
archbishop, who offered him a place in the Kiev diocese; but he had resolved
to go home to the Lemkos, among whom the movement back from the Union to
Orthodoxy was growing, and gave himself to the hard mission among his own
people. He began at Grab, whose Lemkos had decided to return to Orthodoxy
after their Uniate priest was ordered to strike the word Orthodox from the
service and parish books and, the priest having gone on leave to America,
petitioned for Father Maximus, who was appointed by the bishop of
Chernivtsi; on 2 December 1911 he served his first liturgy at Grab, and the
persecution began at once. Denounced to the Austrian authorities, he was
held eight days and fined four hundred crowns; released, he went round the
villages — Grab, Wyszowatka, Długie — serving, and became for his people not
only a priest but a friend and a spiritual authority. On 22 December 1911
the police commissioner sealed the church at Grab and he served in houses;
on 16 January 1912 he was arrested for seven weeks in the Jasło prison, the
villagers of Grab forbidden to speak with the neighbouring villages, and
came out with his zeal unchilled — a good-natured, modest, straightforward
man whose hope passed to those he met, with a will and a character that
would not bend. Freed in the Great Fast of 1912, he was arrested again on 28
March while visiting the priest Ignatius Hudyma at Sniatyn with his wife,
this time for spying for Russia, on the ground that he had measured a bridge
over the Cheremosh; Hudyma, the law student Basil Koldra and the editor
Simon Bendasiuk were charged with him, and all were put in the Lviv prison,
where he was held with common criminals, cut off from the Orthodox and from
letters, and where an emissary of the Uniate metropolitan Sheptytsky, the
priest Nicetas Romaniuk, offered him freedom for the Union and was flatly
refused; the trial was put off for nearly two years in the hope that he
would recant.

It opened on 9 March 1914 and ran almost three months, one of the loudest of
its day, before foreign correspondents and crowds: several hundred
witnesses, experts on religion, officers of the general staff — and the
picture that emerged was of men guilty of nothing but wishing to confess the
Orthodox rather than the Greek Catholic faith and seeing in the Russians and
Ukrainians across the border only brothers in it; with the intercession of
the Emperor Nicholas II and of Archbishop Anthony and a fine defence, the
jury acquitted all four unanimously on 6 June 1914, noting that they had not
engaged in politics or sought to change the frontiers of the empire.
Released on 7 June, he went home to Żdynia while the others left for Russia
and Switzerland, and served, at Grab among other places, with his health
broken by prison, for six weeks. On 1 August 1914 the war began, and with it
the arrests of those suspected of Russian sympathies and mass reprisals
against the Orthodox; on 4 August he was arrested, his service books, his
Russian, Ukrainian and Polish books and even the icons he had brought from
Zhitomir seized, and he was beaten and marched by gendarmes to the prison at
Gorlice, and the next day his pregnant wife Pelagia, his father, his brother
Nicholas and the rest of the family were taken too. The prison was crammed
with peasants from the whole country, twenty or twenty-five to a cell, and
he heartened and comforted them all; death sentences were passed without
inquiry or trial, and trains of prisoners went west to the camps at
Thalerhof and Terezín. With the Russian front going badly the Austrian
authorities resolved to cool the Lemkos’ sympathies by putting one of them
to death, and the priest with a prison record was the readiest choice, whose
death might also end the people’s waking sense of itself. On Saturday 5
September a squad of six gendarmes came from Salzburg and a captain Dietrich
from Linz, who most likely decided on his own to have him shot without any
inquiry — a less likely account has an order from the staff at Kraków,
received at eleven that night. At five in the morning of 6 September the
warder told him to get ready; his wife and father were moved to a cell whose
window gave on the yard; at six he was led out with his hands bound behind
him and his eyes bandaged, two soldiers holding him by the arms, and set
against the wall four paces from two gendarmes of the firing party, the
place of his heart chalked on his clothes. Before the volley he had time to
cry, long live holy Orthodoxy! long live holy Rus’! — and fell, no shot
mortal, so that Dietrich finished him with his own revolver to the head,
before the eyes of his pregnant wife, his father and the prisoners of Żdynia
at their cell windows. The body was buried in the ditch under the fence of
the Gorlice cemetery where those unworthy of Christian burial were put, and
his family were refused leave to be there; only in 1922, at the plea of his
father, who like Pelagia had passed through Thalerhof, was it exhumed, laid
in a metal coffin and buried in the cemetery at Żdynia. He was one of the
first Orthodox missionaries among the Lemkos of Austria-Hungary, and his
death brought them back to Orthodoxy of themselves, Tylawa and Trzciana
leading; the Orthodox Lemkos have always held him their national hero and
spiritual father, sang and wrote verses of him, kept his little
portrait-icons in their houses, and made pilgrimages to his grave. On 9
September 1934 a monument was raised to him at Czarne, and a brotherhood in
his name was founded a year later; the seventieth anniversary was kept at
Żdynia by Bishop Adam (Dubec) of Przemyśl, who two years later laid the
foundation of the Trinity church at Gorlice, built with the help of the
Lemkos of America as a memorial of his martyrdom and consecrated in 1991,
and in 1994 a plaque was set on the district court, once the prison, where
he died. On 7 July 1994 the Holy Synod of the Polish Autocephalous Church
numbered him among the saints, the first canonisation in its history, and
the glorification was kept on 9 and 10 September 1994 at Żdynia and Gorlice
by Metropolitan Basil with the bishops of Poland, Slovakia and America and
thousands of the faithful, his relics left by the Synod’s decision in the
family grave. The life is by Jarosław Charkiewicz; the Russian calendar
keeps him on 6 September.
