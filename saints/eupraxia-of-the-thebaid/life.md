# Eupraxia of the Thebaid

In the days of the right-believing emperor Theodosius the Great there lived in Constantinople a great nobleman named Antigonus, a kinsman of the emperor, wise in word and deed and sound in counsel, merciful to men and open-handed to the poor, and after the emperor the richest man there was. He took to wife a beautiful girl of the imperial house, devout and God-fearing, named Eupraxia, who gave rich gifts to the churches; and the two of them had a daughter, whom they named Eupraxia after her mother. Soon after her birth Antigonus said to his wife that life is short and the riches of this empty world are nothing, and that they had borne one daughter to God and that was enough for them; and from then they lived together without coming together.

Antigonus died young, having lived with his wife two years and three months and the last year in continence. The widow put her daughter into the hands of the emperor and empress, begging them to be father and mother to the child for the sake of their kinsman; and many who stood by wept. When the girl was five the emperor betrothed her to a senator's son, promising to wait until she came of age. Later a senator sought the widow herself in marriage and got the empress to send women to persuade her; she answered that woe would be theirs in the age to come for counselling such a thing to one who had vowed to God to live in the purity of widowhood, and the emperor rebuked the empress sharply for it.

Grieved by that quarrel, the widow left Constantinople secretly with her daughter and a few servants, on the pretext of seeing the family property in Egypt, and went about the inner Thebaid giving alms to churches and monasteries. Near the city was a convent of a hundred and thirty nuns of whom great things were told: none of them tasted wine or oil or grapes or fruit of any kind, and some who had entered as children had never seen fruit at all; their food was bread and water, lentils and greens without oil, some eating once a day at evening, some every second and some every third day; they slept a little on a woollen sack three cubits long spread on the ground, wore hair garments down to the feet, took no medicine when they fell sick but bore the illness as a blessing, and none went outside the gate or spoke with those who came, the portress alone giving answers.

The widow loved that house and came often with her child. Once she offered the abbess twenty or thirty pounds of gold, and the abbess, a deaconess named Theodula, answered that these handmaids had no need of gold and desired no possessions, having despised all things in this world so as not to fall from the riches of heaven; let her bring a little oil for the lamps of the church, and candles and incense, and she would have her reward of the Lord. One day the abbess asked the child whether she loved the house and the sisters, and whether she loved them or her betrothed more; and the girl, not yet seven, said that she did not know him but knew and loved them. Asked where Christ was, she was shown the icon of the Saviour, ran and kissed it, and said that she too promised herself to Christ and would not go home with her mother.

Neither her mother nor the abbess could move her, and in the morning the abbess led her into the church and clothed her in the angelic habit, her mother standing by and praying that the King eternal, who had begun the good work in her, would finish it. The mother kissed her and went out, and went on through the monasteries of the Egyptian desert and the houses of the poor, giving away her goods, until the fame of her almsgiving and her abstinence came to the ears of Theodosius himself.

Some years later the abbess told her privately that she had seen Antigonus in a dream, standing in great glory before Christ and asking that his wife be called out of the body to him. The widow was not troubled but glad, called her daughter, now twelve years old, put the whole of the family property into her hands, and told her to be humble and to serve the sisters and never to think in her heart that she was of the imperial house; and on the third day she departed to the Lord and was buried in that convent. When the emperor sent word that the girl must come and be married, she read the letter and laughed, and wrote back in her own hand that she would not leave Christ for a corruptible and mortal man who is today and tomorrow is eaten of worms, and asked instead that all her parents' goods be given to the churches, the monasteries, the poor, the widows and the orphans, the slaves set free and the debtors forgiven, so that she might serve her Christ unhindered. The emperor and empress wept over the letter, had it read before the whole senate, and did as she asked.

She was twelve when she chose the harder life: at first she ate once a day at evening, then every second day, then every third, and served the sisters in the meanest work — sweeping the refectory and the cells, laying their bedding, carrying water and wood to the kitchen, boiling the food and washing the vessels — and there was none more diligent in the house. It was the rule there that a sister troubled in sleep by the devil should tell the abbess at once, and be set to gather stones under her bedding and strew ashes over it for ten days; and when Eupraxia was so troubled she did it in secret, and the abbess, seeing it, smiled and prayed for her and told her that this was the beginning of her fight with the enemy. What the page tells of her labours and her temptations after that is very long, and only its beginning and its end are given here.

At the last the abbess saw in a vision two shining men who bade her bring Eupraxia to the King, and gates of a beauty she could not tell opening of themselves upon palaces not made with hands, and a most bright King on a high throne, before whom Eupraxia fell down; and she saw millions of angels and a countless multitude of saints watching her, and the Mother of God take her and show her a most beautiful chamber and a crown made ready, and heard a voice say: Eupraxia, here is your reward and your rest; go now, and after ten days come, to have your fill of these things for ever. Telling it to the elder sisters on the tenth day, the abbess said that Eupraxia would die in the morning.

Her friend Julia overheard it and wept, and when Eupraxia adjured her to say why, and heard it, she fell to the ground as one dead and lamented, begging the Lord for one more year to weep for her sins, as for the barren fig tree, since in the grave there is no repentance. The abbess had her brought, told her the whole revelation and comforted her with it, and asked her prayers in turn. Then the fever took her; they laid her in the house of prayer; the sisters came in the morning to give her the last kiss, and she could not answer them; and when the abbess had prayed with them all and said Amen, the holy bride of Christ Eupraxia gave up her honourable and holy soul. The abbess died soon after and was buried beside her, and after that no one else was buried in that place; and many healings were done at their relics, and the devils cried out: Eupraxia, even after death you overcome us and drive us away.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 25 July. saint.gr says the venerable Eupraxia was the daughter of the senator Antigonus and of Eupraxia — the saint had the same name as her mother — who was a kinswoman of Theodosius the Great, whose reign the page dates from 379 to 395. After Antigonus died her mother handed her over to the emperor Theodosius to be settled, and he betrothed her, very young, to a certain senator. But on a journey of hers in Egypt, in her mother's company, Eupraxia visited a women's monastery at Thebes in Upper Egypt, where the abbess was a holy woman called Marina, and the life of those who struggled there pleased her so much that she decided to spend the rest of her own life with them.

Her mother raised no objection. She returned to the East, sold her landed property and came back to the monastery, and there, when she had laid down all the money from the sale, she died in peace. Her daughter Eupraxia distributed that money among the churches and the poor and gave herself to the strictest ascesis for forty-five whole years; and having lived so, ascetically and in holiness, she gave up her spirit peacefully to God, working many miracles. The Greek page adds that her right hand is at the monastery of Iviron on the Holy Mountain, and portions of her relics at the monasteries of Agathonos in Phthiotis, Phaneromeni on Salamis and Leimonos on Lesbos; and above the notice it sets a distich: «Eυπραξία πρόσεισι Xριστώ πλουσία, Πολλαίς κομώσα ψυχικαίς ευπραξίαις» — Eupraxia comes to Christ a rich woman, adorned with many good works of the soul.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her on the Julian 25 July and gives her seven sentences. She was the daughter of Antigonus, a grandee of Constantinople, and a kinswoman of the emperor [Theodosius the Great](/saints/theodosius-the-great). With her mother, a young widow, she moved to Egypt, and there the two of them went about the monasteries giving offerings and praying to God. At seven years old Eupraxia, by her own ardent wish, was made a nun in a certain women’s monastery.

The more she grew, the harder were the labours she laid upon herself: once she fasted forty days. She reposed in the year 413, in her thirtieth year. She had great grace of God, the entry ends, and healed the heaviest sicknesses.
