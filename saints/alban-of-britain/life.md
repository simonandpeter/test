# Alban of Britain

Alban, the protomartyr of Britain, was a native of Verulamium — the Roman
city whose ruins lie beside the town of St Albans, north of London, that
grew up in its place and has borne his name since the ninth century — and,
the antiquaries Butler follows say, a man of note there, who had travelled
to Rome in his youth for learning and the polite arts and lived at home with
some dignity; still a pagan, he was hospitable and compassionate. The
account is Bede’s, drawn from the last recension of the Passion of St Alban,
and Fortunatus already names him in the sixth century among the martyrs of
the whole world, glorious Alban of fruitful Britain. When the edicts of the
emperors against the Christians began to be executed in Britain, a Christian
cleric — Amphibalus, later writers call him, a name Ussher thought borrowed
from his cloak — fled from the persecutors, and Alban sheltered him in his
house. Watching his guest spend nights and days in prayer and vigil, he was
touched by grace, began to imitate his faith and piety, and, learning the
truth of salvation, put off the darkness of idolatry and became a sincere
Christian. After some days word reached the governor that the confessor
still unpunished was hidden in Alban’s house, and soldiers were sent to
search it; Alban put on his guest’s long cloak, the caracalla, went out to
the soldiers in his place, and was bound and dragged to the judge, who at
that hour was sacrificing to his gods. Enraged that the man had given
himself up in place of the one he sheltered, the judge had him brought
before the idols and told him that the punishment the blasphemer had escaped
would fall on him unless he took up the worship of the gods; Alban declared
himself openly a Christian and refused. Asked his family and race, he
answered, what is my race to you? if you would know my religion, I am a
Christian and ready for a Christian’s duty; asked his name, my parents
called me Alban, and I worship and serve always the true and living God who
made all things; told to sacrifice to the great gods if he wished to enjoy
eternal life, the sacrifices you offer to demons help no one who offers them
and grant no prayer, and whoever worships these idols will have hell’s
everlasting pains for his reward. The judge had him scourged, hoping to
break him, and seeing him bear the cruellest torments patiently and even
joyfully, condemned him to be beheaded.

As he was led out to the arena beyond the walls, a great crowd of every sex,
rank and age had gone out before him to see the confessor, so that the
bridge over the swift river between the city and the place of execution —
the Ver, or, Butler thinks, the Colne — could scarcely be crossed that
evening, and the judge was left in the city almost alone. Impatient for his
crown, Alban went down to the bank and lifted his eyes to heaven, and the
river bed dried at that place and the waters parted to give him a way. The
executioner, moved by what he saw, ran to him, threw down his sword and fell
at his feet, begging to die with him or in his place. While the other
executioners hesitated to take up the sword, the confessor went up with the
crowd a gentle hill five hundred paces from the arena, thick with wild
flowers of every kind, and at its top asked God for water, and a spring
sprang up at his feet — the river having done its office and gone back to
its bed, the hill kept its witness. There he was beheaded and received the
crown of life, and the man who struck the blow did not rejoice in it, for
his eyes fell to the ground with the martyr’s head. The soldier who had
refused to kill him was beheaded on the same spot, washed, Bede says, though
not in the water of baptism, in his own blood — Heraclius, Capgrave calls
him, and the Roman martyrology keeps him too. The judge, frightened by the
signs from heaven, ordered the persecution stopped. Bede puts the martyrdom
under Diocletian, on 22 June, most writers in 303 and some in 286; other
sources set it under Septimius Severus, about 209. The cleric he saved was
afterwards brought back and stoned at Redbourn, three miles from St Albans,
the abbey’s records said, and a thousand who followed him into Wales were
baptised and cut down there. In the peace of the Church a church of
wonderful workmanship was built where he suffered, famous for its miracles
in Bede’s day, destroyed by the pagan Saxons and raised again by Offa of
Mercia in 793 with a great monastery, whose abbot sat first among the mitred
abbots in parliament and whose church, bought by the townsmen at the
dissolution, still stands; his shrine, adorned by Offa and his heirs, was
gone by the seventeenth century but for a marble stone over the place where
his dust lies. By his intercession Germanus of Auxerre won his bloodless
victory in the fifth century, and for many ages the island had recourse to
him as its protomartyr and patron. The Serbian calendar keeps him on 23
August of the old style, 5 September, and the Holy Synod of the Russian
Church entered him in its calendar on 9 March 2017, on 22 June of the old
style, 5 July; the Roman martyrology keeps him on 22 June.
