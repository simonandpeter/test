# Alban of Britain

Alban, the protomartyr of Britain, was a native of Verulamium — the Roman
city whose ruins lie beside the town of St Albans, north of London, that
grew up in its place and has borne his name since the ninth century — and,
the antiquaries Butler follows say, a man of note there, who had travelled
to Rome in his youth for learning and the polite arts and lived at home with
some dignity; still a pagan, he was hospitable and compassionate. The
account is Bede’s, drawn from the last recension of the Passion of St Alban,
and Fortunatus already names him in the sixth century among the martyrs of
the whole world, glorious Alban of fruitful Britain.

When the edicts of the emperors against the Christians began to be executed
in Britain, a Christian cleric — Amphibalus, later writers call him, a name
Ussher thought borrowed from his cloak — fled from the persecutors, and
Alban sheltered him in his house. Watching his guest spend nights and days
in prayer and vigil, he was touched by grace, began to imitate his faith and
piety, and, learning the truth of salvation, put off the darkness of
idolatry and became a sincere Christian.

After some days word reached the governor that the confessor still
unpunished was hidden in Alban’s house, and soldiers were sent to search it;
Alban put on his guest’s long cloak, the caracalla, went out to the soldiers
in his place, and was bound and dragged to the judge, who at that hour was
sacrificing to his gods. Enraged that the man had given himself up in place
of the one he sheltered, the judge had him brought before the idols and told
him that the punishment the blasphemer had escaped would fall on him unless
he took up the worship of the gods; Alban declared himself openly a
Christian and refused.

Asked his family and race, he answered, what is my race to you? if you would
know my religion, I am a Christian and ready for a Christian’s duty; asked
his name, my parents called me Alban, and I worship and serve always the
true and living God who made all things; told to sacrifice to the great gods
if he wished to enjoy eternal life, the sacrifices you offer to demons help
no one who offers them and grant no prayer, and whoever worships these idols
will have hell’s everlasting pains for his reward. The judge had him
scourged, hoping to break him, and seeing him bear the cruellest torments
patiently and even joyfully, condemned him to be beheaded.

As he was led out to the arena beyond the walls, a great crowd of every sex,
rank and age had gone out before him to see the confessor, so that the
bridge over the swift river between the city and the place of execution —
the Ver, or, Butler thinks, the Colne — could scarcely be crossed that
evening, and the judge was left in the city almost alone. Impatient for his
crown, Alban went down to the bank and lifted his eyes to heaven, and the
river bed dried at that place and the waters parted to give him a way.

The executioner, moved by what he saw, ran to him, threw down his sword and
fell at his feet, begging to die with him or in his place. While the other
executioners hesitated to take up the sword, the confessor went up with the
crowd a gentle hill five hundred paces from the arena, thick with wild
flowers of every kind, and at its top asked God for water, and a spring
sprang up at his feet — the river having done its office and gone back to
its bed, the hill kept its witness.

There he was beheaded and received the crown of life, and the man who struck
the blow did not rejoice in it, for his eyes fell to the ground with the
martyr’s head. The soldier who had refused to kill him was beheaded on the
same spot, washed, Bede says, though not in the water of baptism, in his own
blood — Heraclius, Capgrave calls him, and the Roman martyrology keeps him
too. The judge, frightened by the signs from heaven, ordered the persecution
stopped. Bede puts the martyrdom under Diocletian, on 22 June, most writers
in 303 and some in 286; other sources set it under Septimius Severus, about
209.

The cleric he saved was afterwards brought back and stoned at Redbourn,
three miles from St Albans, the abbey’s records said, and a thousand who
followed him into Wales were baptised and cut down there. In the peace of
the Church a church of wonderful workmanship was built where he suffered,
famous for its miracles in Bede’s day, destroyed by the pagan Saxons and
raised again by Offa of Mercia in 793 with a great monastery, whose abbot
sat first among the mitred abbots in parliament and whose church, bought by
the townsmen at the dissolution, still stands; his shrine, adorned by Offa
and his heirs, was gone by the seventeenth century but for a marble stone
over the place where his dust lies.

By his intercession Germanus of Auxerre won his bloodless victory in the
fifth century, and for many ages the island had recourse to him as its
protomartyr and patron. The Serbian calendar keeps him on 23 August of the
old style, 5 September, and the Holy Synod of the Russian Church entered him
in its calendar on 9 March 2017, on 22 June of the old style, 5 July; the
Roman martyrology keeps him on 22 June.

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 22 June, as «Sfântul Mucenic Alban din Verulamul Britaniei» — Alban of Verulamium in Britain — and its page prints no full life, only one paragraph: he was still a pagan at the time when the cruel Roman emperors had made laws against the Christians, when he sheltered in his own house a Christian priest who was fleeing the persecutors; and seeing his guest keep watch day and night in unceasing prayer, he felt the breath of God's grace and began to follow that example of faith and reverence.

The Greek calendar keeps him on 17 June, where the Romanian keeps him on 22 June and the Serbian on the Julian 23 August, and saint.gr's account carries the same doubt about the year as the folder already does: Alban was the first martyr in the British Isles, put to death at Verulamium, today's Saint Albans, which took its name from him, most likely during the persecution of the emperor Diocletian in 303 or 304, although some say he died in the reign of Septimius Severus about 209.

Following the story the Venerable Bede tells, the page says he sheltered in his house a priest who was hiding from his pursuers, was so impressed by his guest's goodness that he received his preaching with great zeal and was baptised, and when within a few days it became known that the priest was hiding there and soldiers came to arrest him, put on the priest's clothes, gave himself up in his place and went to trial.

The page prints the exchange. Asked what family he was of, he answered that this was no concern of the judge's, and that he would have him know he was a Christian; pressed again, he said his parents had named him Alban and that he worshipped the living and true God, the maker of all things; and told to sacrifice to the great gods at once if he wished to enjoy eternal life, he answered that they sacrificed to demons, who can give no help and no answer to the desires of the heart, and that the reward of such sacrifices is the unending punishment of hell. The judge, angry that the priest had got away, threatened him with death if he went on denying the gods of Rome; he answered boldly that he was a Christian and would burn no incense to them, and was condemned to be tortured and then beheaded.

As he was led to the hill where the cathedral of Saint Albans now stands, the page says, the crowd following him crossed the river Coln dry-shod at the martyr's prayer; and the wonder so touched the executioner's heart that he threw down his sword, fell at Alban's feet, declared himself a Christian and begged to suffer either for him or with him, so that another soldier took up the sword, and, in Bede's words, the brave martyr's head was struck off and he received the crown of life which God has promised to those who love him.

A jet of water sprang from the place of execution, and it is said that as the head fell to the ground the executioner's eyes started out of their sockets. The page adds that the commander ordered the persecution stopped and honour paid to the martyrs, that the shrine was emptied in the persecution of Henry VIII, and that in 2002 the church of Saint Panteleimon at Cologne, which had held relics of him for centuries, gave a portion back to the place of his martyrdom.
