# Poemen the Great

Poemen and his brothers made a small monastic brotherhood in a little skete
in Egypt, and Poemen was its abbot, with every quality of a true shepherd
of souls. His fame reached far, and many came to see him and ask his
counsel; but he received only the small and the humble, and those who came
out of curiosity he would not receive, even when they were magistrates.

One such visitor, a judge, angry at being refused, arrested the only son of
the abbot’s sister, thinking Poemen would then come to him. Poemen wrote
instead: examine my nephew according to the laws; if he is guilty, punish
him; if not, do as you please — and the judge, admiring the letter,
released the young man. All this Poemen achieved because he cultivated the
foundation of the virtues, humility; a man needs humility, he often said,
as much as the air he breathes. He died in peace in the year 450, at the
age of 110.

The Russian and Serbian calendars keep him the same day, 27 August
(Julian), with the troparion that says he tilled the barren desert with the
streams of his tears. The Sretensky calendar has him born about 340 in
Egypt; with his two brothers Anubius and Paisius he went to an Egyptian
monastery, where all three were tonsured, and they were such strict
ascetics that when their mother came to see her children they would not
come out of their cells — she stood long and wept, and Poemen said to her
through the closed door: if you bear being parted from us for a time, you
will see us in the life to come, so we hope in God’s love for man; do you
wish rather to see us here, the Prologue has him ask, or there in
eternity? — and she went away, content. As a boy, the Prologue says, he
visited the famous spiritual men and gathered experience from them as a
bee gathers honey, and when Paisius saw him he said, this child will save
many; the hand of God is with him. In the brothers’ monastery, ruled by the
eldest, Abba Anubius, the rule was four hours of the night at handiwork,
four asleep and four singing psalms, the day from morning to noon in work
and prayer by turns, from noon to vespers in reading, and in the afternoon
the cooking of the one meal in twenty-four hours, usually of some greens —
we ate what was set before us, Poemen says, and no one ever said, give me
something else, or I will not have that, and so we passed our whole life in
silence and peace. The fame of his labours spread through the whole
country, and when the governor of the region wished to see him he refused,
lest the great should come and honour him and then the people, and he lose
the grace of humility gained with God’s help. Many monks had him for
spiritual guide and wrote down his answers for their own instruction and
others’. Should a brother’s sin be covered with silence if one happens to
see it? — if we hide our brothers’ sins God will hide ours; and if you see
a brother sinning, do not trust your eyes, but know that your sins are like
a beam and your brother’s like a splinter, and then you will never fall
into confusion or be scandalised. Another said he had sinned gravely and
wished to spend three years in repentance — was that enough? That is a great
deal, said the elder; and pressed for the term he thought right, a year or
forty days, he said: I think that if a man repents with his whole heart and
firmly resolves never to return to the sin, God will accept even three
days’ repentance. How to be rid of nagging evil thoughts? If a man has fire
on one side of him and a vessel of water on the other, when the fire
catches him he takes water from the vessel and puts it out; so the evil
thoughts the enemy of our salvation suggests can kindle sinful desires in a
man like a spark, and those sparks must be put out with water — prayer, and
the soul’s reaching out to God. He was a severe faster who took no food for
a week and more, but advised others to eat every day, without surfeit; of a
monk who allowed himself food only on the seventh day and was angry with a
brother he said, he has learned to fast six days and cannot keep from anger
for one. Which is better, to speak or to keep silent? He who speaks for
God’s sake does well, and he who keeps silent for God’s sake does well too;
and again: a man may seem to be silent, but if his heart condemns others he
is talking all the time — and there are those who talk all day with their
tongue and keep silence within, because they condemn no one. A man must
keep three chief rules, he said: to fear God, to pray often, and to do good
to people; and malice will never destroy malice — if someone does you evil,
do him good, and your good will conquer his malice. Once, having come with
his disciples to the Egyptian desert (he was in the habit of moving from
place to place to escape the praise of men) he learned that an elder living
there resented his coming and envied him, and to conquer the hermit’s
malice he went to him with the brethren, bringing food to treat him; the
elder refused to come out, and Poemen said, we will not leave here until we
are found worthy to see and venerate the holy elder — and stood in the heat
of the sun at the door of his cell, until, seeing such patience and
meekness, the elder received him with love and said: what I heard of you is
just, but I see in you good deeds a hundred times greater. So he knew how
to put out malice and gave others the example, and he had such humility
that he often said with a sigh, I shall be thrown where Satan is thrown.
Once a monk came from far away to hear his instruction and began on lofty
and hard-to-grasp matters, and Poemen turned from him and kept silent; the
astonished monk was told that the elder did not like talk of lofty things,
and when he asked instead about the struggle with the passions of the soul
the saint turned to him with a glad face — now you have spoken well, and I
will answer you — and taught him long how the passions are fought and
overcome. He died at the age of a hundred and ten, about the year 450, and
was soon acknowledged a saint of God and given the name Great — for his
great humility, modesty, truthfulness and self-denying service of God. The
Prologue’s verses give his sayings too: to brothers who asked how he could
hear a quarrel unmoved, I died long ago; flies do not settle on boiling
water, and from a warm soul the devils flee; malice is not conquered by
malice — strive to do the malicious man some good, and his heart will be
kindled.
