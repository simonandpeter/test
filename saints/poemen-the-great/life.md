# Poemen the Great

Poemen and his brothers made a small monastic brotherhood in a little skete in Egypt, and Poemen was its abbot, with every quality of a true shepherd of souls. His fame reached far, and many came to see him and ask his counsel; but he received only the small and the humble, and those who came out of curiosity he would not receive, even when they were magistrates.

One such visitor, a judge, angry at being refused, arrested the only son of the abbot’s sister, thinking Poemen would then come to him. Poemen wrote instead: examine my nephew according to the laws; if he is guilty, punish him; if not, do as you please — and the judge, admiring the letter, released the young man. All this Poemen achieved because he cultivated the foundation of the virtues, humility; a man needs humility, he often said, as much as the air he breathes. He died in peace in the year 450, at the age of 110.

The Russian and Serbian calendars keep him the same day, 27 August (Julian), with the troparion that says he tilled the barren desert with the streams of his tears. The Sretensky calendar has him born about 340 in Egypt; with his two brothers Anubius and Paisius he went to an Egyptian monastery, where all three were tonsured, and they were such strict ascetics that when their mother came to see her children they would not come out of their cells — she stood long and wept, and Poemen said to her through the closed door: if you bear being parted from us for a time, you will see us in the life to come, so we hope in God’s love for man; do you wish rather to see us here, the Prologue has him ask, or there in eternity? — and she went away, content. As a boy, the Prologue says, he visited the famous spiritual men and gathered experience from them as a bee gathers honey, and when Paisius saw him he said, this child will save many; the hand of God is with him. In the brothers’ monastery, ruled by the eldest, Abba Anubius, the rule was four hours of the night at handiwork, four asleep and four singing psalms, the day from morning to noon in work and prayer by turns, from noon to vespers in reading, and in the afternoon the cooking of the one meal in twenty-four hours, usually of some greens — we ate what was set before us, Poemen says, and no one ever said, give me something else, or I will not have that, and so we passed our whole life in silence and peace. The fame of his labours spread through the whole country, and when the governor of the region wished to see him he refused, lest the great should come and honour him and then the people, and he lose the grace of humility gained with God’s help; many monks had him for spiritual guide and wrote down his answers — the Sretensky calendar’s life runs on past the point read here. He reposed in peace in deep old age, in the fifth century; the Prologue’s verses give his sayings: to brothers who asked how he could hear a quarrel unmoved, I died long ago; flies do not settle on boiling water, and from a warm soul the devils flee; malice is not conquered by malice — strive to do the malicious man some good, and his heart will be kindled.
