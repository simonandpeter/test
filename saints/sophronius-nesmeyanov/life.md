# Sophronius (Nesmeyanov)

The Russian church calendar of the Сретенский monastery names him fourteenth of fifteen on 21 октября ст. ст., the first of the two the line calls прмчч., venerable-martyrs, after eleven priests and two deacons of 1937. He was a Don Cossack, born on 11 March 1870 into a peasant Orthodox family of the village of Khrushchevka in the second district of the Don region, and the calendar's life opens by calling him one of those men, brave and self-possessed from birth, who carry the cross of a soldier's or a peasant's or a priest's obedience with the same evenness.

Called up at twenty-one, he was put into an artillery brigade at Bendery and served out his time as the unit's carpenter. When it ended in 1899 he took work as a carpenter at a machine works and stayed there nine years. He was thirty-eight when he made his decisive choice, left the world and the trade that fed him, and entered the monastery of the Holy Spirit at Tsaritsyn as a novice; he was tonsured there with the same name and was soon made steward. In 1910 a bishop took him as his cell-attendant and ordained him hierodeacon, and in 1915 a vicar bishop of the Saratov diocese ordained him hieromonk to a church in Saratov. In 1917 he lived some months in the Khvalynsk monastery and was then sent to a missionary school at Podlesnoye.

In January 1919, judging that he could not stand aside while the country was being pulled apart by civil war, he went south and joined the volunteer army as a regimental priest, and served until the white movement was broken. He did not emigrate with it; he chose to stay in his own country whatever it cost him. In 1920 he entered a monastery metochion at Yekaterinodar, whose superior was arrested and exiled soon afterwards and which was closed in 1922. He served in village parishes, came back to the Saratov diocese in 1924, and was moved from one village to another; arrested in 1926 for holding regular classes in the Law of God and nearly brought to trial, he had in the end to move again, to the village of Yuryevka.

Those were the years of the Renovationist schism, and the Renovationist head of the Urals diocese was one of its most energetic men. He not only preached the rightness of the movement but staged public disputes, and he knew that a document carries further than an argument: at every dispute he produced a paper signed by the Ecumenical Patriarch's representative in Moscow to the effect that the Renovationist Synod alone was recognised by the Patriarchs of the East, and called on the Orthodox before the whole hall to produce theirs. The Orthodox had no way of corresponding with any church abroad, and confusion spread among them.

In the end the parishes resolved to get hold of papers of their own, and since no one else had much appetite for the journey the task fell to Fr Sophronius. A petition was drawn up in the parishioners' name to the chairman of the central executive committee, asking leave to apply to the Eastern Patriarchs. The errand was deeply ecclesiastical, urgent, and in a time of persecution far from safe, so he went first for a blessing to his ruling archbishop, whom he knew for a great ascetic, and received it gladly given.

He reached Moscow on 23 December 1927 and was received at the patriarchate by a member of the temporary Synod, who told him that there was indeed correspondence with the Ecumenical and Eastern Patriarchs and that the letters of recognition existed; he asked to see them and to take copies, and was allowed to. The Greek representative, whom he met by chance in the Synod's waiting room, promised him a document of his own and gave it to him at his flat that evening, certifying that the patriarchal church was recognised by the Ecumenical Patriarchate. On 12 January 1928 he carried the papers to the village of Boltai. Renovationist priests who looked at the document were indignant and told him he would be arrested for it and it would be taken from him, and that is what happened.

He was arrested in February and held, and on 13 April the Saratov state police ordered him kept in the provincial isolator. He did not admit guilt. On 19 April he wrote to them from prison that everything taken from him in the search was of a church character; that the Greek archimandrite had given him the document before witnesses and had since denied giving it and called it a forgery; that the signature and the seal could be compared to settle it; that if the denial stood, the archimandrite's authority was lost with every priest and believer, since the Orthodox had trusted him to be an impartial judge between themselves and the Renovationists; and that he asked to be released on his own recognisance or into the keeping of his community.

Meanwhile his parishioners petitioned for him so persistently that the village soviet took fright. The indictment of 20 April called him a priest who had carried on propaganda to stir up religious enmity and superstition, an ardent defender of the patriarchal line and an irreconcilable enemy of the Soviet power, and proposed, for want of material fit for an open trial, that he be exiled by administrative order. Moscow's reviewer wrote that the charge was not established, and on 17 August 1928 the special council ordered him released, counting the time already served as his punishment.

He served next at Ulybovka, and the next wave took him. Arrested on 29 December 1930 as he was leaving the village, and accused of grouping women against the grain levies and the state loans and of breaking up the collective farm's work, he answered on 17 January 1931 that he had nowhere agitated against the measures of the party and the authorities, had grouped neither women nor men, had not spoken against the levies or collectivisation or the loan, had prepared no one to overthrow anything and had threatened nobody. He was exiled. In 1933 he came back to Volsk and took work as a watchman in a church; wanting to be more than a watchman, he went to Tver in 1935 to ask for a parish, and was sent to the Kashin district, then to another village, and in February 1937 to the church of Lozyevo in the Bezhetsk district. He was sixty-six.

The NKVD began collecting testimony against him on 1 September 1937, from a district party propagandist, from the woman in whose house he had first lodged, who was a member of the parish council and its psalm-reader, and from the chairman of the village soviet. He was arrested on 22 September. He denied each charge as it came — the gathering in a villager's hut, the stirring up of a crowd on 25 August, the founding of a society of abstainers, the counter-revolutionary reading. Then, when the whole accusation was read out to him, he fell silent and said nothing at all, and the investigator called in a brigade of three to make him speak.

He was silent under them. The torture was agonising, the calendar's life says, but not terrible, and there was no way of making the priest testify against himself; in the end the investigators wrote that the accused refused to answer the questions put to him, did not admit guilt, and refused to sign for reasons unknown. He was by then in no state to be questioned further, and an indictment was written repeating all the false testimony. Unsatisfied, the investigator came back to him a day later, and now the priest read the written questions and answers himself and signed under each: he had not spread rumours of the Soviet power's fall and did not admit guilt; to the rest he refused to answer and did not admit guilt. Under his last answer he wrote in his own hand that he agreed with the answers but did not agree with the questions.

The brigade was called again, and the same questions were put, and met with the same firm replies or with silence. At the end of the last protocol he wrote, in his own hand again, that he refused to sign it. On 1 November 1937 the NKVD troika sentenced the hieromonk to be shot, and he was shot two days later, on 3 November 1937. He was numbered among the saints at the Jubilee Council of Bishops in August 2000.
