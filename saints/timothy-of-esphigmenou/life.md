# Timothy of Esphigmenou

Timothy was born Triantaphyllos in the village of Paraora, in the district of Kessani in Thrace. He married and had two daughters, and then, by the devil's working, his wife left him with the children, went over to the Muslim faith and married a Turk. After a time he sent word urging her to come back to Orthodoxy. It seems she answered that she agreed, for she repented of leaving her family and of her apostasy, but that she could not escape at once from the state she was in.

So Triantaphyllos presented himself to the qadi and asked to be circumcised. In this way he meant to appear to accept the religion of Islam, doing it only so that he might get his wife out. He was reconciled with her, and she managed to break off from her Muslim husband; the two went to live at Kessani under the outward form of Muslims, while in secret they were Christians. After some months, however, they took leave of their daughters and went to Kydonia, where his wife entered a convent of nuns.

Triantaphyllos went to the Holy Mountain and entered the Great Lavra, where he worked as a gardener. Later he was tonsured in that same monastery and took the name Timothy. He remained at the Great Lavra six years, growing in the spiritual life, and afterwards moved to the monastery of Esphigmenou, where in time he received the great schema. Hearing of the martyrdom of the New Martyr Agathangelos of Esphigmenou, who had suffered in that very year, the desire of martyrdom was born in him also.

He took the blessing of his abbot Euthymius and went to the Hellespont, where he met the priest Germanos, to whom he carried letters of recommendation. Germanos gave him worldly clothes to wear, and the two went into the town of Kessani and tried to bring the apostates back to the Christian faith. His work was soon found out by the authorities, and he was seized and taken to the prison at Adrianople, where he met another monk named Barnabas, whom the Muslims had put to the torture.

Soon afterwards the priest Germanos visited him in the prison and brought him Holy Communion. Both endured many dishonours and torments and stayed faithful to their Christian faith. The steadfastness of the two Orthodox Christians so troubled the Muslims that, astonishingly, they released Euthymius and Barnabas but kept Timothy back, and condemned him to death. On 29 October 1820 he was beheaded, and his holy relics were thrown into the river.

Part of his garments, stained with his blood, was bought by Germanos and given to the martyr's daughters. The rest of the clothing was sent to the monastery of Esphigmenou on the Holy Mountain.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 29 October, and saint.gr tells the whole story, which turns on a feigned apostasy. His homeland was the village of Paraora in the district of Kessani in Thrace, and in the world he was called Triantaphyllos; he married and had two daughters. His wife left him and married a Turk, and when she came to feel her fault she could not easily be freed of the man; so Triantaphyllos thought to embrace Mohammedanism in appearance, to get her out of those hands, and then for the two of them to become monks. He went to the court and said that if they gave him back his wife he would accept becoming a Muslim; the court approved with much satisfaction, circumcised him, and gave him his wife. Some months later the two slipped away to Kydonies, where he settled her in a women's monastery and left for the Holy Mountain himself.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 29 October and gives him a life the Romanian and Greek pages do not. He was from Kisani, a village of Thrace. He was married and had two daughters. The Turks seized his wife and made her a Muslim; and to get her out of the harem he outwardly turned Muslim himself. Having got her out, he put her into a women’s monastery, and went himself to the Holy Mountain — first to the Lavra, and afterwards to Esphigmenou.

There he came to long for martyrdom for Christ, after the pattern of [Agathangelus](/saints/agathangelus-of-esphigmenou) of the same monastery, whom this corpus records as dying in 1818 or 1819, a year or two before him. He was beheaded at Adrianople on 29 October 1820 — the day and the year this folder already recorded, with the city added. His body was thrown into the river; his garment was carried to the monastery of Esphigmenou by the elder Herman, his spiritual father.
