# Isaac of Constantinople

After the finding of the honoured and life-giving Cross and the reign of Constantine the Great, doxologia's life says, the old enemy brought a new trouble into the Church of God: he moved the emperor Valens to defend and to spread the heresy of Arius. Valens raised a persecution against the Orthodox. Some churches he ordered shut, so that the divine service could not be done in them; some he turned into stables for horses; and some he pulled down to the foundations. The wickedness lasted a long time, and the servants of Christ prayed day and night.

Isaac was at first a monk of the eastern desert, following the prophet Elijah and living a life like the angels. Hearing of the persecution the Arians were making against the Church, and of the emperor who helped them, he grieved greatly, left the desert and came to Constantinople, strengthening the faithful in right belief; his word, the life says, was like a lit lamp, and the Spirit of God rested upon him. He prayed that God would look upon those unjustly afflicted, and God raised against Valens the inroad of the barbarians, who crossed the Danube with a great army, took Thrace and came near the City.

Valens had to gather his soldiers and go out to meet them. As he left Constantinople, Isaac came out into the road and cried with a loud voice: emperor, open the churches of the Orthodox and God will prosper your way before you. The emperor did not answer, but despised him as a simpleton and went on. The next day the old man met him again: open the churches and the war will go well, for you will overcome your enemies and return safe and in peace. Valens weighed those last words and began to take counsel about opening the churches, but an Arian general advised him not to listen, and he drove the monk off with mockery and blows.

On the third day Isaac caught the bridle of the emperor's horse and pressed him harder, warning him of God's vengeance if he would not do it. There was a deep ravine there full of cruel thorns and a pool of mud in which even a beast would have sunk and died, and Valens ordered him thrown into it to die there. Isaac lay among the thorns and in the water as on a soft bed among flowers, blessing and singing to the Lord, until three men bearing light appeared, raised him out of the pool whole and unhurt, set him on dry ground and became invisible.

He gave thanks on his knees, then ran by another road and stood again before the emperor, who was frightened at the sight. You wished to kill me in the thorns and the mud, he said, and the Lord kept me alive and brought me out of that pit by His holy angels; hear me, emperor, and open the churches of the Orthodox, that you may conquer and return in glory; and if you will not hear me, you will not come back from the war, but will perish there. Valens gave him to two nobles, Saturninus and Victor, to keep in chains until his return. Isaac cried after him that he would lead his soldiers out and not be able to overcome the enemy, but would flee before them, be taken, and be burned alive with fire.

When the armies met, the barbarians prevailed, and many fell by the sword. The emperor was wounded and fled with the Arian counsellor who had advised him. Coming to a village, tired, he got down from his horse and hid in some straw with him; the barbarians, learning that Greeks were hidden there, set fire round about and burned them both. Some soldiers, not knowing it, came to Isaac in his chains and told him to prepare his answer, for the emperor was returning to question and torment him. Seven days ago, he said, I smelled the stench of his burned bones. In a few days all knew of Valens's end, and they loosed Isaac and honoured him as a prophet of God.

Gratian, the emperor of Rome, came to the Greeks' help and set Theodosius over them, the one called the Great; and Theodosius gathered the scattered army, beat the barbarians and entered Constantinople in triumph. Saturninus and Victor told him of Isaac's boldness and his prophecy. The emperor had him brought with honour, bowed to him and asked his prayers for himself and for all his empire, and Isaac counselled him to keep the right faith and to give the Church back her peace. Theodosius did everything he said, and drove the Arians out of Constantinople, to the great joy of the faithful.

Isaac wished then to go back to the desert and to his first manner of life, but the two nobles begged him to stay in the imperial city and help the Church with his prayers and his teaching, and he yielded. They fell to quarrelling over which of them should house him, until he told them he would live with whichever first built him a dwelling. Saturninus was the quicker, on a fine place outside the city fit for the monastic life, and there Isaac settled; he went sometimes to Victor's house too, and stayed a few days, so as not to slight him.

Many came to him wishing to be tonsured, and out of the wealth of those two God-loving men a great monastery was founded, with Isaac as guide and abbot of many monks and a useful teacher of laymen as well. He was very merciful to the poor, and when he had nothing to give he would take the garment off his back and give that. Coming to deep old age and near his blessed end, he called the brethren, taught them what was of use to their souls, and set over them in his place a man honoured and holy in life, the blessed Dalmatus, from whom that house was afterwards named Dalmatou. The whole city gathered for his burial, and his body was laid in the church of the Protomartyr Stephen. The life names Constantine, Valens, Gratian and Theodosius, but prints no year, so none is recorded here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and gives the life the Romanian line did not. Isaakios came from Syria, saint.gr says, and lived in the years of Valens, who was a supporter of the Arians. When the Ostrogoths encamped in Thrace against the government's prohibition and were threatening Constantinople, Valens was forced to march against them; and Isaakios, who was abbot of the monastery of the Dalmatians, came out to meet this enemy of the Orthodox, caught his horse by the bridle and told him: give the flocks back their best shepherds and you will take the victory without labour; but if you do none of these things and give battle, you will learn by experience that it is hard to kick against the goads, for you will not come back, and you will lose your army besides. Valens was not persuaded, mocked him, and had him thrown into a steep ravine, where by a wonder he took no hurt at all; and on 9 August 378 the imperial army was routed around Adrianople, many of its best generals killed, and Valens himself, taking refuge in a barn to save his life, was burned alive there with his commander-in-chief. As abbot he was present at the Second Ecumenical Council, which met at Constantinople in 381, and contributed greatly to its success. Foreseeing his own end, he appointed the venerable Dalmatius his successor and fell asleep in peace in deep old age in the year 396.
