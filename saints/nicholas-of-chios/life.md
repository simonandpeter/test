# Nicholas of Chios

saint.gr's calendar for 31 Οκτωβρίου names «Άγιος Νικόλαος από τη Χίο ο Νεομάρτυρας», and gives him one of the longest lives on the site. He was martyred on 31 October 1754, at the sixth hour, at the place called Vounaki on Chios. He was born at Karyes of Chios, of devout Christian parents, Peter and Stamatou; from a small child he was graced in body and soul, and he lived as a Christian with much reverence and self-restraint although he grew up without a father's advice, being an orphan. Above all, the page says, he was simple and without malice, and everyone wondered at his patience.

At twenty he agreed with a fellow-countryman, a builder, to go together to Magnesia in Asia Minor to work; and there he kept his Christian way of life and went on in virtue. Then something happened to his mind and he was left deranged, though without doing mad things. Seeing him in that state the Turks brought him to their chiefs to make a Muslim of him; but when they questioned him he answered nothing and stayed silent, as though he did not hear, and the aghas in exasperation beat him, drove him off as a madman, and rebuked the men who had brought him.

His countrymen, fearing for his mind, took him back to Chios to his sister, and told her everything. She, from foolishness, did not keep it secret, and a rumour went about that Nicholas had turned Turk. The aghas of the island heard it, took him, named him Mehmet and dressed him as a Turk — without circumcising him — and he lived by herding the butchers' animals. There, in the mountains of Saint Hypomone, an archimandrite named Cyril met him, talked with him, saw his simplicity and gave him some counsel; and that was the beginning of the change in him.

One night he slept in a half-ruined church of Saint Anne, and saw in a dream a most beautiful maiden who told him to go to the priest of her Son's church to be washed, that he might be made well and she might take him for a bridegroom. He ran to his sister with the dream. The village priest paid them no attention, but the archimandrite Cyril, serving at the church of the Saviour, blessed water for him and read the prayers over him, and the young man came to his right mind; after which Cyril catechised and taught him. From then on Nicholas lived in great repentance, in prayer, in vigil and in strict fasting. But because it had been heard that he had turned Turk, his own villagers feared the Turks' anger and would not receive him in church, for all his tears and protest.

One day men came from the judge and arrested him as though he were a brigand, and with him the village priest and two elders. Asked why, having been a Muslim, he was now a Christian again, he answered that he was born of Christians and brought up a Christian and was a Christian, and had never denied his Christ nor become a Muslim nor ever would, but would die a Christian. Flatteries and promises only kindled him; he rebuked the ignorance and the error of the crowd of Turks and nobody could answer him a word, and so, shamed by a simple unlettered young Greek, they turned to threats and beat him most cruelly with five hundred strokes of the rod on his feet, and threw him into prison with his wounded feet clamped in the stocks.

Brought back to the court, he met the same flatteries — offices, riches, honours — and the same threats, and answered that he accepted neither their flatteries nor feared their punishments and death: he was a Christian, and nothing would part him from the love of Christ; but if they would first grant him one request, he would then obey them. Not knowing what he would ask, they said yes gladly. Then, he said, consent first that I baptise you Christians, and afterwards do with me what you will. They were so angered that they devised new torments: they poured water into the prison, laid down a board of nails and stretched the martyr on it, and set a heavy slab on his chest and belly, chained his neck and kept his feet in the stocks; and he bore it all patiently, glorifying God.

In the night there was an earthquake, the slab fell off him, and it was found that neither were his bones broken nor had the nails gone into his back, and the prison was filled with a sweet smell. All the prisoners were astonished and cried out that the man was a saint, and the priest asked his forgiveness for his blasphemous words. After that they released his villagers, so that they should not see the wonders or spread them, and bound him hand and foot and threw him into the horses' stable, both to keep him from the other prisoners and so that the horses should trample him to death; but he was kept whole and unhurt, the page says, as Daniel was in the lions' pit, and all those days he fasted almost without food and prayed.

Seeing that they gained nothing, the Turks condemned him to death. They led him outside the walls of the Souda of the castle and asked him again whether he would turn Turk, and he, worn out, answered no with a movement of his head only. The executioner made him kneel and stabbed him in the back, then raised him and asked again; made him kneel a second time and cut him a little in the neck, raised him and asked again, saying, do not be distressed, your wounds can be healed. Then the martyr, out of his great longing to suffer, ran and knelt of himself, crying three times, Panagia, help me; and the executioner struck with all his strength, again and again, and the honoured head would not be cut, until he took him by the hair and killed him like a sheep.

Then, the page says, a shattering thing happened: though it was midday, a thick darkness covered the whole island, so that nobody could see another or the road to his house, and in the rest of the island, where the people did not know the cause, they said it was surely the wrath of God; to this day they tell of that terrible darkness. While the darkness held everywhere, the martyr's face shone like the sun, and a heavenly light bathed the holy relic three nights. Unable to bear these signs, the Turks said God was sending fire to burn him, and went with torches and blackened the saint's face so that it should not look bright. Many Christians bribed the guards for pieces of his clothes, or earth wet with his blood, or the cutting off of one of his fingers; and his relics worked strange wonders, as his synaxarion says. At the last, so that the Christians should not take the body and honour it, the impious threw it into the sea, and nobody ever learned where it came ashore.
