# John Lampadistes

John lived in the tenth century and came from the village of Lampadou in Cyprus, near the small town of Galata, the Greek synaxarion says. He was the only child of the priest Cyriacus and his wife Anna, pious and wealthy people who had him after long and heartfelt prayer, and he outstripped all his age in learning the holy writings. Once, before 6 August, when Christians used to take grapes to church to be blessed before eating them, he cut a ripe bunch to show his father God’s blessing on the vine; his strict father rebuked him and struck him, and John took it without a word, prayed with tears, and put the bunch back where he had cut it, and it held to the vine as though it had never been cut.

At eighteen his parents, forgetting their vow to give him to God, pressed him to be betrothed to a rich girl. Torn, he prayed, and heard within him the Lord’s words that whoever loves father or mother, son or daughter more than him is not worthy of him. He went to the girl, told her that he longed for a virgin life and proposed the same to her, and when she refused, the betrothal was broken. Her parents took it as an insult, and with the help of a sorcerer they invited John and his father to a meal and put in his food a poison known in the mines of the district, which blinds a man who eats a little and kills one who eats more.

John, being temperate, ate little and was blinded. Forgiving them all, he left with his faithful servant, also called John, for Marathasa, and made his cell at the monastery of Saint Heraclidius, opposite Kalopanagiotis, where by one tradition the apostles Barnabas and Paul had baptised Heraclidius. For four years he lived there praying and teaching by word and example, and he worked many wonders: once, when his servant was fainting with thirst on a hot walk with no water near, he prayed and struck the rock, and a spring came out of it, known to this day as his holy water.

Three days before he died his sight came back, and he saw three golden-winged eagles circling him, the visit of the Triune God calling him. He died on 4 October at the age of twenty-two, and his parents and the monks buried him in the church of Saint Heraclidius and, at the end of the tenth century or the beginning of the eleventh, built another church in his name over the tomb. The Cypriot chronicler Leontios Machairas writes of him driving out demons in Marathasa, and the entry tells a healing of 1950, when a man of Famagusta given up by his doctors at Kalopanagiotis prayed to him, saw him beside the bed, and was healed of his wound. The wonders restored the monastery’s name, and it is known today as the monastery of John Lampadistes.

The Romanian life tells it more briefly and in places differently: his parents compelled him to marry, and he was poisoned for reasons unknown by his parents-in-law — some sources, it says, record a marriage and others only a betrothal; he fasted without ceasing, eating only every three or four days, and was known for his almsgiving to the poor; his father buried him; and a monastery grew up in his memory at Kalopanagiotis in the Troodos, where by local legend an epileptic was healed at his tomb. Both calendars keep him on 4 October, and the Greek page prints his apolytikion.
