# Antonina of Crodamna

At a time when there was a persecution against the Christians, and many of them were being killed with torments of many kinds for the faith in Christ, a Christian virgin named Antonina, of the city called Crodamna, was seized and brought before Festus the merciless governor. She had passed her life in the right faith and in good works, and the Lord counted her worthy of the struggle of martyrdom. The governor began to coax her with cunning words: "Lady Antonina, since thou art an honourable and wholly wise virgin, I wish to make thee priestess of the goddess Artemis, to give thee gifts and honour, and to set thee highest in all my house."

She was long silent, and then answered: "O Festus, why dost thou promise me empty gifts? Better that thou too shouldst wish to share my riches, so that by believing in my Lord Jesus Christ thou mightest gain the treasury of everlasting good things." He answered that the gods forbid he should believe in one who had been nailed and hanged on a cross; and she said that she did not deny that the Lord was nailed on the cross and laid in a tomb, but confessed this of him too, that on the third day he rose from the dead and now sits at the right hand of God the Father. Asked why she would not sacrifice, she said she would not worship gods without feeling, for they were demons, and in them was no power and no help at all.

Angered, Festus had the soldiers strike her on the face, and then swore that if she did not obey the emperor's command he would beat her with dreadful beatings, order her taken to a house of harlotry and condemn her to be burned with fire; and he gave her three days to think. "Do what thou wilt, unclean tormentor," she said. He gave her to four soldiers, and she went to prison with a glad soul and a cheerful face; and the governor secretly charged the chief keeper of the prison to do her no harm but rather to coax her away from Christianity. She answered him nothing, so that the keeper wondered; and kneeling on the ground she prayed to the Lord day and night and took no food.

When the three days had passed a thunder was suddenly heard so strong that the bolts of the prison were broken and the doors opened of themselves, and a great light lit all the prison, and a voice came from heaven: "Antonina, rise and strengthen thyself with food, taste bread and water and be brave. Fear not Festus the pagan governor, since I am with thee." She said Amen, rose, tasted a little bread and water, and lay down a little to rest.

In the morning the governor sat in judgement and had her brought. As she went she raised her eyes to heaven and gave thanks, praying not to be forsaken to the end, and a voice was heard again from heaven: "Go on thy way without fear, for I am with thee." When she came where Festus sat she laughed; and asked why, she said: "I have seen beforehand that the seat thou sittest on will soon be overturned and thou wilt perish; that is why I laughed at thee." He had her beaten with ox sinews, and she thanked God for counting her, wretched and sinful, worthy to have part with his saints. Then, more angry still, he said: "This evil and hateful woman not only blasphemes our gods but mocks us ourselves with insults; take her to a house of harlotry, that she may have her fill of her desires."

The soldiers took her and brought her to a certain house. While the holy virgin sat there praying earnestly to God, the angel of the Lord appeared to a soldier named [Alexander](/saints/alexander-companion-of-antonina), who was twenty-three years old, and said: "Go to the governor and speak with him to let thee go in to Antonina; and when thou hast clothed her in thy garment let her go out from there unknown to that wicked governor." The good soldier of Christ went to the governor and said: "Governor, command me to go in to Antonina; and if I persuade her it will be well, and if not, then do with her as thou wilt." The governor gave him leave and said: "If it please thee, do with her what thou wilt."

Alexander went into the house where the holy virgin was and fell at her feet, saying: "Servant of God, virgin Antonina, the Lord has sent me to thee to tell thee what he has said to me, how thy virginity is to be kept." She was afraid; but suddenly a great light shone in the house and a voice was heard: "Fear not, Antonina, for he who called thee to this struggle, being compassionate and of great mercy, calls this Alexander also to the same martyr's crown. Put on his garment and, keeping thy head down, go out from here; so the governor will not know thee, for I will keep thee from being known by him." So she took Alexander's garment and put it on, covered her head, went out, made a sign with her hand towards the governor and said, "Do what thou wilt." So she escaped like a deer out of the snare and went on her way rejoicing.

The governor, thinking it was Alexander who had left the house, sent four soldiers in to her with orders to abuse her and then bring her out to be insulted. What they found was Alexander in her clothes; and the two were brought together before Festus, and answered him with one voice that no such torments could force them to worship his unclean gods, which have neither understanding nor power. He ordered the hands of the holy martyrs cut off; and they stretched out their hands to the sword, saying that by no torments would he bend their godly mind. The soldiers drew their swords and cut off their hands, and they, as though they felt no pain, glorified God.

Then the governor had a deep pit dug; he hung them up and had them beaten until they should feel the pain of the wounds laid on their bodies, and had the pit filled with wood and brushwood to burn them in it. Beaten cruelly, they felt nothing, as though they were in bodies not their own, and gave glory to the Lord. Seeing this he added torments to torments and had their sides burned with lighted torches; and Alexander and Antonina cried out with a great voice that they suffered these things for the love of Christ, believing that in the age to come, after this temporal life, they would take an everlasting reward in the heavenly kingdom, and that the Lord would soon destroy him, since he tormented the guiltless servants of God so cruelly.

Festus in his fury had the wood in the pit lit, had the martyrs smeared with pitch and thrown into the pit of fire, then dry pitch laid on the wood so that their bones should be burnt utterly to ashes, and then the pit stopped up with earth, so that the women should not gather their bones as the Christians were used to do. After that he went to his house and tasted neither food nor drink, but was struck dumb; for an unclean spirit, which the holy angels had sent into him, tormented him until the seventh day, and on the seventh day, tortured with a heavy devilish torment, he gave up his unclean soul in bitterness. When he had so perished the persecution against the Christians ceased there. The two martyrs finished their suffering on a Saturday, at the ninth hour of the day. The page gives no year.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 10 June, and saint.gr puts a year to the martyrdom, «(; - 313)». It calls her a shining star of the small town of Kardamos — or Krodamos, both forms are on the page — where she was born, and says that, endowed with beauty outward and inward, she lived in self-restraint and modesty and spent all her strength and all her savings on serving, nursing and relieving the poor and the wretched about her. Her Christian conduct was denounced to the eparch Phystus, who arrested and questioned her; with an unbroken spirit and a divine pride she confessed her faith in Christ and called on the eparch himself to repent and be baptised a Christian. Disheartened that every effort of his fell on empty ground, and knowing how much she valued her virgin honour, he ordered her thrown into a brothel; but the warm prayer of the virgin brought a terrible earthquake on that house, so that the women there drove her out of it themselves. Phystus seized her again and shut her in another refuge, and from there a Christian named Alexander got her away by lending her his own clothes. Both were taken, mutilated, smeared with pitch and burnt, and their relics were afterwards carried to Constantinople and laid in the monastery of Maximinus, where a synaxis of them was held.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two of them together on the Julian 10 June, the menologion day both the other calendars keep, and its heading names them in one line. Both were from Alexandria, it says — where the Romanian life calls her a Christian virgin of the city called Crodamna, which is the name this corpus keeps her under; the two readings are recorded and neither is preferred. It tells the escape as the earlier books do: at the command of an angel of God the soldier went into the prison, though until then unknown to her, wrapped her in his own military cloak, told her to bow her head and walk out past the guards at the door; so the virgin got away and the soldier stayed behind.

Then he was brought before the judge and questioned for the name of Christ, and she, hearing of it, gave herself up to the judge; and he put them both to torments of many kinds, cut off their hands, beat them on the bare body, burnt their wounds with candles, and at the last threw them into a fire laid in a pit and heaped earth over them. **And this book dates it**: they suffered for the love of Christ and passed into the courts of the King of heaven on the third of May, 313 — the year the Greek calendar had bracketed and this folder had never recorded, and a day neither of the other books gives. The day is the date of the passing as this book states it, not a day it keeps them on.

The malicious judge Festus, at the hour of the martyrs’ death, was struck dumb, and an evil spirit seized him and tormented him seven days and at the last killed him. That is on these pages already in more words, down to the seventh day, and a third book agreeing to it is worth recording and changes nothing else.
