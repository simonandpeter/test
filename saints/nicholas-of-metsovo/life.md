# Nicholas of Metsovo

doxologia.ro's calendar names him and gives no life. The line for 17 mai reads «Sfântul Nou Mucenic Nicolae din Metsovo» — the holy new martyr Nicholas of Metsovo — and his own page on the site carries that line, the feast date, and tabs for icons, places of pilgrimage, monasteries and articles, but no Viață and no Tropar. Nothing on it says when he lived, under what rule he died, or how he came to be called after Metsovo in Epirus.

What the calendar does say is held here and nothing more: that the Romanian church keeps a new martyr of that name and that place on 17 May. The rank «Nou Mucenic» places him among the martyrs of the centuries under Ottoman rule, which is what the word means in this calendar, but the page names no year and none is claimed. No other calendar has been checked for him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same civil day and gives him a surname, two bynames and a year: Nicholas Basdanis, called also Vlachonikolas or Exintatrichis, born at Metsovo in Epirus of poor but very faithful parents and dead, by saint.gr's head line, in 1617. He lived in a hard and troubled time, it says, for after the failure of the risings of Dionysios the Philosopher, metropolitan of Trikke, the persecutions and pressures of the Muslims against the Christians had reached their peak. Young, he went to Trikala in Thessaly and worked in a Turkish bakery, and after a while the Turks, using methods of terror, forced him to become a Muslim; and when he understood the greatness of his Christian and national fall he returned to Metsovo and lived as a Christian there.

Poverty and the hard conditions of life at Metsovo drove him back to Trikala to sell kindling, and there a Turkish barber who had been a neighbour of the baker he once worked for recognised him, seized him, dragged him through the street and abused him publicly for betraying Islam and becoming a Christian again. Fearing the consequences, Nicholas gave the man his load of kindling and undertook to bring him a load every year, and on that agreement was let go. Back at Metsovo he made a severe reckoning with himself and saw that these continual falls and guilty compromises are no mark of a true disciple of Jesus; and so he took the great decision never again to compromise in a matter of faith, and, if it should be needed, to offer even his life on the altar of the love of Christ. The page adds that some synaxaria, Nikodemos the Hagiorite's among them, keep his memory on 16 May, and that he was formally entered in the Orthodox calendar in 1988 by the Ecumenical Patriarchate and is honoured on the seventeenth; it names two services written in his honour, at Venice in 1757 and 1771 and in the Epirot Leimonarion of 1968, and prints neither.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 16 May — the day Nikodemos the Hagiorite's synaxaria keep him on, as the Greek page itself reports, and not the seventeenth on which the Ecumenical Patriarchate entered him in 1988 — and its account is the shortest and the plainest of the three. He was of Epirus by birth. For the faith of Christ he was tortured by the Turks and beheaded at Trikala, in 1617. His head, the Prologue says, is kept to this day in one of the monasteries of Meteora in Thessaly. He works many wonders and heals the gravest sicknesses, and he is known above all for driving the locusts away from the fields.
