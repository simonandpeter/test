# Sergius (Srebryansky)

The Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery keeps him on the twenty-third of March, old style, as «Прп. Сергия исповедника (1948)», and he is the one of that day’s nine new martyrs and confessors whose life it tells. In the world he was Mitrofan Vasilyevich Srebryansky, born on 1 August 1870 in the village of Trekhsvyatskoye in the Voronezh district, the son of a priest. He finished the seminary and did not go straight into orders.

Under the influence of the populist ideas of the time he entered the veterinary institute at Warsaw instead, and there, among students indifferent to the faith and in Catholic Poland, he began to attend the Orthodox church diligently. In Warsaw he met Olga Vladimirovna Ispolatovskaya, also a priest’s daughter, who had finished the gymnasium at Tver and meant to be a teacher; they were married on 29 January 1893. In Warsaw he thought again about the road he had chosen, and judged that to serve people only outwardly, as a specialist helping peasants with their husbandry, was not enough.

He was ordained deacon on 2 March 1893 and priest on 20 March 1894, and was appointed to a dragoon regiment. From 1897 he was rector of the regimental church of the 51st Chernigov dragoons at Orel, whose patron was the Grand Duchess [Elizabeth Feodorovna](/saints/elizabeth-of-russia); he was presented to her at the glorification of the elder of Sarov in 1903 and made the best of impressions on her by his sincerity, humility and plainness. When the Russo-Japanese war began his regiment went east and he went with it, without a thought of avoiding it, setting up a field church wherever he could.

He kept a detailed diary through that war which was printed in the journal of the military clergy and then as a book of its own, and in it he set down what he had seen: how the Russian soldier loved his country and gave his life for it without fuss, how the newspapers of the capital described the front as though the enemy were writing them, and how deeply the people had divided over the faith, the believing and the unbelieving living like two different nations. He was made dean of the 61st infantry division in March 1905 and came home with the regiment in June 1906, and was raised archpriest that October.

In 1908 the Grand Duchess was working on the founding of her convent of Martha and Mary, and of the several plans submitted to her his was the one she liked well enough to build on; she asked him to be its confessor and the rector of its church. He did not want to leave Orel — you finish giving the cross after the liturgy, he said, and the people keep coming, one wants a word, another advice, a third is in a hurry to tell you his grief, and the hours go by — but he went. He was later tonsured with the name Sergius.

Arrests followed, and exile, and a return under watch; in 1930 an OGPU troika sentenced him to five years in the Northern region, and through the exile and the transports he was badly ill with myocarditis, and was let go at last on account of his age, his illnesses and the work he did well. He lived out his last years at Vladychnya in the Tver country, in the one-storey log house with a shingle roof where his wife’s father had lived.

He died on 23 March 1948, and the life quotes the inscription on the great white cross they set over him with its unquenched lamp: here lies the body of the priest-archimandrite Sergius — the archpriest Mitrofan. He said to his spiritual children while he still lived, do not weep for me when I die; you will come to my grave and say what you need, and if I have boldness with the Lord I will help you. The burial is remembered for a lark that came down low over the open grave and sang while they sang, and for the people who pressed forward with handkerchiefs and little icons to touch to the body.
