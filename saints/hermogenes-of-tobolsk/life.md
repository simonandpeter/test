# Hermogenes

Hermogenes — George Efremovich Dolganov in the world — bishop of Tobolsk
and Siberia, was born on 25 April 1858 in the family of an Edinoverie
priest of the Kherson diocese who later became a monk. He finished the
full course of the law faculty at Novorossiysk, and there too took the
courses of the mathematical and historical-philological faculties; then he
entered the St Petersburg Academy, where he was tonsured with the name
Hermogenes, and on 15 March 1892 became a hieromonk. In 1893 he finished
the Academy and was made inspector and then rector of the Tiflis seminary,
with the rank of archimandrite; unwilling to serve the anti-church and
materialist spirit of the age, he encouraged missionary work among the
people of the Russian borderlands. On 14 January 1901, in the Kazan
cathedral of St Petersburg, he was consecrated bishop of Volsk, vicar of
the Saratov diocese, and in 1903 was made bishop of Saratov and summoned
to sit in the Holy Synod. His ministry burned with an unflagging spirit:
mission flourished by his labours, religious readings and talks outside
the services were set up, their programme drawn up and led by the bishop
himself; he went often round the parishes and served with such reverence,
awe and prayer that people forgot whether they were in heaven or on
earth, and many wept for tenderness and spiritual joy, and in the
disorders of 1905 his sermons brought the maddened rioters to their
senses. John of Kronstadt loved and honoured him greatly, saying he was at
peace about the fate of Orthodoxy and could die knowing that Bishops
Hermogenes and Seraphim (Chichagov) would carry on his work, and foretold
his martyrdom in a letter of 1906: you are in the contest; the Lord opens
heaven to you, as to the archdeacon Stephen, and blesses you. At the end of
1911, at a session of the Synod, he broke sharply with the chief procurator
Sabler, who with the silent consent of many bishops was hurrying through
institutions and rulings of a plainly uncanonical kind — a corporation of
deaconesses, the funeral service for the heterodox — and on 7 January a
decree signed by the emperor dismissed him from the Synod and ordered him
to his diocese by the 15th; ill, he missed the term, and was exiled to the
Zhirovitsy monastery in Belorussia, his sharply hostile attitude to
Rasputin being another cause. The disgraced bishop’s lot in the monastery
was hard: he was seldom allowed to serve, and when he was, the honours of
his rank were withheld; at times he was forbidden even to leave the
monastery. He grieved often for the future of the fatherland and said with
tears: it is coming, the ninth wave; it will crush and sweep away all the
rot and the rags; a terrible thing will happen that freezes the blood —
they will kill the tsar, they will kill the tsar, they will surely kill
him. In August 1915 he was moved to the Nikolo-Ugreshsky monastery of the
Moscow diocese, and after the February coup of 1917 was appointed to the
see of Tobolsk.

His special care was for the soldiers back from the front, drugged with
Bolshevik propaganda, for whom he set up a soldiers’ section of the
John-Demetrius brotherhood, to the fury of the Bolsheviks, who wanted the
soldiers embittered so as to rule them the more easily; in that mutinous
time he called his flock not to bend the knee before the idols of the
revolution, fighting communism, denationalisation and the distortion of
the Russian soul. On the see of Tobolsk while the royal passion-bearers
were held there in captivity, he had the Abalak icon of the Mother of God
brought them for their comfort. On 25 December 1917 in the Protection
church of Tobolsk, the imperial family being present, the deacon Evdokimov
proclaimed the many years for them as the Typikon prescribes; the rector
and the deacon were arrested — the archpriest Vasiliev told the
interrogation he was not answerable to crayfish and dog deputies, and the
deacon said, your kingdom is a minute’s; the tsar’s defence will soon
come; wait a little and you will get your due in full — and to the local
Bolshevik organ’s inquiry the bishop answered in writing, refusing any
personal dealings: Russia is not in law a republic; no one has declared
her one or has the right to, save the proposed Constituent Assembly; and
by Holy Scripture, the law of the state, the church canons and history,
former kings, tsars and emperors out of the government of their country
are not deprived of their rank as such or of the titles that go with it,
so that in the acts of the clergy of the Protection church I saw and see
nothing reprehensible. At the Liturgy he always took out particles for the
imperial family, holding his love for them sacred, and it is recorded that
during the emperor’s Tobolsk exile he asked his forgiveness for having
believed the slanders about Rasputin, and the emperor forgave him with a
humble heart. In January 1918, after the Bolshevik decree separating the
Church from the state, which in fact put believers outside the law, he
issued an appeal to the people ending: stand in defence of your faith and
say with firm hope, let God arise and let His enemies be scattered.

The authorities prepared his arrest, and he, untroubled, fixed a
procession of the cross for Palm Sunday, 15 April 1918: I expect no mercy
from them, he said; they will kill me, and more, they will torture me; I am
ready, ready this moment; I fear not for myself nor grieve for myself, but
for the townspeople — what will they do to them? On the eve, 13 April,
armed Red Guards came to the bishop’s rooms, and not finding him searched
them and defiled the altar of the house church. The procession gathered a
multitude; from the walls of the Kremlin the house where the imperial
family was held could be seen, and the bishop, going to the edge of the
wall, raised the cross high and blessed the august passion-bearers, who
watched the procession from their windows. Escorted by militia on foot
and horse, the procession drew many, but on the way back — it ended at
half past four — the ranks thinned, and the militia, first by a trick,
easily drove off the rest with rifle-butts and arrested the bishop; the
alarm was rung from the bell-tower by the bishop’s house, and the
Bolsheviks drove the ringers down with shots. He was put in the
Ekaterinburg prison, where he prayed much, and wrote to his reverently
loved and unforgettable flock in a letter smuggled out: do not grieve over
my imprisonment; it is my spiritual school; glory to God, who gives such
wise and salutary trials to me, who so badly need strict and extreme
measures on my inner spiritual world … from these shocks between life and
death the saving fear of God grows and is confirmed in the soul. After
some months the regional council of people’s commissars demanded a
ransom, first of a hundred thousand roubles, then, seeing it could not be
raised, of ten thousand; when the money, given by the local merchant D. I.
Polirushev, was brought by the clergy the authorities gave a receipt for
it, and instead of releasing the bishop arrested the three of the
delegation — the archpriest Ephraim Dolganov, the priest Michael Makarov
and Constantine Minyatov — of whose fate nothing more is known; their
martyrdom, it seems, went before his. Soon he was taken to Tyumen and by
steamer to the village of Pokrovskoye; all the prisoners were shot except
the bishop and the priest Peter Karelin of the church of the Kamensky
works, dean of the second district of the Kamyshev uyezd of Ekaterinburg
province, and the two were shut in a filthy hold as the boat made for
Tobolsk. On the evening of 15 June, as they were moved from one boat to
another, the bishop, coming to the gangway, said quietly to the pilot:
tell the whole great world, baptised servant of God, to pray to God for
me. About midnight of 15–16 June the Bolsheviks first brought Fr Peter
Karelin on to the deck of the steamer Oka, tied two great granite stones
to him and threw him into the Tura; the bishop met the same end — by some
accounts he was bound to the paddle-wheel, which was then set in motion
and tore his living body. His remains were washed ashore on 3 July and
found by peasants of Usolskoye, and buried the next day by the peasant
Alexis Maryanov where they were found, the stone laid in the grave with
them; soon the town was freed by the troops of the Siberian government,
and the remains were taken up, vested as a bishop’s and solemnly buried in
a vault made in the John Chrysostom chapel, on the site of the first grave
of St John, metropolitan of Tobolsk. The hieromartyrs Hermogenes, Ephraim,
Peter and Michael and the martyr Constantine were numbered among the new
martyrs and confessors of Russia at the jubilee Council of Bishops in
August 2000; the Russian calendar keeps on 20 August (Julian) the finding
of his relics in 2005.
