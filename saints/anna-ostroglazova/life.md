# Anna (Ostroglazova)

Anna Ivanovna Ostroglazova was born on 19 November 1900 at Kaluga, the daughter of an archpriest. She finished a secondary school and entered the institute of popular education, left it after two years in 1920, and took work as a bookkeeper in the Kaluga teachers' college. Teachers and students alike grew fond of her for her gentle temper, and many of the students came to her with their troubles. She was a deeply believing, devout and serious woman, and when [Augustine (Belyaev)](/saints/augustine-belyaev) took the Kaluga see she became his closest helper.

Gathering material for the order of her arrest, the NKVD officer wrote of her: «дочь попа, девица, активная церковница» — a priest's daughter, a spinster, an active churchwoman. After she was arrested on 16 October 1937 the college was required to give a character of its employee, and what it wrote is the longest document on the page: that she had worked there about fifteen years and coped with the bookkeeping; that she was extremely silent, so that it was exceedingly hard to make out her face, never speaking at meetings or in the political circle and answering teachers and administration in short replies or remarks.

It went on that she was a minister of religion's daughter, lived with her father, was always closely surrounded by churchpeople, went often to church and prayed hard, bowed before the bishop, and that the very look of their house was like a church; that she showed an interest in every side of the college's affairs, so that students sometimes handed her applications on matters that were not financial before the administration saw them; and that she was known to have urged a cleaning woman to write herself down a believer in the all-union census.

At the interrogations she held herself with dignity and calm. Told to give frank evidence about her anti-Soviet activity, she said she had carried on none and did not admit herself guilty. Told that the investigation had exact information that she had agitated against the order, she said she had nothing to add to her answer. Asked to confirm that she belonged to a counter-revolutionary monarchist organisation, she did not confirm it. Read the evidence of a man who named her a member and the archbishop's trusted person who had brought up the intelligentsia, and the children of the clergy and of the former people, in a counter-revolutionary religious spirit, she did not confirm that either; nor the charge of sabotage in her bookkeeping, nor that of spreading counter-revolutionary rumours.

On 19 November 1937, her thirty-seventh birthday, an NKVD troika sentenced her to ten years in a corrective labour camp, and there she died a martyr's death of hunger. No year and no camp is named, and the day's line closes the entry with the decade alone. She was glorified among the new martyrs and confessors of Russia in August 2000 by the jubilee council of bishops.
