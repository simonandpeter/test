# Agape of Aquileia

Agape, [Chionia](/saints/chionia-of-aquileia) and [Irene](/saints/irene-of-aquileia) were three sisters, in the body and in the spirit, who lived near Aquileia in Italy at the time when the emperor Diocletian was staying in that city. Word had reached him from Rome that the prisons there were full of Christians who would not deny their Christ and who all held to the teaching of Chrysogonus; he ordered the Christians put to death and Chrysogonus brought to him, and had him beheaded at a desert place outside the city, his body left on the shore for the beasts and the birds.

Near that place lived a priest named Zoilus, a holy man, and next to him the three sisters. Zoilus learned by a revelation from God where the martyr's body lay, took it up with the head, laid it in a wooden coffin and hid it in his room. Thirty days later Chrysogonus appeared to him in a dream and told him that within nine days the three virgins of Christ would be put to torture, that he was to tell Anastasia, the servant of God, to care for them and rouse them to the brave struggle until they were crowned, and that he himself would soon be released from this life. The same was revealed to Anastasia.

Not many days afterwards the emperor had to go into Macedonia, and all the Christians held in the prisons of Aquileia were taken after him, the three sisters among them, with Anastasia following at a distance. In Macedonia he gave the examination of the Christians to Dulcius the governor, who was to force them to sacrifice and destroy with various torments those who would not be persuaded.

The sentence upon the two elder sisters was given by Sisinius the count: «Agapia și Hionia, care nu s-au supus prin îngrozirea de judecată să împlinească împărăteasca poruncă, poruncesc să se ardă» — Agape and Chionia, who have not submitted, I order to be burned. Hearing it the virgins were filled with joy and cried out with a loud voice, thanking the Lord Jesus Christ for counting them worthy to confess His most holy name and asking Him to receive their souls. They were thrown into the fire and gave up their souls in prayer; and that great fire, burning as it was, touched neither their bodies nor so much as their clothes.

[Irene](/saints/irene-of-aquileia), the youngest, was brought out the next day and died apart from them, shot with arrows on a hill; Anastasia's servants took up her body by night and laid it beside her sisters'.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and differs from the Romanian notice in its details. saint.gr says the three were sisters and spiritual shoots of the Church of Thessalonica, and that the souls of all three virgins were adorned with many Christian graces. When the persecution of the Christians under Maximian came, the sisters took refuge on some high mountain; but their hiding-place was discovered, and in the year 304 they were arrested and brought before the ruler Dulcitius. He tried every way to make them deny Christ, and they, with the virtues they had for weapons, went on confessing Christ crucified. Then Agape and Chionia died, having been thrown into the fire, while Irene, shut up in a brothel where nobody dared to touch her, met her martyr's end from an arrow shot at her by a soldier. The relics that were left from the pyre, it adds, were gathered up by reverent Christians and buried west of the city a little way from the walls, and a small church was raised there which was afterwards made larger. Its couplets are two, one for Agape and Chionia — «Χιὼν τὸ πῦρ ἦν τῇ Χιονίᾳ τάχα, Οὗ συμμετασχεῖν ἠγάπησεν Ἀγάπη», the fire was perhaps snow to Chionia, and Agape loved to share in it — and one for Irene, «Βέλος σε πέμπει πρὸς τὸν εἰρήνης τόπον, Ἀφ' αἱμάτων σῶν ἐκμεθυσθὲν Εἰρήνη», an arrow drunk with your own blood sends you to the place of peace, Irene. A third line names the day, «Χιονίην τ' Ἀγάπην ἐκκαιδεκάτῃ κατέκαυσαν».

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the three sisters on the same day as the other two calendars and tells the story with several things neither of them has. They were sisters by birth, from the neighbourhood of Aquileia. When the emperor Diocletian was staying in that city he gave orders for the death of the noted spiritual father Chrysogonus; and an old presbyter named Zoilus was shown in a vision where the martyr's body was lying unburied, hurried to it, laid it in a chest and kept it in his own house. On the thirtieth day after that Chrysogonus appeared to him and told him that within nine days these three maidens would suffer, and that he himself would depart in the same time; the same was revealed to Anastasia, who had gone after her teacher. And indeed after nine days the old man departed, and the sisters were brought to trial before the emperor.

The emperor urged them to bow to the idols and all three refused and confessed their firm faith in Christ. It was Irene, the entry says, who told him how foolish it is to bow to things of stone and wood, ordered for an agreed price and made by the hands of some mortal man. In his rage he threw them into prison; and when he set out for Macedonia the slaves and prisoners were taken along after him, these three among them, and he handed them to a commander named Dulcitius for examination. Inflamed with a dark passion, Dulcitius meant to defile the virgins; but as he went to enter their prison, at the hour when they were praying to God, his mind was taken from him, and he fell upon the black cauldrons and pots standing before the door and embraced and kissed them, and came away sooty and black all over. When the emperor heard of it he set another commander, Sisinius, over their trial.

After heavy torture the judge sentenced the two elder sisters to be burned. The Prologue says nothing of the flames leaving their bodies and their clothes untouched, which is the Romanian notice's wonder, and nothing of the relics gathered from the pyre, which is the Greek page's; what it adds instead is that Saint Anastasia gathered all three bodies into one place and buried them honourably. They all suffered honourably for Christ the King and Lord, it ends, about the year 304 — the year this folder now holds, where it held none, and the same year the Greek page gives for the arrest.

**The Russian calendar tells the sisters' story at length where the other three sources give a notice, and it names four men none of them names.** They were sisters by blood, it says, living near the Italian city of Aquileia at the end of the third century and the beginning of the fourth, orphaned while they were still young. They led a devout Christian life and turned away the many who came asking to marry them, and their spiritual guide was a priest named Zeno. It was shown to him in a dream that he would shortly die and that the holy virgins would be taken for torture; and the same was revealed to the great-martyr [Anastasia](/saints/anastasia-pupil-of-chrysogonus), who was at Aquileia then and was called the loosener of bonds because she went fearlessly to the Christians in the prisons, encouraged them and helped them. She hurried to the sisters and urged them to stand bravely for Christ. Soon the priest died, and the three virgins were seized.

They were brought before the emperor Diocletian, whose reign the page dates 284 to 305. Seeing how young and beautiful they were, he offered to find them noble bridegrooms from his own retinue if they would deny Christ; and they answered that they had one heavenly Bridegroom, Christ, for faith in whom they were ready to suffer, and called the gods of the pagans idols made by human hands. As he was setting out for Macedonia he had the sisters carried there too, and handed them over to the governor Dulcitius. The sight of them filled him with an unclean passion, and he sent word that they should have their freedom if they would do what he wanted; they answered that they were ready to die for their heavenly Bridegroom.

So he resolved to take them by force in the night. While they were at prayer and glorifying the Lord he crept to the door and meant to go in; an invisible power struck him, he lost his reason and ran off, and in his blundering he fell into a kitchen among the cauldrons and pans and griddles and came out black with soot, so that his servants and soldiers scarcely knew him. When he saw himself in a mirror he thought the women had bewitched him and determined on revenge. In court he ordered them stripped before him, and the soldiers try as they might could not do it, for the clothes clung to the holy virgins' bodies as though grown to them; and in the middle of the trial he fell suddenly asleep, and nobody could wake him until he had been carried indoors, when he woke at once.

When all this was reported to the emperor he was angry with Dulcitius and gave the virgins to a judge named Sisinnius. He began the questioning with the youngest, and finding her immovable he sent her to prison and turned on the two elder sisters; and when they could not be shifted either he ordered them burned. They thanked the Lord for the martyr's crown when they heard the sentence, and went to him in the fire at prayer; and when the fire went out their bodies and their clothing were unscorched, and their faces as calm and beautiful as those of people fallen quietly asleep. The next day the youngest was brought out, threatened with her sisters' end and then with a brothel; she answered that her body might be given to violence but her soul would not be defiled by denying Christ. Two shining soldiers overtook the guards on the road and told them their master's order was to leave the girl on a high mountain and report it done; they were angels of God, and Sisinnius, who had given no such order, searched a long time and could find no road to the summit. Then one of his soldiers wounded her with an arrow from his bow, and she cried out that she laughed at his powerless malice and was going pure and undefiled to her Lord Jesus Christ; and she lay down on the ground and gave up her spirit one day before holy Pascha. The great-martyr learned of it and buried the three with honour. The shorter reading the same index serves puts their arrest at Diocletian's own visit to Aquileia, which filled every prison in the place, and adds out of the service sung to them that they feared neither the onset of wild beasts nor the cutting off of parts of the body.
