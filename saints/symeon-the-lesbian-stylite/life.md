# Symeon the Lesbian

At the beginning of the eighth century there lived at Mytilene on Lesbos
Adrian and Constanto, who had seven children, five of whom became monks;
three of them were David, Symeon and George. David, the eldest, born in 717
or 718, learned a little of letters and at sixteen, herding his father’s
sheep in a great storm, saw St Anthony in a vision calling him to the
monastic life and bidding him cross to Asia Minor and Mount Ida, opposite
Lesbos and a little to the north, and be a monk there; he went gladly and
lived thirty years in a cave on Ida in great asceticism, eating wild herbs,
was sent by another vision to the bishop of Gargara to be ordained deacon
and then priest, and back on Ida, at an angel’s direction, built a church of
Cyricus and Julitta and a monastery that quickly filled. After ten years,
his father being dead, his mother came to see him bringing the youngest of
her children, Symeon, then eight years old, born in 765 or 766; Symeon
stayed with his brother, and their mother went home to Mytilene and died
soon after. Symeon learned his letters in his brother’s monastery, became a
monk at twenty-two and was ordained priest by the bishop of Gargara at
twenty-eight; two years later David died at sixty-six, having foreseen his
death and told Symeon to go back to Mytilene, and Symeon obeyed and settled
at the church of the Panagia by the south harbour of the town, at the Mole,
where, to imitate the labour of the old Symeon the Stylite, he mounted a
pillar and lived on it in terrible asceticism, fasting, hardship and prayer.
He took to himself his brother George, a monk too, born in 763, who was also
ordained priest, and with their sister Hilaria, a nun, and other monks they
built a monastery to which crowds of Christians came thirsting for the word
of God and the blessing of the holy monks.

Then the fury of the iconoclasts broke the peace again: the emperor Leo V
the Armenian renewed the persecution, the bishop of Mytilene George was
exiled and an iconoclast Leo set in his place, who turned at once on Symeon
and his monks, had him condemned to death by fire — from which he was saved
by a wonder and left for a time undisturbed on his pillar — then forced him
to leave Mytilene and withdraw with the monks to the islet of St Isidore in
the gulf of Gera, and at last got from the emperor Michael II the Stammerer
an order exiling him to Lagousa, an uninhabited island opposite the Troad,
where he went with seven disciples and lived on a pillar ten metres high,
while George stayed at Mytilene minding the monastery. Later Symeon went to
Constantinople, seeing that he could serve the Church there, settled in the
monastery of Nicetas of Medikion, and from it travelled from the Hellespont
to the islands of the Aegean and to the Black Sea, strengthening the
Christians with his word and comforting the fathers the iconoclasts had
exiled, working as a fisherman wherever he stopped to earn what he needed,
less for himself than for those in want, healing the sick by God’s grace,
and founding a convent that filled with nuns. Under Theophilus, who renewed
the savage persecution after Michael’s death, he and his company were seized
to be imprisoned and made away with; the empress Theodora’s intervention
saved him, but not from the hundred and fifty strokes of the rod the emperor
ordered, nor from exile on Aphousia in the Propontis with other famous
preachers of Orthodoxy, Theophanes and Theodore the Branded among them,
where he built a church of the Panagia and a monastery and gathered into it
all the fathers the iconoclasts had driven out. George at Mytilene suffered
too: the iconoclast bishop harried him, seized and sold the monastery and
all its goods, and drove him with his monks to a poor little village called
Myrsina, where the Christians still found him and where he taught and worked
many wonders. When Theophilus died in 842 the empress Theodora recalled all
the exiled fathers, Symeon and George among them, and the two, with the
future patriarch Methodius the Confessor, became her most trusted
counsellors; when Methodius was made patriarch in 843 at Symeon’s proposal,
Symeon and his disciples settled in the monastery of Sergius and Bacchus.
George refused the see of Ephesus for his age — he was eighty — and after
much pressure accepted the metropolis of Mytilene, and came home by imperial
dromon with gifts from the empress, from Petronas and from Bardas for the
island’s poor and an escort of her generals and courtiers, to a welcome of
great joy; the saints took their monastery back and kept in it, after so
many years of persecution, the Nativity of the Theotokos, 8 September 843,
and George was enthroned in St Theodora’s, the metropolitan church, on 14
September, the Exaltation of the Cross. A year later, in 844, Symeon died
and was buried in the monastery of the Panagia; George healed a sick friend
in Gotthograecia that winter and foretold the man’s death in seven years,
and himself died on the evening of Great Saturday of 845 or 846, warned by
an angel at Smyrna, and was laid with great honour in his brother’s tomb.
The Greek synaxarion keeps the three brothers together on 1 February and
Symeon alone on 1 September, which is the line this entry stands under.
