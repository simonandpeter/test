# Onesimus

In Colossae, a city of Phrygia, there lived a man of note and of office named Philemon, who believed in Christ, was afterwards counted worthy of the rank of bishop, and was numbered among the seventy apostles. Before his apostleship Philemon kept a slave named Onesimus, who wronged his master and, fearing punishment, fled from him to Rome. There he found the Apostle Paul in chains, heard the holy preaching from him, learned the faith in Christ and was baptised by him, and from then on served him at Rome together with Tychicus.

When Paul sent Tychicus to the Colossians with his letter, he sent Onesimus with him, and by Onesimus's hand wrote a separate letter to Philemon his master, asking him to forgive the man his fault and to receive him not as a slave but as a beloved brother, and as Paul himself. In that letter the Apostle calls Onesimus his own son, begotten while he was in bonds, once useless to his master and now useful to them both. Philemon did it gladly: he received Onesimus with love, gave him his freedom, and sent him back to Rome to serve Paul there.

Onesimus stayed at Rome, serving the holy apostles until their end, and was set by them as bishop for the preaching of the word. After their end he left Rome and went through many cities and countries preaching Christ — in Spain, in Carpetania, in Colossae, at Patras — and then took the throne of Ephesus, after Timothy and after [John the Theologian](/saints/john-the-theologian). His episcopate at Ephesus is marked, the life says, by the letter Ignatius the God-bearer wrote to the Ephesians from Smyrna, when Onesimus met him on the road with some of the Ephesians as he was being taken from Antioch to Rome to be given to the beasts.

In old age he was seized by the unbelievers and brought to Rome in the reign of Trajan, before Tertyllus the eparch. Asked who he was, he answered that he was a Christian; that he had once been the slave of a man and was now the faithful slave of Christ, who had bought him not with silver or corruptible gold but with His own precious blood. Then he set out at length, to the eparch's own question, what the vain life is and what the service of idols is, and urged Tertyllus to leave it. The eparch shut him in the darkest prison, and there he spent eighteen days as in a bright paradise, and the faithful came to him.

After the eighteen days the eparch, making a show of mercy, did not condemn him to death but drove him from the city and sent him into exile at Puteoli, where he did not stop preaching. Hearing of it, Tertyllus took him again, and finding him unmoved in the faith had him stretched out and beaten with staves by four men without sparing, so that the bones of his legs and others were broken; then his head was struck off. A Christian woman of the imperial family took his body, laid it in a silver coffin, and kept the martyr's memory.
