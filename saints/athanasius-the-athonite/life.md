# Athanasius the Athonite

Athanasius was brought forth into this mortal life by the great city of Trebizond, learned his letters in Byzantium, and was given as a gift to God by the mountains of Kyminas and of Athos. His parents were of good family and right-believing; his father came from Antioch and his mother from Colchis, and they lived at Trebizond. His father died before he was born, and his mother, having borne him and had him enlightened by holy baptism, followed her husband to God. The child's name in baptism was Abramius.

Left an orphan from his swaddling clothes, he was taken in and fed by a nun of good family. From his infancy the signs of what his life would be showed in him: small as he was, he was wise in everything and good in his ways, so that when he played with children of his own age they did not make him emperor or general in their games, but abbot. And this was right, for from childhood he was schooling himself in the monastic life, seeing the nun who was raising him spend her time in prayer and fasting and striving to follow her as far as he could.

Set to learn grammar, he outstripped those of his own age; and growing in body and in understanding he passed out of childhood. Then the nun who had been a mother to him departed to the Lord, and Abramius, orphaned a second time, wept for her as for his own mother and wished to go to Byzantium to seek the wisdom of books; and God, who cares for orphans, arranged that his wish should be fulfilled.

Doxologia's account of him is a long one, gathered, it says, out of a larger book about his life, his struggles and his wonders, and it closes with the healings worked at his intercession after his death. The Romanian calendar keeps with him on the same day [his six disciples](/saints/six-disciples-of-athanasius-the-athonite), who were covered by the falling dome of the church they had climbed with him to inspect. No year is printed for his birth or his death, so none is recorded here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 5 July, and in the same breath as his six disciples. saint.gr says he came from Trebizond, of a very devout and well-off family that gave him everything he needed for his studies, which he finished at Constantinople; that it was there the desire to become a monk was born in him, and that he went first to Mount Kyminas in Asia Minor, to the monastery whose abbot was [Michael Maleinos](/saints/michael-maleinos). Having come to be honoured by everybody there he left for Athos and put himself under an old ascetic in great humility, and then, after a divine revelation, went into the inner parts of the Holy Mountain. There, at the repeated entreaty of the emperor Nikephoros Phokas, whom he knew, he built a church in honour of the Mother of God and many cells for the monks, and so founded the Great Lavra in 963, the oldest monastery on the Mountain, which bears his name.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 5 July, the same menologion day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep him on, and it gives the year neither of them had: so ended this great lamp of monasticism, it says, in the year 980. Born at Trebizond of God-fearing parents and early left an orphan, he was taken by God’s Providence by a certain voivode, brought to Constantinople and put to his studies; and for his meekness and humility he was the favourite of all his own age. At the children’s games, this book says, the children chose one for emperor and one for voivode, and Athanasius for abbot — as a kind of foretelling.

His schooling done, Athanasius — Abramius until his tonsure, as the Romanian life also has it — withdrew into the desert of Maleinon near the Holy Mountain, where he struggled as the disciple of [Michael Maleinos](/saints/michael-maleinos), then famous. Wanting a stricter ascesis still, he moved to the Holy Mountain for silence; but many who wanted the ascetic life began to gather about him, and so he was forced to build his glorious Lavra. The Byzantine emperors helped him abundantly in it, first the emperor Nikephoros Phokas, who himself meant to withdraw and become a monk, and afterwards the emperor John Tzimiskes.

Many temptations came at him from demons and from men, and like a brave soldier of Christ he overcame them all by his measureless meekness and his unbroken prayer to the living God. Full of the grace of God he was counted worthy to see the most holy Theotokos, who wonderfully brought water out of the rock and promised to be for ever the Stewardess and Abbess of the monastery. In work and in prayer he went before his brethren and loved them all with the love of a spiritual father and shepherd. His death came suddenly: he had gone up with six monks over a newly built narthex of the church to look at a wall then being raised, and the wall gave way and buried them all. After his death he appeared more than once to his brethren, whether to comfort or to rebuke.
