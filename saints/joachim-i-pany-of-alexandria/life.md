# Joachim I Pany

Joachim the Pany — the very great — was the first patriarch of Alexandria
after the Turkish conquest of Egypt in 1517, when Sultan Selim I defeated the
Mamelukes and took from them the lordship of Egypt and the caliphate. Born at
Athens in 1448, he was a monk at the monastery of Sinai and at the Lavra of
Sabbas the Sanctified in Palestine, and ascended the ancient apostolic throne
of St Mark in August 1487.

He spent himself on the reorganisation of the Patriarchate with a sharp and
practical mind; he sat at the council of Jerusalem of 1526, at which the
so-called Sinaitic question was argued and settled; he sought and secured
support for his hard-pressed throne from the tsar Basil; and he stoutly
resisted the approaches, above all of the Venetian archbishop of Crete Peter
Lando, to consent to the union of the churches and sit in 1562 at the Roman
Catholic council of Trent.

That stand deepened still further the church’s sense of his holiness and of
his hold on the faith of the fathers, and chroniclers of the day record
wonders worked by his faith. He died in extreme old age in 1567, in the
hundred and seventeenth year of his life, having been patriarch for eighty
years. His name was entered in the church’s calendar by a patriarchal and
synodal act of 17 September 2002, under Patriarch Peter VII, and a service
composed by Metropolitan Cyril of Rhodes was published in 2009 under
Patriarch Theodore II in his predecessor’s memory; his precious skull is
treasured at the monastery of St Sabbas. The Greek calendar keeps him on 17
September.

The Russian calendar keeps him on 17 September old style, dated 1567; the Sretensky calendar prints no life, and the Russian church calendar at azbyka.ru says that after the Church of Alexandria glorified him the Holy Synod of the Russian Church entered him in its diptych of saints on 7 May 2003. Its life says that when Selim I took Egypt Joachim obtained a guarantee of all the patriarchate’s privileges, that he kept up ties with the Russian court and received money from it for his throne, and that the monastery of St Sabbas at Alexandria was wholly restored in his time. It tells two wonders.

When plague raged in Egypt, a physician hostile to the Christians spread the slander that they had bewitched the water; the sultan summoned Joachim, argued with him about the faith, and ordered him to move a mountain near Cairo, and after days of fasting and prayer with the faithful the patriarch bade it move in the name of Christ, and it shook and left its place, and was called in Turkish “stand, mountain”. Then his enemies had the sultan make him drink poison; he signed the cup with the cross, drank it and was unharmed. The life gives his age at death as 119, where the Greek life says he died in his hundred and seventeenth year.
