# Joseph the Wonderworker (of Volok)

Joseph — John Sanin in the world — was born on 14 November 1440, or 1439, at
Yazvishche near Volokolamsk, to pious parents who both later took the
monastic habit. Sent at seven to the elder Arsenius at the Volokolamsk
monastery of the Exaltation, he learned the Psalter in a year and the whole
of Scripture in the next, and could afterwards say the monastic rule from
memory without a book in his cell. At twenty he went first to the strict
elder Barsanuphius near Tver, found the rule there too mild, and with his
blessing went on to Paphnutius of Borovsk, who tonsured him Joseph on
13 February 1460 and gave him the kitchen, the bakery and the infirmary,
where he nursed the sick, the life says, as though tending Christ himself.
He spent some seventeen years there, read and sang — his voice was compared
to a swallow’s — and on Paphnutius’s death was made igumen. When most of the
brethren refused the strict common life he wanted, he went to see how the
Kirillov and other coenobitic houses lived, came back, met the same refusal,
and left with seven monks for the forests of his own country, where Prince
Boris of Volokolamsk gave him land at the meeting of the Struga and the
Sestra. A storm felled the trees before their eyes as if clearing the
ground; the wooden Dormition church was consecrated on 15 August 1479, and
that is the monastery’s birthday.

He built much of it with his own hands, was first in church and last out,
and walked the cells at night, tapping softly on a door where he heard idle
talk. His rule was total non-possession, the surrender of one’s own will,
and constant work: everything held in common, nothing taken into a cell
without a blessing, part of every meal given to the poor, and up to seven
hundred fed from the monastery in a hard year. The copying of books made the
Volokolamsk library one of the finest in Russia; Dionysius and his sons
painted the stone Dormition church in 1485; and among his disciples were two
metropolitans of Moscow, Daniel and Macarius. Against the Judaisers who had
come to Novgorod in 1470 and reached the grand prince’s own court he wrote
The Enlightener, the first handbook of Russian Orthodox theology, and with
Gennadius of Novgorod saw the heresy condemned at the councils of 1502 to
1504. Foreseeing the day of his death, he received the Mysteries, blessed
the brethren and died in peace on 9 September 1515. His life was written in
the 1540s by his disciple Savva the Black, bishop of Krutitsa; the whole
church has kept him since 1591.

The Greek synaxarion keeps him on 9 September as Joseph the wonderworker “in
Velon” — its spelling of Volok — and says it has no details; the Russian
calendar keeps him the same day of its own reckoning.
