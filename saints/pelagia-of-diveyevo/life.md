# Pelagia of Diveyevo

Pelagia Ivanovna Serebrennikova was born in October 1809 at Arzamas in Russia, saint.gr says, the daughter of Ivan Ivanovich Surin, a merchant, and of Paraskeva Ivanovna Bebeseva; she had two brothers, Andrew and John. Her father died young, and her mother married again, to Alexei Nikitich Korolev, a hard and unfeeling man whose children by his first wife treated Pelagia and her brothers as harshly as he did.

While still small she fell gravely ill and kept to her bed a long while; and when at last she got up she was an altogether different person. Where she had been unusually clever and clear in mind, she now began to do foolish things — going out into the garden in winter, lifting her skirt, standing on one foot and spinning round like a dancer while uttering cries nobody could make out. They scolded her and beat her without any result. Her mother said years afterwards that she had suspected then that Pelagia had taken up the calling of folly for Christ, though at the time it seemed the worst thing that could have happened to an already burdened family.

As she grew she became a very handsome girl, tall and upright; suitors made nothing of her strange behaviour and asked for her hand, and her mother welcomed that for a poor orphan who was also a fool. But Pelagia heard a secret voice within her calling her to the desert road of those who truly love the longing for God. At nineteen she was married to Sergei Vasilievich Serebrennikov, in the church of Saint John the Theologian at Arzamas, on 23 May 1828.

Her young husband, hoping to help her, took her with her mother to [Seraphim](/saints/seraphim-of-sarov) at his hermitage at Sarov. The saint welcomed them, sent the others to the guest house, and kept Pelagia with him to talk. When Sergei and his mother-in-law came back to the cell to see why she was so long, they found Seraphim making a prostration to the ground before her and giving her a prayer rope, calling her Matushka; and Ivan Tambartsev, who shared the saint's cell and was there, said that after she had gone Seraphim came up to him and told him that this woman would become a star to give light to the whole of Russia.

Her strange behaviour enraged her husband. She kept all-night vigils, slept in coffins, begged, gave away to the poor the money he gave her, and went about the streets half naked; in the end he turned her out of the house. She went back to her mother's, where new griefs waited: her stepfather beat her, and his youngest daughter, who envied her particularly, set a good marksman to shoot her as she went about the town at her follies. He missed, and heard Pelagia tell him that he had shot himself instead of her — which came true not long after, when the man killed himself with a gunshot.

After all this her mother went again to Sarov, told the elder everything and asked his advice; and he told her not to bind Pelagia but to leave her free to follow the road that pleased God. From that day nobody hindered her. She spent almost every night in the courtyard of the church, praying in the open air all night long with tears of compunction; by day, dressed in rags, she went about the streets howling and calling everyone to the love of God.

Four years passed so. In 1837, after Seraphim's repose, the eldress Juliana Grigorieva, a nun of the monastery of Diveyevo who was known for the gift of foresight, met her in the road and asked her mother's leave to take her away with her; and it was done. Before she left the house Pelagia made a prostration to her relatives and said: forgive me, for the love of Christ; I shall not come back to you until I die.

At Diveyevo she went on as before. Some sisters respected and valued her, some feared her, some mocked her, and a few even struck her. While still alive she worked many wonders: she healed the artist M. P. Petrov, whose hand had been paralysed, put out a fire from far off, and appeared to many in their sleep and healed them afterwards. Four years before her repose she prophesied that Jacobinism and terror would spread in Russia and that they would kill the tsar Alexander II, for whom she wept and prayed without stopping.

After twenty years of that hard life Seraphim of Sarov appeared to her in sleep and urged her to withdraw into her cell and avoid people; and there she lived weeping and praying, fed chiefly on black bread, of which she made little balls to use as a prayer rope while she said the prayer of Jesus. Feeling her death a few days before, she received communion of the immaculate mysteries and made a prostration to all the sisters of the monastery; that was on Saturday 28 January 1884. At a quarter to two on the morning of Monday 30 January she breathed her last.

They dressed her in the clothes she had liked to wear — a white blouse, a sarafan, a woollen shawl, and a white silk kerchief about her head — laid her in a cypress coffin, and kept her nine days in her cell, with thirty or forty memorial services a day and the psalter chanted without ceasing. They buried her behind the sanctuary of the church of the Holy Trinity, as she had herself foretold seven years before. Her relics, the page says, are at the monastery of Diveyevo in Russia.
