# John Nannos of Thessalonica

saint.gr says the new martyr John, called Nannos, was carried away by the reading of the Lives of the saints and the martyrs, and, with the enthusiasm of his years upon him, wished to enter the choir of the martyrs himself. The only way he could see to it was to deny his faith and then wash the denial away in his own blood, making himself in that strange manner a new martyr out of those who had once denied Christ.

His father, who was also called John, came from Gynaikokastro, a village in the valley of the Axios, and his mother Thomais from Kolovi, near Polygyros in Chalkidike; but both were living at Thessalonica, where they married and had two sons, Theodore and John. The father was a shoemaker, and to find a living he left Thessalonica and settled at Smyrna; and when his sons were grown he took them to him and taught them his trade.

At Smyrna the young man spent his days at work, and his free hours — for he was unlettered himself — in hearing his brother read the holy Scriptures and the lives of the saints and martyrs, until the longing for martyrdom took fire in his heart. On 3 May 1802, telling nobody, and while he had been sent out by his father on an errand, he presented himself to the Turks and declared that he wished to join their religion. His father, alarmed at his delay, went looking for him with some kinsmen, and so learned of the apostasy; they hurried to find him, but the men about him drove them off violently and they could not come near.

On Sunday 25 May he put on Christian clothes, set the Turkish head-covering on his head, and presented himself again at their court, to confess this time that he was a Christian and that his name was John and not Mehmet. They were astonished, and tried to turn him: they offered him a costly robe and much money if he would confess Mohammed as God, and came at last to offering that he need only declare himself a Turk before them and might then go where he liked and believe what he liked. It was enough for them that he should leave the court as Mehmet and not as John.

A Turkish agha, seeing his patience, proposed sending him to Algiers in a ship crewed only by Turks, so that he should remain a Turk whether he would or not. John, afraid that the martyrdom he wanted would be frustrated that way, asked two days to consider, and they gave them to him, not seeing that the ship would sail meanwhile. Called back at the end of the second day, and knowing the ship gone, he said that he had not repented but wanted martyrdom now more than before; and they resolved to put him to death.

First they called his father, who was afraid and refused to come, saying that he had nothing to do with him any longer. So on Thursday 29 May 1802 John was led out to the Soan Bazaar, the place of executions, where a crowd had gathered to watch — not Christians only but many Turks, Franks and Armenians, who were astonished at the courage of the martyr.

After the beheading Christians tried to buy something of his to keep as an amulet, and the Turks gathered more than three thousand piastres from them, and went so far as to want to dismember the martyr to earn more. To prevent the cutting up of the honourable relic a devout Christian of Moscow named Panagiotis Panagiotopoulos bought it, bribing the judge, who was a friend of his, and the prefect; and they let him take the relic away and bury it. saint.gr says the account of this martyrdom was written by the hieromonk Nikephoros of Chios, and prints no hymn for him.
