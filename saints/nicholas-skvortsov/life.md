# Nicholas (Skvortsov)

Nicholas was born on 13 July 1875 in the village of Bunyakovo in the
Bronnitsy district of Moscow province, the son of the priest Gregory
Skvortsov and his wife Julia, finished the Moscow seminary in 1895 and was
ordained priest for the church of Cosmas and Damian at Kosmodamianskoye in
the Moscow district. In December 1917 he was moved to the church of the
apostles Peter and Paul in Lefortovo in Moscow, and there, under the
persecution, served the people of God as zealously as before. When the
teaching of the law of God was banned from the schools he organised it in
the church: several teachers, and of the children all who wished, more than
ten groups by age, twice a week, on Sundays and Thursdays. At four o’clock
exactly the children gathered in the yard; he came and blessed them, they
went into the church, and the young people, about a hundred, sang O Heavenly
King under his direction before the groups went off to their corners of the
church, standing, since there were no chairs or benches, each with its own
teacher, for a little over an hour — the smallest, who could not yet read,
first on the pictures in Ushinsky’s readers and then on the church’s own
icons, from which they had to know and tell the people of sacred history —
and the lesson ended with It is truly meet sung together and his blessing.
In the spring of 1922 a lesson was cancelled without warning: the commission
for the seizure of church valuables had come, a lorry with armed men drove
into the yard, the parishioners were pushed out and the gates locked, the
church shut with only the priest inside, the silver rizas taken from the
icons and every metal vessel but one, and when the people were let in again
the sight of the stripped church brought many to their knees in tears. The
lessons went on a little longer, until that same year he was arrested for
teaching religion illegally, held a week and released, and to the grief of
the children and their parents the teaching stopped. Every June the
wonder-working Jerusalem icon of the Mother of God was brought from
Izmailovo to Peter and Paul in memory of the deliverance from the hurricane
of 1904, one procession going out from Izmailovo and another from his church
to meet it, eight men carrying the icon; bishops were invited, most often
Juvenal (Maslovsky) and Procopius (Titov), with whom he grew close — the
people loved Procopius for his piety, gentleness and simplicity, knelt in
the street when he blessed them as he drove past, and were so many at his
services that he preached in the yard from a pulpit carried out for him; at
the end of the twenties the processions were forbidden. He often asked the
archpriest Vladimir Strakhov, professor of the Moscow academy, to serve and
preach, and after the akathist to the Joy of All Who Sorrow on Sundays
Strakhov lectured on the ascetic life out of Theophan the Recluse. Like many
devout people he loved Seraphim of Sarov, and went every summer with his
flock on pilgrimage to Sarov and Diveyevo until the monasteries were closed;
in time he was made archpriest, and several attempts to remove him from the
rectorship were defeated by his parishioners, who knew his selfless service.
In 1925 he was denounced to the OGPU for having said at the burial of a
teacher who had killed herself that she did it because the Soviet power
persecuted her, was arrested on 28 April and put in the Butyrki, and
released when it proved that he had said nothing of the kind.

In the census of early 1937, which asked about religion, the students who
took it — some of them his parishioners — were cursed by their superiors
because so many people insisted on being written down Orthodox and even
looked at the form to see that it was; to anyone who wavered and asked him
what to do he always said firmly, write it as it really is. That year the
NKVD man set to watch the church told his wife to persuade him to stop going
about in a cassock and wear “civilian dress”; he thought and said, you know,
mother, if I wear the cassock they will arrest me and punish me severely,
and if I wear civilian dress they will punish me just the same, so let it be
the cassock. In April 1937 he was moved to the Znamenskaya church at Perovo,
and on 29 August 1937 arrested and put in the Butyrki. He denied agitating
among the believers at the funerals he served at the German cemetery, and
denied teaching the law of God secretly to school-children in his admirers’
flats; the money he gave, he said, went to his sister and to the old people
on the church porch. The warden of Peter and Paul, told to characterise him
politically, refused, saying he had met him rarely and never talked with
him; a parishioner said he had great authority with the believers, gathered
the children round the community, held the community in his hands, and that
when he was moved to Perovo the parishioners saw him off in tears; and the
priest Demetrius Tsvetkov, often called by the NKVD as a duty witness
against his brethren, said that two girls had asked him what was read in
church that day because Father Nicholas would question them, and that only
Skvortsov could have given a girl a book by the priest Petrov. On 26
September 1937 the troika sentenced him to death; he was shot the next day,
27 September 1937, and buried in an unknown common grave at Butovo. The life
is by the igumen Damascene (Orlovsky); the Church keeps him on 15 September
of the old style, 28 September, and in the synaxes of the new martyrs of
Butovo and of Moscow.
