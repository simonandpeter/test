# Patermuthius (17 December)

saint.gr's calendar for 17 December keeps three venerable-martyrs together, Patermuthius, Copris and Alexander, and Patermuthius is the first of them. The page says that they were Christians full of piety and zeal, that they were tortured and at last beheaded by order of Julian the Transgressor, whose years it gives as 361 to 363, and that this happened while Julian was on campaign against the Persians.

Of Patermuthius himself it says one thing, and it is the thing the company is remembered by: that [Copris](/saints/copris-17-december), who was a young man, lost his nerve a little at the start, and that Patermuthius strengthened him with his warm words. All three then underwent the martyr's death, the page says, chanting hymns to Christ; [Alexander](/saints/alexander-17-december) is the third of them. Its epigram has Copris cry out to him, father Patermuthius, I have been made sharer with you of your life and of your sword.

That is the whole of the entry. The page names no city and no province, no judge and no prison; it gives no year of birth for any of the three and dates the martyrdom only by the reign; it lists no relic and prints no hymn. The corpus keeps another martyr Patermuthius on 17 September, who suffered in an earlier persecution with Elias and the bishops Peleus and Nilus, and he is a different man.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two of them on the Julian 9 July, where the Greek calendar keeps them on 17 December — two calendars and two days, and both are recorded. Both were Egyptians, it says, tortured by the emperor Julian the Apostate; the first was seventy-five years old and the second forty-five, which are ages and not dates and are recorded nowhere as years. And it tells a fall and a recovery that no other book has. The emperor **succeeded** in turning Copres from the faith of Christ and winning him over to the idols, and the turned Copres cried out: I am Julian’s and not Christ’s.

But when the elder Patermuthius rebuked him and warned him of the eternal torments, Copres started up and cried out before the emperor: I am Christ’s and not Julian’s. Both of them were then beheaded with the sword. With them suffered also a soldier of the emperor’s who, seeing their courage in suffering, became a Christian himself; his own folder stands on the Greek 17 December with theirs, and this reading writes him no Serbian row, because the heading of the entry names the two of them and not him. They suffered honourably for Christ and went to Christ, this book ends, in the year 361 — a year inside the interval the Greek page gives by Julian’s reign and his Persian campaign, so the folder’s recorded interval is left standing rather than narrowed to it.

This day prints the same two names twice. The entry that follows this one opens by saying that though of the same names, those were different men from these — two venerables and wonderworkers of the end of the fourth century, where these two are martyrs of Julian’s reign. The book makes the distinction itself, and this folder is the first pair’s.
