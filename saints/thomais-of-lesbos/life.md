# Thomais of Lesbos

Thomais was unknown as a saint on Lesbos, her own island, until 1961, when Professor Ioannis M. Fountoulis made her known in a series of articles after scholarly research. saint.gr says she was born on Lesbos between 910 and 913 to Michael and Kale, people of means, upright and very devout, who had met their childlessness mourning and downcast but never stopped praying; at the last the Mother of God warned Kale in a divine dream that she would not only bear a child but that the child would stand out in gifts and in holiness.

The daughter they had they named Thomais, and as she grew she was marked out by her gifts and her beauty. She had no inclination to marry and admired the monastic life, but in obedience to her parents' wish she was married between 934 and 937, at twenty-four, to a man named Stephen — a name, the page says bitterly, that became for her a crown of thorns for the rest of her life. She was known to everyone as a model wife, and she suffered terribly from her husband's brutality: beatings, blows to the face, kicks even in the mouth, burnings, wounds opened all over her body.

They left Mytilene and settled in Constantinople, and her parents seem to have followed her there, abandoning their property and meeting many privations. After her father's death her mother entered the convent known as ta Mikra Romaiou, on the seventh hill between the Selymbria gate and the gate of the Polyandrion, became a nun, and was later its abbess. Thomais met the whole of her married martyrdom with prayer, patience and good works.

God blessed her faith with the grace of wonders when she asked His help for people who were suffering, and fourteen are recorded from her lifetime in Constantinople: she healed a man possessed, a paralytic, a man with a cancer and others. After thirteen years of that marriage she died at thirty-eight and was buried in the convent where her mother had been a nun and was buried; forty days later her relic was taken up whole and laid in a costly reliquary in the convent church, and on her honourable hands the marks of her husband's blows could still be made out.

Her tomb became a spring of wonders — a possessed man named Constantine, a paralytic named Eutychianos, a nun with terrible headaches, a man with epilepsy, a fisherman whose lost nets came back full of fish, a woman healed of pains in her bowels who built a great arch over the grave in gratitude — and the last recorded is the healing of her husband himself, who fell into possession after her death and was brought to her tomb in chains. Her body was probably lost when the Franks took Constantinople in 1204. The service for her was composed by Gerasimos Mikragiannanites in 1967 and printed in the Lesbian Menaion.
