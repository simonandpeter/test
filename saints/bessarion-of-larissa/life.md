# Bessarion

Bessarion was born at Porta Panagia near Trikala in 1490, a few years
after the fall of Constantinople; though conditions were very hard he was
nursed and raised by his devout parents in the sacred waters of the faith
and the ideals of the nation, in a family full of the fear of God. From
youth he was marked by intelligence and good sense, uprightness of
character, purity and sobriety, humility and deep faith and boundless love,
and very young showed his bent for monasticism: at only ten he became a
novice under the metropolitan of Larissa, Mark, who ordained him deacon
and presbyter; in 1517 he was chosen bishop of Elassona, in 1521 took the
government of the bishopric of Stagoi, and in March 1527 was raised to the
throne of the metropolis of Larissa, holding too, as locum tenens until
August 1529, the bishopric of Stagoi — and shone with the holiness of his
life and an astonishing work of good, while bearing with wonderful
forbearance slanders and hard trials, in which he shone as gold in the
furnace. As a hierarch he did a most important work, pastoral, social and
charitable, and so won the souls of the Christians and became a great
religious and social figure: he ransomed and freed many captives, and took
particular care for the protection and relief of the poor, the naked and
the hungry. Despite the hard times he proved, too, a busy “minister of
public works”: under his own eye and on his own initiative he paid for and
built a long series of valuable and difficult works for the common good —
churches, roads and above all bridges, one more witness to the church’s
priceless service to society in every age and place — and to raise the
money made many long journeys, as far as the Balkans, Hungary and
Czechoslovakia. As a bridge-builder he inspired a good many bridges: on the
Portaikos river of his own Porta Panagia, at Sarakina near Kalabaka on the
Pineios (both of which join the Thessalian plain to its mountains), the
bridge of Korakos on the Acheloos joining Thessaly to Epirus, and the first
bridge of Plaka, joining Tzoumerka to Arta and Ioannina. For the peace of
his soul he built on Koziakas the monastery of the Saviour of the Great
Gates, Dousiko, between 1527 and 1534/35, on the ruins of an old house of
the same name; it was raised again from its foundations by his nephew and
second founder Neophytos II of Larissa in 1550–1552/57, frescoed by the
master painter Tzortzis in 1557, and stands in excellent condition to this
day. He wrote three testaments and laid down that Dousiko should be closed
to women, which is kept to this day with great reverence. On 13 (or 15)
September 1540, a Monday, he gave his holy soul into God’s hands, and has
been hymned since then as a saint and wonderworker; the sicknesses he heals
above all are dysentery, smallpox, scarlet fever and most of all plague,
and he heals the diseases of animals too, kills locusts, calms the storm
at sea and helps the fruitfulness of the earth. His fragrant skull is kept
in his monastery. His memory is kept on 15 September with particular
reverence and splendour at his monastery, at Pyli and at Kalabaka, and at
Trikala his skull is carried in festal procession on the Sunday of the
Samaritan woman; the first hymns and synaxarion texts for him were written
very early, by Pachomios Rousanos in 1552, and some sources, the
synaxarion adds, know of another Bessarion, archbishop of Larissa, who
lived before this saint. The Romanian page has him repose on 13 September
1541, aged nearly fifty-one; the Greek and Romanian calendars keep him on
15 September with a troparion that calls him Bessarion the merciful, who
loved the poor and supplied them.
