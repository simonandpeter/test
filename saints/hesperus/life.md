# Hesperus

Hesperus, his wife [Zoe](/saints/zoe-wife-of-hesperus) and their children [Cyriacus](/saints/cyriacus-son-of-hesperus) and [Theodulus](/saints/theodulus-son-of-hesperus) lived in the second century, saint.gr says, in the years of the emperor Hadrian. They came of Pamphylia, and they belonged as slaves to a Roman named Catallus and his wife Petradia, who forbade the Christian household to pray and to hymn its Creator, and at other times ordered them to do work that went against the spirit of the Gospel.

Their synaxarion tells, the page says, how Zoe used to go in the evening to the guard who kept the masters' house and tell him to go and sleep, because he was tired, and take his duty in his place. Outside the gates of the great house there were dogs that tore at any poor man who came to ask for help or charity; and Zoe would take the bread given her for her family's daily keep, throw a little to the dogs to quiet them, and share out the rest among the poor, saying to them: become Christians, servants of Christ, for He alone is the Saviour of the world.

When in the year 125 Catallus had a son, he wanted all his slaves to keep the occasion, and ordered them to a common table to eat their fill of meat offered to idols, and wine. Hesperus and his family kept to dry food. Their Roman master noticed and ordered them to eat by force, and every one of them refused and said that they were sorry that this once they would not obey their master. Beside himself with rage, Catallus had a great furnace lit, and when it was thoroughly hot he threw the four martyrs into it. But when they opened the furnace in the morning they found all four of them dead and their bodies unburnt: the Lord of life and of death had granted them incorruption and the glory of heaven.

Hesperus is the father, and the day's listing names him first. The page gives no year for his birth, no account of him apart from the household, and no hymn; its couplet for him and his wife says only that fire deprived Hesperus and Zoe together of life, who longed for the one life that has no evening.

The Serbian church keeps the four on this same menologion day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović tells the household in seven sentences which differ from the Greek notice in what the dogs were for. In the time of the emperor Hadrian, it says — and it prints his years, 117 to 138 — a certain pagan named Katal bought as slaves Hesperus, his wife Zoe and their sons Cyriacus and Theodulus. Being convinced Christians, they would taste nothing of the sacrifices to the idols; what was given them they threw to the dogs, and they themselves went hungry and endured.

When Katal learned of it he was greatly angered and began to torture all his slaves grievously. He tortured the children first, but the children stood unshaken in the faith and, more than that, asked their torturer for heavier torments. At the last all four were thrown into a fiery furnace, where after a prayer of thanksgiving they gave up their spirits to the Lord; and their bodies remained whole and unburnt by the fire.

**No year was taken from this page, although it does print the bounds of the reign.** 117 to 138 contains the 125 the Greek notice gives for the year the master got a son and called his slaves to the feast, so widening the folder's death to the reign would make it vaguer than its own source already allows; nothing was touched. **Where the two books part is the bread**: this one has the household throw its own food to the dogs and starve, where the Greek page has Zoe quiet the dogs with a little of the family's bread and share the rest among the poor. Both were read and neither was chosen.

The Russian church calendar keeps the four of them on the same day, puts the martyrdom in the second century under Hadrian, who reigned from 117 to 138, and tells the household at length. The husband and wife had received Christianity in childhood and brought up their children in the same deep faith. All four were slaves of a noble Roman named Катулл who lived in the city of Attalia in Asia Minor, and while serving their earthly master they never defiled themselves with food offered to idols, which the pagans required of everyone.

Once Катулл sent him away on business to Тритония, and in that time the two sons resolved to run off rather than live in constant company with pagans. Their mother would not bless them for it; so the young men asked her blessing for an open confession of their faith in Christ instead, and were given it. When the brothers told Катулл that they were Christians he was astonished, but did not give them over to torture: he sent them with their mother to their father in Тритония, hoping the parents would talk the children out of the faith. There they lived quietly for a time and prepared themselves for the contest ahead of them.

For the birthday of Катулл's son all the slaves were brought back to Attalia and a feast was held in the house in honour of the goddess Fortune. Dishes were sent out to the slaves from the masters' table, and among them meat and wine that had been offered to the idols. None of them touched it; [Zoe, wife of Hesperus](/saints/zoe-wife-of-hesperus) poured the wine out on the ground and threw the meat to the dogs. When Катулл learnt of it he ordered her sons, [Cyriacus, son of Hesperus](/saints/cyriacus-son-of-hesperus) and [Theodulus, son of Hesperus](/saints/theodulus-son-of-hesperus), to be tormented. They were stripped and hung on a tree and torn with iron instruments before their parents' eyes, and the parents through the torture went on teaching their children to endure to the end for the faith.

Then the parents themselves were put to cruel tortures; and at the last all four of the martyrs were thrown into a red-hot furnace, where with prayer they gave up their souls to the Lord. Their bodies were kept unharmed in the fire, and the singing of angels was heard, glorifying the contest of the Lord's confessors. The day page carries no hymn for any of the four.
