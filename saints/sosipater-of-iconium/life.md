# Sosipater of Iconium

Sosipater was an apostle out of Achaia. He and [Jason](/saints/jason-of-tarsus), who was a man of Tarsus and the first of that city to come over to the right faith, became disciples of the apostle Paul after receiving the faith of Christ, and Paul calls them both his kinsmen in the letter to the Romans. Jason was chosen bishop of Tarsus, his own city, and Sosipater took the governance of the Church of Iconium; both shepherded their churches well and increased them.

They went afterwards into the parts of the West, and on the island of Corfu built a very beautiful church in the name of Stephen the First Martyr, serving God there and drawing many to Christ. Denounced to Kerkylinos, the ruler of the island, they were shut in a prison where seven robbers sat — Saturninus, Jakishchol, Faustinian, Januarius, Marsalius, Euphrasius and Mammius — whom they brought to the faith, making sheep of wolves; the seven were then boiled in a cauldron of pitch, brimstone and wax, and the gaoler, who also believed, was maimed and beheaded.

The two apostles were handed over to a noble named Karpianus and sent back to prison. Kerkyra, the ruler's daughter, seeing what they suffered and learning it was for Christ, confessed herself a Christian, gave away her ornaments to the poor, and was shut up apart; she was given to an Ethiopian for her ruin, but a bear seized him at the prison door and she drove it off with the name of Christ, healed him and taught him, so that he died a martyr. Her prison was burnt about her and she was found unhurt; she was hanged, choked with smoke and shot with arrows until she died.

The ruler then pursued the believers, who hid on a nearby island; swimming after them he was drowned in mid-sea by the anger of God, as Pharaoh was. Jason and Sosipater came out of prison and taught freely. A later governor had them thrown into an iron bath of boiling pitch, brimstone and wax; they were unburnt, while many unbelievers standing round were burnt, and many who saw it believed.

The king of the island believed also, hung a stone about his neck and cried for mercy to the God of Jason and Sosipater; they taught him, baptised him and named him Sebastian. When his son fell ill and died a few days later, they prayed and raised him. They worked many other wonders, built beautiful churches with the king and increased the flock of Christ; and at last, in old age, their life being finished, they went up to Christ whom they had desired. The page gives no year.

The Romanian calendar keeps him a second time on 10 noiembrie, among the company of six apostles of the Seventy held that day — Olympas, Herodion, Sosipater, Erastus, Tertius and Quartus — and the short notice it prints for him there says only that he is one of the seventy apostles, that the Apostle Paul mentions him in the Epistle to the Romans, that he was bishop of Iconium, and that he ended in peace.

The Greek calendar keeps the two apostles on 29 Απριλίου, a day later than the Romanian, and saint.gr spends most of its notice on where Jason came from and what Paul meant by calling him his kinsman. Jason it gives as of Tarsus, or of Thessalonica by an old manuscript that Nikodemos the Hagiorite notes, and Sosipater as of Achaia. It rehearses the two places in the New Testament where Jason's name stands — the Acts, where the Jews who could not find Paul in Jason's house dragged Jason and the other brethren before the politarchs, and the Epistle to the Romans, where he is among those sending greetings.

The «συγγενεῖς» of that epistle, the page argues, most probably means that Paul and Jason were of one trade and not kin by blood; and it cites Chrysostom, Theophylact, Theodoret of Cyrus and Oecumenius for the two Jasons being one man. Jason seems to have kept a small weaving workshop, in which Paul found work — which would mean he was settled at Thessalonica, perhaps permanently; and the one certain thing, the page says, is that Jason lived at Thessalonica and became a disciple of the apostle Paul. Dorotheus of Tyre's report that he was one of the Seventy disciples it sets aside.

At Corfu the two were prisoners of the ruler Kerkyllinos and were handed over to the eparch Capreanus, and in the prison they turned seven robbers to Christ, who were martyred later, and the gaoler Antonius with them. The torments they bore moved the ruler's daughter Kerkyra, who embraced the faith. They were thrown into an iron cauldron of pitch and resin: Jason came out unharmed and Sosipater died in it. At that trial Kerkyllinos himself repented, was catechized, baptized, and renamed Sebastianos; and Jason, the Acts of the Saints say, lived to great old age serving the Church of Corfu and building churches, one of which the people of Corfu reckon the oldest in their town.

On the relics the Greek page says two different things and neither is resolved here. Its list of holy relics puts Jason's skull at the Metropolis of Thebes and Sosipater's at the Metropolis of Thebes and Levadeia, with fragments of both at the Metropolis of Kerkyra and at the monastery of Dionysiou on the Holy Mountain, of Jason also at Kykkos in Cyprus and of Sosipater also at the metropolitan church of Veroia; but its last line says that the honourable heads of both are kept with reverence at the holy monastery of Hosios Loukas in Boeotia. The couplets say of Jason that he received the end of a corruptible life and found another that has no end, and of Sosipater that the Father shows the glory of His countenance to him who has died.
