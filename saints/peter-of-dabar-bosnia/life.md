# Peter

Peter — John Zimonić in the world — was born on 24 June 1866 at Grahovo in
Herzegovina, then under the Ottomans, the son of the priest and vojvoda
Bogdan Zimonić, a famous popular hero who fought in the Herzegovinian rising
of 1875–1878 against the Turks, after which the province passed to
Austria-Hungary; like his father he was bound all his life to the fate of
the Orthodox people of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Taught first at home, he
studied at the seminary of Reljevo from 1883 to 1887, then at the Orthodox
theological faculty at Chernivtsi, took his degree with distinction in 1892
and spent a year at the university of Vienna; from 27 October 1893 he was a
lecturer and from 14 October 1895 professor at the theological school of
Reljevo, a tireless worker and gifted teacher whom the seminarians
especially loved for his modesty, his courtesy and his sincerity. On 6
September 1895 he was tonsured with the name Peter in the monastery of
Žitomislić, ordained hierodeacon the next day and hieromonk the day after;
on 20 July 1901 he was made consistorial adviser of the metropolis of
Dabar-Bosnia at Sarajevo and in 1903 archimandrite, and in that year the
Synod of the patriarchate of Constantinople, to which the Bosnian sees then
belonged, chose him metropolitan of Zahumlje and Herzegovina, news received
with special joy in Orthodox Herzegovina; he was consecrated on 27 May 1903
in the cathedral of Mostar, which the Ustaše later left a heap of ruins. He
had to restore a neglected diocese and at the same time fight the Austrian
authorities for Serbian autonomy in education, a fight largely won by 1905,
when the Serbs received their church autonomy, after which he could give
more time to the church courts, to building churches and to organising
Serbian schools: besides many churches he built the great metropolitan’s
residence at Mostar and opened a monastic school at Žitomislić. The years
that followed went against the Orthodox Church and the Serbian people — the
annexation of Bosnia in 1908, the Balkan wars, and the First World War,
which began with terror against the Orthodox clergy, several priests of his
diocese being killed or dying in camps. He stayed at his post and did
everything to ease his flock’s lot: a patriot and fearless defender of the
people’s and the Church’s interests, on the eve of Austria-Hungary’s
declaration of war on Serbia he demanded of an Austrian general in the
cathedral that the expulsion of Serbs cease, or he would appeal to the
emperor or call the Serbian people to rise, and in the famine of the war he
gathered children and sent them to better-provided districts. From 1918, in
the new Kingdom of the Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, he worked for the reunion
of the scattered parts of the Serbian Church, and on the death of
Metropolitan Eugene (Letica) was elected to the see of Dabar-Bosnia at
Sarajevo, confirmed by King Alexander’s decree of 7 November 1920, keeping
the administration of Herzegovina besides.

His work was the Gospel, and the preaching of peace and good neighbourhood
in a Bosnia of many peoples: many Muslims and Catholics of Sarajevo knew and
respected him, the traders and craftsmen of the Muslim quarter came out to
greet him with reverence when he walked through it, and every Christmas he
went in person to greet the Catholic archbishop. Many churches were built
and consecrated under him, among them the fine church of the Transfiguration
in New Sarajevo before the war, and he planned a seminary in the city; the
Brotherhood of St Sava spread books and journals and organised lectures, the
Church ran charities, an orphanage and a soup kitchen for orphans, and he
gave special care to the teaching of the law of God, whose lessons he often
visited himself; he ordained about forty priests and lived in a rare harmony
with his clergy and people, received several decorations of state and the
Church’s highest orders, and when Patriarch Barnabas died in 1938 his name
was among the likeliest for the patriarchal throne — before the matter was
settled, Patriarch Gabriel (Dožić) remembered, Peter came to him himself and
on his knees begged him to take up that heavy cross. On 6 April 1941 Germany
and its allies attacked Yugoslavia and Sarajevo was heavily bombed; at his
people’s insistence he left the city for a time, but on 21 April came back
to a city where the Ustaše were already at work with arrests and pogroms. He
knew already of the death of his brother bishop, the hieromartyr Platon of
Banja Luka, and refused to leave his flock — where I shared good with the
people I share evil with them, and so I must share the people’s fate and
stay in my place. On 27 April Gestapo officers searched the metropolis; one
asked whether he was the metropolitan who had wanted war with the Germans
and added that he ought to be killed, and to their insults against the
Serbian bishops he answered, sir, you are badly mistaken: we are not guilty
of the war, we attacked no one, but we will not let ourselves be destroyed;
we are not a drop of water that is easily swallowed but a people with a
right to live — and told the Gestapo man that it was the Germans who were to
blame for the Ustaše’s unpunished violence. One of the first orders of the
“independent” Croatia was a ban on the Cyrillic alphabet; in early May the
Ustaša commissioner for Bosnia, the Catholic priest Božidar Bralo,
telephoned to demand that he order the Orthodox clergy and parishes to give
it up and replace their documents and seals with Latin script at once, on
his own responsibility — the Cyrillic alphabet cannot disappear in
twenty-four hours, he answered, and besides, the war is not over yet. He
refused likewise to sing a thanksgiving for the head of the Ustaša state,
Ante Pavelić, expected arrest at any moment, and refused every counsel to
leave. On 12 May 1941 police agents took him from the metropolis; three days
later they brought him back for an inventory of the money and took his
panagias, decorations and securities, and on 17 May he was taken to Zagreb
and put in the police prison, where by a cellmate’s testimony, tired and old
as he was, he bore himself bravely and heartened the other prisoners. From
Zagreb he was moved to the camp of Kerestinec, shaved, stripped of his
mantle and cruelly mocked, and later to the concentration camp of Gospić,
where he was tortured again — led about the camp yard in the rain, beaten
with rifle butts and made in mockery to preach to the prisoners. What
followed is not known exactly. Through 1941 and 1942 the Holy Synod of the
Serbian Church tried repeatedly to learn his fate and obtain his release,
without result; by one account he was moved from Gospić to Jadovno and
killed there by a hammer blow to the head, his body thrown into one of the
pits that were the graves of thousands, by another he was killed at
Jasenovac and his body burned in the brick kiln, and his grave is unknown —
but it is beyond dispute that he died a martyr for Christ and for
faithfulness to Orthodoxy, fulfilling to the end the word that the good
shepherd lays down his life for the sheep. In 1982, for the fortieth
anniversary of his death, his successor Metropolitan Vladislav (Mitrović)
wrote that by his goodness and noble heart he had enjoyed great authority
among all the citizens whatever their faith and nation, and, preaching love,
forgiveness and mercy, had laid down his life as a martyr. In 1998 the
Bishops’ Council of the Serbian Church glorified him among the hieromartyrs,
several churches have since been built in his name, and on 27 December 2000
the Holy Synod of the Russian Church entered his name in its calendar, on
the day the Serbian council had set. The Serbian calendar keeps him on 4
September of the old style, 17 September, and the Russian calendar the same
day.
