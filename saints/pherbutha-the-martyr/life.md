# Pherbutha, with her sister and her niece

saint.gr puts Pherbutha and her companions in the fourth century, in the days of the emperor Constantine, when Shapur II was king in Persia. When the Persian queen fell ill, some women laid the illness at the door of the Persian Pherbutha and of her sister, saying it came of their working magic. The two were arrested and examined, and they answered that they were altogether innocent of such a crime, since they held the Christian faith, which condemns all sorcery.

Their guilt was not proved. But the declaration that they were Christians turned the judge against them, and so, as supposed witches and as Christians, they were sentenced to death. Condemned with them was the daughter of Pherbutha's sister, her niece — a tender little girl, the page says, and very young. The monstrous sentence rather gladdened the holy women, because they were suffering for Christ.

The manner of their death was of the cruellest. They were sawn lengthways from the neck down to the feet, and their bodies, halved as they were, were nailed up on stakes. The page gives no year, no city and no judge's name; it names neither the sister nor the niece, so neither of them is kept here as a person of her own. It prints two couplets, one for Pherbutha and one for the other two, and no hymn.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the three women on the same menologion day and tells how they came to be killed. In the time of the Persian king Shapur, it says, the bishop Saint [Simeon](/saints/simeon-of-persia) was put to death; and Pherbutha, the bishop's sister, was taken into the palace at the queen's own wish. She was of unusual beauty, and many suitors pressed her for that, among them the pagan priests and the diviners; she refused them all, and so raised a great anger against herself. When the queen fell ill about that time, the priests all explained to the king that the queen had been poisoned by Pherbutha, and as a cure for the sick queen they prescribed this: that Pherbutha, her sister and their servant, being Christians, should be sawn through with a saw, three halves of the bodies laid on one side and three on the other, and the queen carried between them. The king agreed to that bloodthirsty proposal of the diviners.

So Pherbutha suffered for Christ with her sister and the third woman in the year 343, the entry says, and earned by it the unfading crown in the immortal kingdom of her Lord. The year falls inside the fourth century this folder already holds and is narrower than it, so the interval was not narrowed. **Who the third woman was is not the same in the two accounts**: the Greek page makes her a niece, and this heading and this body both call her a slave-girl of the household, a widowed sister standing beside her. Neither reading was altered to fit the other, and this folder's English name keeps the Greek one's.
