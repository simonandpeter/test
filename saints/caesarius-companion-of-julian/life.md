# Caesarius, companion of Julian

The Romanian life sets Caesarius in the reign of Claudius at Rome, who had put his own mother to death for her faith in Christ and had no mercy left for Christians. Caesarius came out of Africa to a village the life calls Tarachini, saw the sacrifices offered there, spat on them and trampled them. He was seized, kept three days in prison without food, and handed over to the proconsul.

His hands were tied behind him and he was dragged before the governor's carriage as far as the temple of Apollo. There he prayed, and the temple fell from its foundations with the chief priest of the idols and many others inside it. Leontius, seeing that, fell at the saint's feet, believed in Christ and was baptised before them all; the priest [Julian](/saints/julian-companion-of-caesarius) gave him the holy Mysteries, and he gave up his soul at once, as he had asked in the saint's prayer.

Loxorius the commander then arrested Julian and Caesarius the deacon and ordered them sewn into sacks and thrown into the sea. The saints told him that they would be drowned but that he would be bitten by an evil serpent and lose his soul by a bad death; two days later a great serpent coiled about him by the shore and crushed him, and he lay swollen and pitiable until he died. Their bodies were brought out of the sea and buried near the city by a priest named Eusebius and by Felix, who had been sent by a vision.

The Greek calendar keeps the two on the same day, 7 October, and gives the whole story the Romanian line left out. saint.gr sets them in the days of the Caesar Claudius II, about 268. Caesarius was from Africa and came to the city of Tarracina in Italy; seeing the people sacrificing to idols he rebuked them sharply, was arrested and brought before the proconsul, who imprisoned him, and was then led under guard to the temple of Apollo, which fell down at his prayer. Seeing that, [Leontius](/saints/leontius-the-hypatikos) the hypatikos threw himself at his feet and asked for baptism.

Julian the presbyter baptised him, and Leontius had no sooner communicated of the immaculate mysteries than he breathed his last. The tyrant Loxorios then ordered Caesarius and Julian put into sacks and thrown into the sea, and so they gave up their souls to God. Shortly before the end Julian foretold to Loxorios that within two days he would not be able to walk that shore; two days later he was bitten by a venomous snake as he walked where the saints had been killed. Two Christians, Eusebius the presbyter and Felix, found the bodies and buried them with fitting honour, and the tyrant had them beheaded for it.

The Russian calendar keeps the two on its own 7 October, «Мчч. Иулиана пресвитера и Кесария диакона (I)», and dates them to the first century where the Greek page puts them about 268, under Claudius II; the disagreement is recorded on this folder's Russian row and is not resolved. Its index of names calls him «Кесарий Терракинский, диакон, сщмч.», Caesarius of Terracina.

The short life behind the line gives him the first move of the story. He was shut in prison at the Italian town of Terracina for insulting the pagan gods, and when he was led there bound towards the temple of Apollo the shrine fell down at his approach, killing the priests and a crowd of people. It was then that the idol-servers seized [Julian](/saints/julian-companion-of-caesarius), a Christian priest. By the emperor's order both were thrown into the sea; their bodies floated up and Christians buried them, and the Russian life adds that the relics of Caesarius are at Rome.
