# Ketevan

Ketevan, a descendant of the royal Bagration family and great-granddaughter
of King Constantine of Kartli, became the wife of David, heir of Alexander II
of Kakheti, and ruled the kingdom herself; her deep piety showed in her care
for the needs of the Georgian church, building churches, shelters and houses
for travellers and the homeless, and after her husband’s death she withdrew
into solitude. Constantine, called Okayan, her husband’s brother, accepted
Islam and, on the orders of Shah Abbas I, sent assassins to kill his dying
father Alexander II and his brother George, then had the bodies put on
camels and sent to the queen, who, horrified, mourned the innocent
sufferers and buried them at the cathedral of Alaverdi; then the faithless
Constantine asked for her hand, threatening force if she refused. She
gathered her people of Kakheti, went against him and defeated the apostate,
who died an inglorious death with many of the Persian army, and under her
wise rule peace and justice were restored in Kakheti; Shah Abbas gave back
her son Teimuraz, who had lived years at the shah’s court as a hostage and
kept his Orthodox faith unstained. When the shah, threatening Georgia with
destruction, forced the lords of Kakheti to hand over notable hostages,
Ketevan went among them as a volunteer, wishing to forestall disaster for
the Georgian nation and the church, and came to Isfahan. There the shah
urged her to accept the Muslim faith and got a flat refusal, and she was
thrown into prison, where she spent ten years of a martyr’s sufferings:
neither the baseness of the Persian courtiers, nor the shah’s cunning
offers to raise her to be queen of Persia, nor the promise of great wealth,
nor the pleading of the courtiers and nobles could draw from her a single
word of blasphemy against Christ. She was hung crucified on a tree in the
shape of a cross and tortured with red-hot pincers; a helmet heated red was
set on her head, she was beaten with steel rods and her body pierced with
nails, and the blessed martyr gave her soul into God’s hands on
13 September 1624. Three shining pillars came down on her body, the sign of
her victory. Augustinian friars who had witnessed the martyrdom carried her
bones to Rome, to the basilica of St Peter, and gave part of them — her
precious head and right hand — to King Teimuraz I, who laid them under the
altar of the cathedral of St George at Alaverdi in Kakheti. The
catholicos-patriarch Zacharias numbered her among the saints and set her
feast on 13 September; the Romanian calendar keeps her on that day.
