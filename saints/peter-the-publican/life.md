# Peter the Publican

Peter was the chief collector of taxes in Africa in the reign of Justinian (527–565), a cruel and unmerciful man. Once, pestered by a beggar who would not stop asking, he threw a piece of bread at him. That night he dreamed that he had died and that angels were weighing his deeds on the scales of God’s judgement: there was nothing to lay on the side of good deeds but the bread he had thrown at the beggar in annoyance, and even that halted the scale of his evil deeds as it sank. Peter understood the dream, repented and changed his life entirely, giving generously to those in need and feeding and clothing many. Then he saw Christ in a dream, dressed in a garment Peter had once given to a beggar; so he gave all he had to the poor and told his own slave to sell him into slavery and give the money to the poor, which the slave did.

For many years Peter worked for his master without complaint. One day merchants who had known him before recognised him and told his master who his servant was. Hearing them, Peter left the city at once, and as he went he worked a wonder: the gatekeeper, a deaf-mute slave, received Peter’s command in the name of Jesus Christ to open the gate, did so, and at once could hear and speak. He hurried to tell his master, adding that when the saint gave the command a fire had come out of his mouth and touched his face. They all went to look for Peter, but in vain; he stayed hidden until his death. The Russian calendar says his life was handed down by John the Merciful, Patriarch of Alexandria, who had it from a man who knew Peter.

The Ohrid Prologue, for the Serbian calendar, names him Peter the Merciful, a servant of God of the sixth century, and tells his story in the reflection for the day with differences. There the beggars made a wager among themselves that one of them would get alms from Peter, and the one who begged would not leave off until Peter, in a rage, struck him with a loaf, having nothing else to hand. Peter then fell suddenly and gravely ill, and in a vision saw demons heaping his sins on one side of a balance while grieving angels had nothing to set on the other but that loaf, and the loaf outweighed all his sins. From then on he became the most merciful man in his city, gave all his property to the poor, and at last sold himself into slavery for thirty gold pieces and gave that price away as well; that, the Prologue says, is why he was called the Merciful.
