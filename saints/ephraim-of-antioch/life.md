# Ephraim of Antioch

saint.gr says Ephraim came from the city of Amida — a city of Turkish Kurdistan and a port on the river Tigris, the page explains, called Diyarbakır today. He began in the world as comes of Antioch, under the emperor Justin the Thracian, who reigned from 518 to 527; and from a layman, at the emperor's own suggestion, he went up to the patriarchal throne of Antioch in the year 526.

As patriarch he was very active, the page says, in clearing his province of the Monophysites; and as the holy Photius reports in the Myriobiblos, at pages 228 and 229, Ephraim also wrote many works against them. A stylite of Hierapolis, a fanatical Monophysite, he brought back to the right faith by a wonder. For twenty years he shepherded the people as he ought, and he died peacefully — which agrees with the year 546 the page's own head gives him.

That is the whole of the notice: no year of birth, no council, no other act of the episcopate, no relic and no hymn. Over him the page prints a single distich, in which Ephraim the shepherd is now with the Shepherd, the small with the great — and it exclaims at the greatness of the worth of it.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 8 June, where the Greek calendar keeps him on 7 March — and not on its own Julian 7 March, which prints two other entries and does not name him. Its account is three times the length of the Greek one and does not agree with it. In the days of the emperor Anastasius, it says, Ephraim was voivode of the eastern parts, known to everyone for his great piety and mercy and much respected for it; and when Antioch had to be rebuilt after an earthquake and a fire, the emperor appointed him to direct the work, which he did with diligence and love.

Among the ordinary workmen, masons like the rest, was a bishop who had left his bishopric for reasons unknown and laboured as a common hand, and nobody knew what he was. One day the man lay down with the others to rest from the heavy work and fell asleep, and Ephraim, looking at him, saw a pillar of fire going up from him to the heavens. Astonished and afraid, he called the man to him and adjured him to say who he was. The man held back a long while and at last admitted that he was a bishop, and foretold that Ephraim would shortly be consecrated patriarch of Antioch — the see standing empty, because the old patriarch Euphrasius had perished in the earthquake. And in those very days Ephraim was elected and consecrated.

For his goodness, his purity and his zeal for Orthodoxy God gave him a great gift of wonderworking. Once, to convince a heretic that Orthodoxy is the truth, he put his own omophorion into the fire and prayed to God; and the omophorion stood three hours in the fire and came out unharmed, and the heretic was appalled at the sight and threw off his heresy. He ended in peace in 540, where the Greek page gives 546 and the folder now holds both. The Greek page’s emperor is Justin and not Anastasius, and its reason for his elevation is the emperor’s own suggestion and not a prophecy; both readings are recorded and neither is preferred.

The Russian calendar's reading is a third, and it is the Prologue's story told shorter and with two things the Prologue has not. Ephraim was a Syrian, it says, and a military commander under the emperors Anastasius and Justin, a man marked by kindness, piety and compassion for everyone in distress; and the earthquake of 526 that destroyed Antioch it reads as a punishment for the city's drift into the heresies of Nestorius and Eutyches, with the patriarch before him crushed by a falling column. The emperor sent Ephraim to rebuild the city; one of the workmen, a bishop who had left his see to labour unknown, foretold him the patriarchal throne and asked him not to leave off works of mercy and to fight the heretics hard; and in 527 he was elected.

So this account has the prophecy of the Prologue and the Greek page's two emperors at once, and it dates the election to 527 where the Greek page gives 526. Of the stylite near Hierapolis it says more than either: the man would not be persuaded, and tried to frighten the patriarch by proposing that a great fire be lit and both of them walk into it, and then did not dare to enter it himself, so that Ephraim prayed and laid his omophorion in the fire instead, and after three hours the wood was burnt and the omophorion came out unharmed. It closes with his repose in 545 and with what he wrote: that in the Person of the Lord two natures are joined, the divine and the human.
