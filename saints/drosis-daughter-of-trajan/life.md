# Drosis, daughter of Trajan

In the reign of Trajan five women lived apart in a hermitage, keeping all the commandments of God — for which reason, the life says, they were called canonicae — and it was their charge to gather up the bodies of the saints, anoint them with myrrh, wrap them in clean linen and carry them back to the place where they lived. Drosis, the emperor's daughter, heard of it and came to them secretly, while the guards of the imperial bedchambers slept, bringing a costly garment and asking to go with them to take up the body of a saint.

Adrian, to whom she was betrothed and who was one of the emperor's counsellors, had soldiers set to watch over the bodies of the dead Christians, to find out who was carrying them off. The watchmen took the five women and Drosis with them, and brought them before the emperor when day came. Seeing his daughter among them he was appalled, and had her kept under guard in the hope that she would repent of what she had done; the five canonicae he ordered thrown into a vessel of molten bronze.

Of that bronze, mixed with earth, the bottoms were made for the great bronze basins of the public baths the emperor was then building again for the pleasure of the idolaters. When the day of the festival was proclaimed and crowds came running from every side, the first man to approach the door of the bath fell down dead, and so did the rest, and no one could get inside. The emperor called the servants of the idols and asked whether the Christians had worked some spell, and they answered: no, emperor, it is the bronze vessels made with the women's bodies that have done this.

So he had the vessels melted down and new idols made from them in the likeness of the five virgins, to dishonour them, and set up in the bath. Then he saw in a dream five clean lambs grazing in a garden, and a dreadful shepherd said to him: behold, those whom thou didst think to set up naked in the bath, to the dishonour of the Good and Merciful Shepherd, He has taken from thee and placed in this beautiful place, whither thy daughter Drosis shall come also. The emperor woke in a rage and had a great furnace lit, and posted a notice: men of Galilee, who worship the Crucified, spare yourselves further torments and us further labour, and let each of you throw himself into the furnace that is made ready.

Drosis heard of the order and longed to follow the five. Lifting up her eyes she prayed that if it were God's will that she be saved and escape the mad religion of her father, He would help her out of the bridal chamber of the unbelieving Adrian and take her where the five virgins were who had led her to His fear. Then she changed out of her royal clothes and slipped out while the guards slept, to throw herself into the furnace.

On the road she thought: how shall I go to God without a wedding garment, since I have not received baptism and am still unclean? and she prayed to the King of kings, saying that she had left her kingdom for love of Him, to be set as a doorkeeper of His; and she took out the myrrh she had brought with her, anointed herself, went down into a lake and baptised herself, saying, The servant of God, Drosis, is baptised in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, Amen. She kept herself after that for seven days and was fed with the food of angels; some lovers of Christ who stayed near her learned her story; and on the eighth day, having prayed God to show her what she should do, she fell asleep in the Lord.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, with five virgins beside her whom it does not name, and its account of her end is not a martyrdom. saint.gr makes her the daughter of the emperor Trajan (98–117), who tortured and put to death many Christians and left their bodies unburied; but five «κανονικαί», that is five virgins, used to go out secretly by night and bury the Christians' relics at their own place of asceticism. Drosis helped in that work, having taken the pattern of it from the life and the offering of the monastics. Her betrothed, though, watched her and seized her at the moment of a burial.

When the saint and the five nuns were brought before Trajan, the emperor in his agitation ordered the five nuns put to death at once and his daughter closely guarded. One night she managed to escape from the palace and found refuge in the house of some Christians; and there, eight days later, while she was praying, she fell asleep in peace. The page prints three couplets over the day — one on the whole company, one on Drosis, who left every tie of her father's house and found Christ the longed-for Bridegroom, and one on the κανονικαί, that though they were melted down, bright virgins, they now dwell in the heavens with the angels.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her on the same menologion day and gives her end, which neither of the other calendars did. She was the daughter of the emperor Trajan. She was caught with five other women gathering by night the bodies of those who had suffered for Christ, and for that her father dealt harshly with her. Those five women were severely tortured and thrown at the last into molten copper, where they gave up their souls to their Lord; the Prologue names none of them, as the Greek calendar names none.

Drosis was kept under strict imperial guard. But she escaped out of the palace and baptised herself in a river, and after eight days she gave up her soul to God. The entry gives no year of any kind, so the reign of her father is still the only date this folder holds.
