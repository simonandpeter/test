# Babylas, Bishop of Antioch

Babylas — this great and wonderful man, if it is possible to call him a man, Chrysostom says of him — was bishop of Antioch; the Russian calendar puts his death under Decius (249–251), the Greek and Serbian under Numerian. The emperor, staying at Antioch, held a great festival to the pagan gods — having first, the Prologue says, slain with his own hand the little son of a barbarian king given him as a pledge of peace, and offered him to the idols — and while the holy and God-fearing bishop was celebrating the Divine Liturgy in the church, praying for his flock and teaching it to bear every trial for the faith of Christ bravely, the emperor, wanting to see the mysteries, resolved to enter and defile the Lord’s holy place by his visit. Told of it, Babylas broke off the service, went out before the church and told the emperor that as an idolater he could not enter the holy temple where the one true God is glorified — whom else in the world would he fear, Chrysostom asks, who dealt so with an emperor? He taught emperors not to stretch their power beyond the measure God gave, and showed priests how to use theirs. Shamed, the emperor went back and planned revenge; the next day he summoned Babylas, reproached him and bade him sacrifice, and when he refused unshakeably had him chained and thrown into prison, and tortured too three children — Urban of twelve, Prilidian of nine and Epolonius of seven, whose spiritual father and teacher Babylas was, sons of an honourable Christian woman, Christodula, who suffered for Christ herself. Each child was given as many blows as he had years, then imprisoned, and at last all three beheaded, Babylas in chains watching and heartening them; then he laid his own head under the sword, and was buried in his chains, as he had asked, in one tomb with the three wonderful boys, about 250. Their wonderworking relics remained, the Prologue says, a lasting witness of their heroism in the faith; their Russian, Greek and Romanian hymns are recorded with the day.
