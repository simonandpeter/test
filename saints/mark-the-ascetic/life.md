# Mark the Ascetic

This saint was a lover of labour in everything, and gave himself at the same time to the searching of the Holy Scriptures, reaching the highest perfection in the hermit's life and in virtue. The writings he left are the witness of it — doxologia points the reader to the first volume of the Romanian Philokalia — full of every kind of teaching and profit; and the working of wonders was entrusted to him by Christ the Saviour.

Of those wonders one must be told here. The saint was once in the yard, examining himself, when a wild hyena came to him bringing her cub, which was blind, and in a humble manner begged him to have mercy on her and heal the cub's blindness. The saint spat upon the sick eyes and prayed, and gave the cub its sight. A few days later the hyena brought him a large ram's skin in thanks; but he would not take it until she promised that she would attack the sheep of the poor no more.

If the saint was so merciful towards the nature of the beasts, the life asks, how merciful must he have been towards men, whom the sharing of one nature itself obliges to be full of compassion? So great was his purity that the priest of the monastery said on oath that he had never once given the Holy Mysteries to the monk Mark: whenever he came to communicate he was communicated by an angel, of whom only the hand was seen, from the elbow, holding the spoon.

He had withdrawn from all the cares and the noise of the world at the age of forty, and having passed sixty years in the hermit's life he went over to the Lord. He was small of stature, thin-bearded, and his head was lit from within by the grace of the Holy Spirit. doxologia gives no year for his birth or his death, and names no country.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr gives him a life and two wonders. It says he was an Athenian who lived in the fourth century; that having taken a considerable education he left Athens and went to Antioch, where he was a disciple of [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom); that afterwards he was tonsured a monk and gave himself to the study of the divine Scriptures and reached the highest degree of virtue. Once, while he was keeping silence, a hyena came up to him whose cub was blind, in a humble posture as though begging him to have pity on the little one's blindness; and the venerable one, understanding, spat on the animal's eyes and it received its sight. Days later the hyena came back to him carrying the skin of a great ram in return for the healing, and he would not take it until the beast promised to stop harming the sheep of the poor. He had reached such a height, the page says, that the presbyter of the monastery declared he had never once given him Holy Communion, but that an angel's hand supplied it to him; and he fell asleep in peace at a great age.

Of his works, saint.gr says, only some discourses, letters and books of counsel survive, and their practical use is such that it was said of their author «Πάντα πώλησαν καὶ Μᾶρκον ἀγόρασαν» — sell everything and buy Mark. It prints four of his precepts: that it is better for men to harm you than for demons to have power over you; that the simple but humble-minded man is wiser than the wise; that whoever remembers his former faults guards himself against those to come; and that if you have not suffered afflictions you should not think you have virtue, for nothing that grows up in comfort is anything at all. The same Greek day carries a second page, for a Mark the Athenian, which says the information about him is confused and that he may perhaps be this same man; nothing here is decided from that, and the hymns the site prints under both names — which name Athens and the abba Serapion — are not taken into this folder, because the life this page tells knows nothing of either.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović calls him an ascetic and a wonderworker and gives his arithmetic. He was tonsured by his own teacher [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom) in the fortieth year of his life, and after that passed sixty years more in the desert of Nitria in fasting, in prayer and in the writing of useful books. He knew the whole of Holy Scripture by heart. He was very merciful, and wept over the misery of every creature of God's.

Once, weeping, he prayed to God for the blind whelp of a hyena, and the whelp received its sight; out of gratitude the mother hyena brought him a ram's skin, and the saint forbade her to kill the sheep of poor men thereafter. He received Communion from the hands of angels. His homilies on the spiritual law, on repentance, on sobriety and the rest belong to the first rank of the Church's literature, and the great patriarch [Photius](/saints/photius-of-constantinople) himself praised them.
