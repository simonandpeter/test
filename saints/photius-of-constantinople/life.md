# Photius of Constantinople

Photius lived in the days of the emperors Michael, Basil I the Macedonian and Leo VI the Wise. He came into the world in the city of Constantinople, in the year 810 — others say 820 — in a believing family that had fought for the honour and veneration of the holy icons. His father was Saint Sergius, brother of the empress Theodora and of the patriarch Tarasius, and his mother was Irene; they were persecuted by the iconoclast emperor Theophilus, his father's property was taken and he was sent into exile with his wife and children for the honouring of the icons, and died in banishment as a confessor.

He was first a pupil, spending hour after hour in the library at his studies; then a teacher of philosophy in the University of the Magnaura, the first university of medieval Europe; then the holder of important offices in the state; and at the last he was enthroned on the patriarchal throne of Constantinople, being also a practiser of almsgiving and of the love of men. Whoever was around him and wherever he found himself, and taking no account of the hardships of his two exiles, he fought everywhere for the true Orthodox faith, for the apostolic tradition and for the ancestral faith, which is also the content of the patristic teaching. He fought against the Manichaeans, the iconoclasts and other heretics, and many of them he restored to the Orthodox Church.

In the year 858, when by the emperor's command the patriarch Ignatius was put out of the patriarchal throne, they came to Photius, who was greatly praised for his worthiness and his faithfulness; and from that beginning came the long contest, and the exile in which he was held by the shores of the Bosphorus, subject to many wants and hardships.

With the years things changed in Byzantium. Basil I, wishing to make good the injustice he had done Photius, called him back from exile; the two patriarchs, Ignatius and Photius, were reconciled; and when the aged Ignatius died on 23 October 878 Photius was restored to the patriarchal throne of Constantinople, and held it until 886, when he was forced to withdraw a second time, Basil I being succeeded on the throne by his son Leo VI the Wise.

He fell asleep in the Lord in the year 891 while he was in exile at the Monastery of the Armenians, leaving us as a testament his spiritual teachings and his glorious sufferings in defence of Orthodoxy against the contrary devices of those who blasphemed the right faith. A small portion of his relics is at the monastery of Dionysiou on Mount Athos. His theological work, the page says, justifies the struggles of the Church and attests the Orthodox faith and the conscience of the Church; and in that conscience Photius has been seen as a defender of Orthodoxy and a chief exponent of the Church of the ninth century.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and its page adds his family and the day of his consecration. His parents were St Sergius, whom it keeps on 13 May, and Irene, of a godly and distinguished house that fought for the honour and veneration of the holy icons; Sergius was brother of the patriarch Tarasius, was paraded bound by the neck through the streets of Constantinople under the iconoclast emperor Theophilus, lost his property and was exiled with his wife and children to a waterless place, where he died of the hardships as a confessor. Photius, the page says, was born at Constantinople in 810 — by others in 820 — first distinguished himself in the highest civil offices, and came to the patriarchal throne in 858 when Ignatius was violently removed from it by the emperor's order; he was tonsured monk first, and consecrated bishop on Christmas day of 858 by Gregory Asbestas of Syracuse, Basil of Gortyna and Eulampius of Apamea. Fragments of his relics, it notes, are at the monasteries of Dionysiou and Xenophontos on the Holy Mountain and at Kykkos in Cyprus. Its couplet has him say, dying, that he is not troubled at his end, having been made ready for it.

The Serbian church keeps him on the Julian 6 February, and Bishop Nikolai's Ohrid Prologue gives him five short sentences: a great lighthouse of the Church, a kinsman of the emperors and grandson of the glorious patriarch [Tarasius](/saints/tarasius-of-constantinople), an irresistible defender of the Church against the papal lust for power and the other Roman corruptions of the faith. In six days, it says, he passed through every rank from layman to patriarch; he became patriarch at Christmas 857 and made his end in the Lord in 891.

Two of those are not what the other sources say. Grandson of Tarasius, where the Romanian and Greek lives both make him Tarasius's nephew — his father Sergius being Tarasius's brother — and Christmas 857, where both of them put his coming to the throne in 858, the Greek page naming Christmas day of 858 and the three bishops who consecrated him. Neither difference is settled here, because none of the three says how it arrived at its reading. The year of his repose, 891, all three agree on.
