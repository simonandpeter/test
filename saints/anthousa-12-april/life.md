# Anthousa (12 April)

doxologia.ro's calendar names her and gives no life. The entry for 12 April reads «Sfânta Cuvioasă Antuza» — Anthousa, with the rank the Romanian calendar gives to those who lived the monastic life — and the page behind that line carries no Viață tab and no Tropar tab, only the day's Epistle and Gospel, icons and articles. A name, a rank and a day is the whole of what this source has.

No century is given, no place, no parentage and no manner of death; nothing here says whether she was a martyr, an abbess or a solitary. This folder records what the Romanian calendar keeps on 12 April and stops there, and nothing further should be read into the bare name.

**The Russian calendar keeps her on the same menologion day and tells, where the Romanian gave a name, the whole of who she was.** Its line is «Прп. Анфусы девы (801).» and its life makes her the daughter of the iconoclast emperor Constantine Copronymus, whose reign it dates 741 to 775, by his first wife, a Khazar. She and her brother, the future emperor Leo the Khazar, were twins and were born on 25 January 750. The empress suffered greatly in the birth, and Constantine called the venerable abbess [Anthousa](/saints/anthousa-the-venerable-27-july) out of the confinement he had put her in and asked her prayers; that Anthousa foretold the twins and what would become of them, and the daughter born as she had said was named after her.

When she grew up her father pressed her to marry, but she had wanted the monastic life from her youth and would not be persuaded; and after his death she spent the whole of her own property on the poor and on orphans. [Irene](/saints/irene-wife-of-leo-iv), the pious empress and wife of her brother Leo, held her in love and respect and invited her to reign jointly with her, which she would not have; when she was at the palace she wore the dress of an emperor's daughter and a hair shirt under it.

She received the tonsure from the patriarch [Tarasius](/saints/tarasius-of-constantinople), whose years the page gives as 784 to 806, and founded the Homonoia monastery at Constantinople, which became known for the strictness of its rule. She made herself the example of humility there, doing the heaviest work, cleaning the church and carrying water, and never sitting at table at the meal but waiting on the sisters; and she watched strictly that nobody left the house without real need. She lived fifty-two years and reposed peacefully in 801, which is the year the day's line prints and the folder's first date of any kind.
