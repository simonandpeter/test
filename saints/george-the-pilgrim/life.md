# George the Pilgrim

From his earliest years George ate little, kept the fasts and loved to be alone, and his great joy was to pray in the village church even when there was no service, most often at night; by day, out with the cattle, he would withdraw to hidden places and make many prostrations, his mind on the things of God. Once he had learned to read he prayed with the Psalter, until he knew the whole of it by heart.

At twenty-four he was joined in marriage to a believing young woman named Pelaghia, and God blessed them with five children. Neither as husband nor as father did he let his spiritual habits go, and though he lived in poverty himself he gave alms to the poor at all times. When those close to him reproached him for it he was not troubled, but told everyone with confidence and peace: do not be troubled, God takes care to feed us; our duty is to pray without ceasing and to do His will.

After fourteen years of marriage, in 1883, with his wife's consent he set out for Jerusalem as a pilgrim, with several peasants of his village. He walked as far as Constanța and went on by ship. He stayed forty days, going three times a day to the Holy Sepulchre for the Liturgy and the other services, and then to Bethlehem, Jericho, the Jordan, Nazareth and Tabor. At the cave of Saint Xenophon a hermit foretold that he would never become a monk but would live going from place to place, in want, in poverty and in unceasing prayer.

Before that life began he spent forty days of hard fasting in the desert of Upper Egypt, beset by the devil with apparitions of wild beasts and venomous serpents, and with hunger, thirst, heat and mosquitoes. When the enemy knocked the cap from his head again and again to provoke him to anger, he left it on the ground and promised God that he would go bare-headed until death; when his boots were thrown away he asked for the gift of going barefoot all his life, summer and winter. He kept both promises.

He went on pilgrimage to Mount Athos as well and came home after three years. He did not stay long in his village, but began to travel as in apostolic times, a stranger and a pilgrim within the borders of the country, going from church to church through the parts of Sibiu, Făgăraș and Brașov, praying almost the whole night, walking unhurried, barefoot and bare-headed. Nearly every year he made a pilgrimage to Jerusalem, leading companies of pilgrims.

In 1895 he crossed the Carpathians and settled at Piatra Neamț, where he was given a little room in the bell tower of the princely church of Saint John, founded by Stephen the Great, and there he remained to the end of his life. He slept at most three hours in the tower, then prayed in the church and made hundreds of prostrations; by day he walked the streets of the town whispering psalms, barefoot and bare-headed, and did not return until he had said the whole Psalter through.

He would buy a sack of bread from a bakery and share it out among the poor who gathered round the tower, and he gave away whatever money had been put into his hands on the road. Then, the bodily alms finished, he began the alms of the soul, speaking with each of those burdened by the troubles of life, encouraging him, counselling him and praying for his request. He spoke with people only of God and the spiritual life, and when he did not speak he preached by his silence, carrying always the Gospel or the Psalter in his arms.

The life records his gifts. A train would not leave the station until the conductor who had put him off for having no ticket was sent to fetch him back. At Târgu Neamț a young Jewish woman in mortal danger in childbirth was delivered when he opened her door and cried out, open, with God and the Mother of God; she and her child and all her kin were baptised. To Ioanichie Moroi, abbot of Sihăstria, he foretold more than twenty-five years beforehand the troubles that would come upon him and upon his community.

He knew the day of his death beforehand and said so in words that read as prophecy: I shall die when the peoples are troubled, and at my death there will be a feast and the bells will be rung throughout the land. In 1916, on the feast of the Dormition, his body was found asleep, just as the country was entering the war and the bell-ringer of Saint John's was climbing to ring. He was buried in the town cemetery of Piatra Neamț in the same torn sheepskin coat, barefoot, bare-headed, with the staff of his wandering.

Eighteen years later a disciple who meant to move his relics to Râșca came with a cart to Piatra Neamț, took them up and set out; but the cart did not reach Râșca. It came to the monastery of Văratec, in whose crypt the relics have lain ever since. The Holy Synod of the Romanian Orthodox Church numbered him among the saints, with his commemoration on 17 August.

The Greek calendar keeps him too, and its page tells the story of his pilgrimages with dates. saint.gr calls the faithful lay Christian George Lazar the model of the true Romanian pilgrim, and says his virtuous life altogether makes him a unique phenomenon in the spiritual life of the Church in the last hundred years. Old George Lazar, as he is still called, was born in the community of Sugag in the province of Alba in 1846. At twenty-four his parents married him and left him heir of their property; he lived with his wife about twenty years and was blessed with five children, and lived a holy Christian life, conscientious in his work, with prayer, fasting and almsgiving, his one occupation being the acquiring of the virtues.

In 1884 he went to venerate the All-holy Tomb of the Lord and stayed a year in the monasteries of the Jordan desert and of Sinai; afterwards he trained himself a year and a half on Athos and returned to his own country. He lived many years more with his family and settled his children, and in 1890 departed as a pilgrim for the monasteries of Moldavia. Having venerated all the holy places he settled for good in the town of Piatra in the district of Neamț, where he lived like a true hermit.

saint.gr puts his death on the day on which the Dormition of the Theotokos is celebrated: at the hour when the bell-ringer of the princely church of Saint John at Piatra-Neamț was climbing the tower to ring the bells for the general mobilisation, old George Lazar lay in his cell with the psalter beside him, and at that moment the most reverent pilgrim George gave up his soul into the hands of Jesus Christ. The Greek calendar keeps his memory two days later, on 17 August, which is the day this folder's Greek row records; the page prints no hymn of its own to him.
