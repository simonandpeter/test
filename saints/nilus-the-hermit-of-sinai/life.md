# Nilus the Hermit of Sinai

Doxologia's life gives him as a man of Constantinople, well brought up, who mastered the whole of book learning and became a chosen orator in words. Come to full age he was joined to an honourable wife and had two children by her, a son and a daughter; and for the goodness of his family and the greatness of his wisdom he was chosen prefect of the city in the reign of the emperor Maurice, living in honour and in purity, and pleasing God.

Then, reckoning up the vanity of this world that abides in wickedness — nothing in it steady, nothing right and everlasting, but all things overturned, full of injustice and passing away — he set himself to seek the eternal life, where the gladness is true and unchanging. He counselled his wife to agree to that good resolve, and she did. They gave away the whole of their property to the poor and gave freedom to their servants.

Afterwards they divided their children between them: the wife took her daughter, and he took his son, Theodulus by name, and they went out of Constantinople, leaving the world and everything in it. His wife went with her daughter to Egypt, entered a convent of virgins there, received the monastic life and spent her days well in it, serving the Lord. Nilus went with Theodulus to Mount Sinai, and settling in the desert among the holy fathers he received the habit of the monks, fasting and labouring after their manner.

As they were living so, barbarians fell upon that desert, coming without warning like wild beasts; they killed many of the holy fathers and carried off others captive, and among these they took Theodulus. His father lamented bitterly for him, and the more when he heard that the barbarians meant to slaughter him as a sacrifice, as it is written in the discourse set down about him. But God kept Theodulus alive and whole: the bishop of the city of Elusa ransomed him from the barbarians and made him a cleric for the service of the Church.

Learning of it, Nilus went alone to Elusa and was received with honour by the bishop, who ordained him priest although he did not wish it, and begged him to stay. He would not, wanting to return to Sinai; so the bishop gave him his son and sent them away in peace. Coming back with Theodulus to his first place in Sinai, he lived many years, composed many discourses of the desert full of wisdom and profit, and then, having written various letters, passed to the Lord. The page gives no year.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 12 November, and tells his life through another book, quoting S. Eustratiades's hagiology at length. He was distinguished in learning and in office, it says there, eparch of Constantinople and most devout; married, he had two children, a son and a daughter; but having an unquenchable zeal for the monastic life he persuaded his wife to leave Constantinople with him, and they came to Alexandria and separated there, the wife taking the little daughter and Nilus his son Theodoulos, with whom he went off to Mount Sinai to live as an ascetic. There barbarians fell on them and carried off, among others, his son, for whom he wept and lamented bitterly — the page refers to the thirty-eight abbas of Sinai on 14 January. In that desert rest he gave himself to the writing of ascetic works, which witness to his wisdom and to his love of ascesis; and living in fastings and prayers he fell asleep in peace. His holy bones were brought back by the emperor Justin, who reigned 518 to 527, and laid in the church of the apostle Paul at the Orphanage, beneath the altar. The century the page cannot settle: it names the reign of Theodosius the Great, prefers Theodosius II by conjecture, and says that the synaxarion's Maurice is not right.

The Russian calendar's life is the fullest account of him read here, and it begins in the capital. He was a native of Constantinople and a disciple of [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom), and while still a young man, well educated, he was appointed prefect of the city. He was married by then and had two children, and the glitter of court life weighed on both husband and wife. Under Chrysostom's influence they agreed to part and give themselves to the monastic life: his wife went with their daughter to a women's monastery in Egypt, and he went with their son [Theodulus](/saints/theodulus-son-of-nilus) to Sinai.

There the two of them dug a cave with their own hands, and that cave was his dwelling for forty years. By fasting, prayer and labour he came to a high spiritual stature, and people of every rank and station began to come to him, from the emperor down to the farmer, and each found counsel and consolation. In his solitude he wrote a great deal. His letters are known, among them angry rebukes of the emperor who exiled his teacher, and his ascetic writings are widely known: perfect in form, deeply orthodox, full of sincere feeling and clear thought.

The desert cost him much. Saracens carried off his son and set him aside for sacrifice to their gods; at the father's prayers God saved him, and he found the young man with the bishop of Emesa, who had bought him back from the barbarians. That same bishop ordained the two hermits presbyters, and after the ordination they returned to Sinai and lived there together until the father's death. The day page carries a troparion and two kontakia to him, and they are now in this folder with the English typed here.

The Ohrid Prologue begins him in office rather than in the desert. He was first prefect in the capital, in Constantinople; a married man with one son and one daughter. Seeing the vicious life of the capital he agreed with his wife that they should withdraw from the world, and so they did: the wife went with the daughter to a women's monastery in Egypt, and he went with his son [Theodulus](/saints/theodulus-son-of-nilus) to Mount Sinai.

A full sixty years he struggled on Sinai, it says, and wrote beautiful books on the spiritual life. It closes with one of his own sayings: the passions of the body take their origin from the needs of the body, and against them abstinence is needed; the passions of the soul are born of the needs of the soul, and against them prayer is needed.

And it gives a year and an age where this folder has only a century: he reposed in peace about the year 450, in the eightieth year of his earthly life. The century this folder records comes from two calendar lines that print nothing but «(V)», and about 450 falls inside it, so the interval is left as it stands and the book's own figures are recorded here rather than used to close it.
