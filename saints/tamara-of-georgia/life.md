# Tamara of Georgia

Her name, the Romanian page begins, is Hebrew and means "palm tree". Tamara was born in 1166, the daughter of King George III and of Queen Burdukhan of Georgia, and after her father's death she was crowned queen at eighteen. The courtiers pressed her again and again to marry, for the sake of the dynasty; but the marriage to Yuri, son of Prince Andrei Bogolyubsky of Vladimir-Suzdal, brought her no happiness, for the new king was unsteady in character and without faith, and in the end he was driven out to Constantinople.

In 1195, when the kingdom of Georgia was invaded by a Turkish army, she prayed without ceasing on one of the hills of the Metekhi monastery until word came that the Orthodox Georgian army had beaten the Muslim army; and within a few years, the page says, the Georgian army came to be known as the protector of the Transcaucasus, because of Tamara's faith and the faith of her people.

Many years later one of the sultans of the Ottoman court sent her a proposal that she convert to Islam. She refused, answering that his proposal reckoned only with the wealth and the size of the Ottoman army and not with the divine judgement; that she trusted not in the Georgian army but in the right hand of Almighty God and in the endless help of the Holy Cross, which he did not acknowledge; and that God's will, and God's judgement, and not his, would be done. Soon after, the Georgian army beat the Turks at Basini.

She endowed monasteries not only in Georgia but in Palestine, Cyprus, Greece, the Holy Mountain, Bulgaria, Macedonia, Romania and Constantinople, and many monasteries were built in Georgia itself; she abolished the death sentence and every form of bodily torture. Fasting, long prayers, prostrations and a stone bed on which she slept a few hours a night wore her health down, and she would not speak of it for a long while; when the pain made her accept physicians they could give no exact diagnosis. Where she was buried is still unknown: some say her grave is at Gelati, others that her relics are in the monastery of the Holy Cross in Jerusalem. The page gives no year for her death.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, and saint.gr's notice gives the reign and the end that the Romanian life did not. Tamara the Great, queen of Georgia, was of the ancient Georgian dynasty of the Bagratids, it says, and in 1178 she reigned together with her father George III; her reign is known in Georgian history as the Golden Age. She was marked by her great piety and by her missionary work, and continuing what her grandfather had begun she spread Christianity through the whole of Georgia and raised up churches and monasteries.

In 1204 Rukn ad-Din, governor of the sultanate of Rum, sent her an order that Georgia must deny its faith in Christ and take up Islam. She refused, and in a battle near Basiani the Georgian army defeated the Muslims. Her wise and just reign won her the love of her people. She passed the last years of her life in the cave monastery of Vardzia, where her cell was joined to the church by a window through which she could pray to God during the services, and she fell asleep in peace in 1213 and was numbered with the saints. The page adds that her memory is kept also on the Sunday of the Myrrhbearers, which moves with Pascha and is not a day this corpus can hold.

The Russian church calendar keeps her on the same day and gives her two lives. The shorter, life6862, puts her reign at 1184 to 1213, calls her the daughter of George III by the beautiful Бурдухан, and says that Christianity had already spread through the various parts of Georgia in her mother's time; the Georgian tales praise her meekness, her love of peace, her wisdom, her religion and her beauty, and some of the tribes of Georgia honour her as a healer of sicknesses. She cared for the poor, for widows and for orphans, she was a patron of poets and writers, she built many churches and the sumptuous palace of Vardzia, and the Church numbered her among the saints for those cares and gifts. She called a council of the church which put right the disorders of church life and removed unworthy hierarchs.

The longer, life968, puts her birth about 1165 — where the Romanian life this folder was written from gives 1166 — says she came of the old Georgian dynasty of the Bagratids and was her father's co-ruler from 1178, and calls her reign the golden age of Georgian history. In 1204 the ruler of the Seljuk sultanate of Rum demanded that Georgia renounce Christianity and accept Islam; she refused, and in the battle near Basiani the Georgian army broke the coalition of the Muslim states. She spent her last years in the cave monastery of Vardzia, where she had a cell communicating by a small window with the church so that she could send up her prayers during the services, and she died in peace in 1213.
