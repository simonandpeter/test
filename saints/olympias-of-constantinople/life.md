# Olympias of Constantinople

Olympias was called after her mother's name. She was born in Constantinople of famous and high-born parents: her father was one of the most honoured senators, Anysius Secundus, and her mother was the daughter of the famous eparch Ablabius, who is remembered in the wonders of Nicholas the hierarch of Christ, for he was first after the emperor in the days of Constantine the Great. That elder Olympias had first been betrothed to Constans, the youngest son of Constantine the Great, and when he was killed before the wedding she was given to Arsaces, king of Armenia, and was soon left a widow; married then to the senator Anysius Secundus, she bore this Olympias, whom her parents betrothed while she was still a child to the son of the eparch Nebridius.

The wedding was put off, and after twelve months the bridegroom died, so that Olympias was left virgin and widow at once. She would not be betrothed to another, though she was now of full age, but wished to spend all the days of her life in virginity and chastity. Her parents died and left her heir to great riches and lands without number, and she committed the whole of it to God and dealt it out with a generous hand to those in need: to churches, monasteries and the dwellers in the desert, to hospitals and poorhouses and hostels for strangers, to orphans and widows, to those in danger of great poverty, to those in prisons and in chains, and to those far off in exile. She herself lived in prayer and fasting, putting the body to death and making it servant to the spirit.

Theodosius the Great then reigned, and he had a kinsman named Elpidius whom he wished her to marry, since she was young and very beautiful; but she would not, and though the emperor sent to her many times, and she heard threats and knew he was moving to anger, she sent him answer that if God had willed her to be married He would not have taken away her first husband; but knowing that a husband would not profit her in this life He had loosed the man from living with her and freed her from the heavy yoke of marriage, laying His own good yoke upon her mind.

The emperor in his anger ordered the eparch of the city to take all her property into his keeping until she should be thirty years old. The eparch, less at the emperor's command than at the urging of Elpidius, pressed and straitened her so that she had authority neither over her goods nor over herself: he would not let her speak with the bishops who were pleasing to God nor go to church, that vexation might drive her to a husband. She only rejoiced the more and gave thanks to God, and wrote to the emperor that he had done her an imperial mercy, and the service of a bishop, in ordering another to manage and guard the heavy burden she had been caring for — and would do her a greater kindness still if he would command his steward to deal it out where she had meant it to go.

What the page tells between that and her end is left here unsummarised. At the last, after a fire in the city, she was brought before a court and questioned in terror, for the eparch was cruel and without humanity; and though nothing was proved against her he judged unjustly, that Olympias should pay much gold for the burning of which she was not guilty. After that she left Constantinople and went to Cyzicus, but her enemies gave her no peace there either, and condemning her to banishment they exiled her to Nicomedia. When [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom) learned of it he wrote her a letter out of his own exile, comforting her in her troubles; and having spent a long time in banishment and borne many hardships, she departed to the Lord.

Her honourable body was still unburied when she appeared in a dream to the bishop of Nicomedia and told him to put it in a wooden coffer and cast it into the sea, that it might be buried on whatever shore the waves carried it to; and the bishop did so. The coffer came ashore at a place called Brochthi, where there was a church of the apostle Thomas, and God made known to the people of that place whose body it was; they found it and laid it in the church of the apostle, and healings of every infirmity were given there.

Many years later barbarians came and burned the church. The coffer burned, but the relics remained whole and unburnt, and the barbarians threw them into the sea; and where they were thrown the water ran with blood, God making known the suffering of His servant. The faithful drew the wonder-working relics out of the sea again, and when Patriarch Sergius heard of it he sent the priest John to bring them with honour to Constantinople. When the priest took them up much blood ran from them, to the wonder of all, that after two hundred years blood should run from dry bones as from a living body. They were carried to the convent of virgins that Olympias herself had built and laid there, and many wonders were done at them, all sicknesses being healed and devils driven out.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 25 July, and calls her the Deaconess. saint.gr says Olympias lived in the years of the patriarchs Nectarius and Saint [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom), about 395, and that her father Akoundos held the rank of count — though by others, it notes, he was called Seleucus. She had great beauty of body, quick wits, education and much wealth. She married the prefect of Constantinople Nevridius, but he died a little while afterwards and she was left a widow very young; the emperor Theodosius tried to persuade her to take a second husband, a certain official named Elpidius, and she, honouring her husband's memory and burning with the longing to serve the Church out of her riches, refused the second marriage.

So she gave herself to the great archbishop of Constantinople John Chrysostom, and full of eagerness she gave his archdiocese thousands of gold coins and estates. Within the Church she held the title of Deaconess, and she founded a monastery as well, near the church of Saint Irene. Later, when Chrysostom was exiled, Olympias fell into deep mourning, and to comfort her the great hierarch sent her a number of letters, of which seventeen survive. She died in exile at Nicomedia, only fifty years old, a little after the death of the holy Chrysostom. Portions of her relics, the Greek page adds, are at the monasteries of Agathonos in Phthiotis and of Ntaou Penteli, and above her notice it sets a distich: «Oλυμπιάς πεσούσα πατρίδος φίλης, Προς την άνω χαίρουσα βαίνει πατρίδα» — fallen away from the homeland she loved, Olympias goes rejoicing towards the homeland above.
