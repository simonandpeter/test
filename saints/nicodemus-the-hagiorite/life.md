# Nicodemus the Hagiorite

Nicodemus was born on Naxos in 1749 to pious and virtuous parents, Antony
and Anastasia Kallivoursi — his mother afterwards a nun of the Chrysostom
monastery of Naxos with the name Agatha; his name in the world was Nicholas,
and from childhood he showed a great virtue and a formidable intelligence.
He learned his first letters on Naxos and went on at the school of the same
town under the archimandrite Chrysanthos, the brother of Kosmas of Aetolia,
and at sixteen to the Greek school at Smyrna, where under famous teachers he
took a higher education and virtue together.

After certain adventures he came in 1775 to the Holy Mountain and was
tonsured at the monastery of Saint Dionysius with the name Nicodemus; the
fathers, seeing his great natural and acquired gifts, made him reader and
secretary of the house, where he was a pattern of service and of works of
virtue, and then he withdrew to a cell and gave himself, ascetically, to the
study and writing of many edifying, theological and hagiological books — the
Synaxaristes, the Eortodromion, the New Ladder, the Unseen Warfare and many
more.

After various adventures in a short life he died of a stroke at sixty, in
the early hours of 14 July 1809, in the cell of the Skourtaioi at Karyes;
his last words were his answer to the disciples who asked whether he was at
rest — I have put Christ within me, and how should I not be at rest? He was
buried at the Lavriote cell of the Skourtaioi at Karyes, leaving an immense
spiritual and written work that is a capital of the people of the Orthodox
Church, which entered him in its calendar, worthily, in 1955. The Greek
calendar keeps him on 14 July, and this entry stands under 20 September,
where the synaxarion’s day list carried him when the corpus was first read.

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 14 iulie and prints a long life of him there. It has him at sixteen sent to Smyrna to the teacher Hierotheos, where besides the sacred books he learned Latin and French and became a skilled reader of old Greek — the thing, it says, that fitted him for the work God had set him, to make the treasures of the Church's tradition known to an oppressed Greek people. Back on Naxos he met the monks Gregory, Niphon and Arsenius, driven off the Holy Mountain over the Saturday memorials and nicknamed kollyvades by their opponents, and through them Macarius, metropolitan of Corinth, who two years after the tonsure came to Athos and charged him with editing the Philokalia for the press.

The Romanian life adds a journey the Greek one does not. Hearing of Paisius of Neamț, who was leading a thousand monks in Moldavia in the bringing down of the mind into the heart, Nicodemus took ship for Moldavia to meet him, and a storm turned him back. In 1782 he withdrew with Arsenius to the bare island of Skyropoula off Euboea, and there, thirty-two years old and without books or notes, wrote the Handbook of Good Counsel on the guarding of the five senses out of his memory alone. At the last, having confessed and communicated, he took in his hands the relics of Macarius of Corinth and of Parthenius Skourtaios and asked them to plead for him, and at dawn on 14 July 1809 he gave up his soul; one of those present cried out that it were better a thousand Christians had died that day than Nicodemus.
