# Pimen (Myasnikov) of Ugresha

Pimen — Peter Dmitrievich Myasnikov in the world — was born on 10 August
1810 in the city of Vologda; his parents Demetrius Afanasievich and
Avdotya Petrovna, devout, honest and comfortably off, were of the merchant
estate. At seven he was set to learn his letters — Vologda had no public
schools then, and the children of the middling sort learned where they
could — with Pelageya Krylova, a dressmaker, one of three unmarried
daughters of an old Zyryan widow who baked pastries, and after two years
of it he and his friend Andryusha still could not read properly, so
Andryusha was apprenticed to a coppersmith and Peter finished with his
godfather, who taught him to read well and fluently, though handwriting
never came to him and his hand stayed small and hard to make out. At ten
or eleven, able to read freely, he took to the large clear Elizabethan
Bible in his parents’ house, and it left ineffaceable traces: he grew cold
to everything temporal, thought more of heaven than of earth, and began to
consider how to enter a monastery. His father was churchwarden of his
parish, and Peter was in church whenever he could be, and went often to
the Holy Spirit monastery, where he came to know an ascetic who lived in a
little cell built for him in the yard of the respected Achabyev sisters
and hardly went anywhere but church, who sustained the boy’s devotion. At
seventeen he got the book The Spiritual Alphabet and, reading it, was
finally confirmed in the thought of renouncing the world; knowing his
father’s stern, unbending nature and his coolness to monasticism he feared
to tell him, but happily two of his younger sisters had asked leave to
enter the Goritsy Trinity convent, which eased the explanation, and the
father, not gladly nor at once, agreed not to hinder him. In June 1832 he
entered the Novoezersk monastery of St Cyril, a novice in the cellarer’s
office, and never forgot what he saw there; after the death of the
archimandrite Theophan on 3 December 1833 he stayed some months more, went
home in June to get his release from the merchant community, and was given
by Ignatius (Brianchaninov), then igumen of the Lopotov monastery, two
letters — to the Optina elder Leonid (Nagolkin) and to the hieromonk
Hilarius the younger, once Brianchaninov’s companion at Svir — on which he
was warmly received at Optina and became cell-attendant to Hilarius, its
sacristan. On Ignatius’s recommendation Hilarius was made igumen of the
Nikolo-Ugreshsky monastery of the Moscow diocese in 1834, and took his
novice with him. He came to Ugresha in his twenty-fourth year looking far
younger — short, sturdy, with lively penetrating eyes that saw and noted
everything, broad dark brows, thick silky dark-chestnut hair and no sign
yet of beard or moustache — in a thick black cassock of the Optina cut and
a sealskin “Solovki” belt that the Ugresha brethren found very odd and
teased him about, calling it a sheepskin and saying no one dressed so in a
state monastery; he took no offence and wore it while it served. Within a
fortnight he asked to be received into the brotherhood, and lived his
first five years in a curtained corner of the superior’s hall. On 26 March
1838 Hilarius tonsured him with the name Pimen for the great ascetic of
monasticism, as if foretelling that his own name would be honoured in the
monasticism of his day and remain in everlasting memory; he spent a whole
week without leaving the Dormition church where he was tonsured, was soon
relieved of the cell-attendant’s post and given his own cell, and to his
old obediences added the church’s. Ordained hierodeacon in February 1839,
he was made acting treasurer the same year, not yet thirty, though older
hieromonks were passed over — Metropolitan Philaret’s choice fell on him —
and less than a year later was ordained hieromonk, on 25 April 1840, and
confirmed treasurer on 26 February 1844. Hilarius cared little for the
monastery’s material needs, thinking of its spiritual order, and all the
burdens of administration lay on Pimen, who grew into them little by
little, preparing unawares for the many-sided work that the great and
populous house, which flourished and grew famous through him, would later
demand. On 16 October 1853, by his efforts and with Philaret’s support, the
monastery was made coenobitic instead of a state house, and Hilarius
having asked to retire, Pimen was made igumen and superior. Of his moral
qualities it can be said that for himself he was neither covetous nor
acquisitive nor fond of things: with tens and hundreds of thousands in
his hands he kept not one rouble for himself and had all his money
entered as the monastery’s; for the monastery he thought and strove
constantly to increase and secure its material welfare, rejoicing when
something was done for it and grieving when it was delayed, yet remembered
his monastic calling first, took every step with great care lest he give
occasion for an unfavourable word about monasticism, and accepted
benefactors’ gifts with great circumspection. Very exacting with the
brethren, he gave with full measure when comfort, reward or help was
needed — five or ten roubles and more to the senior brethren going to
Moscow, and to a hieromonk going after many years on pilgrimage to Kyiv or
home a sum ample for the journey, sometimes several hundred roubles; he
knew how to demand and to punish severely, and loved to comfort and reward
a worthy brother without stint; his pocket was always full of silver, and
he never refused a passer-by, a beggar, a burnt-out peasant, a workman or
a pilgrim, giving each what he judged needful; he paid the taxes or quit-rent
of well-behaved novices, even several tens of roubles — he labours for the
monastery and the brethren, why should the monastery not help him? In the
fasts he ate moderately what the rule allowed and kept from fish in Great
Lent without crushing himself with excessive abstinence — God grant us to
keep exactly what the rule lays down, not to be clever but to fulfil it in
simplicity. He was always zealous for the services, deeply imbued with the
monastic spirit, kept the church typicon strictly and allowed no departures
in the service or its hours, once fixed and never changed; while his health
allowed he came to matins, at three on weekdays and an hour or half an hour
earlier on Sundays and feasts, named who should read the six psalms, chose
the Prologue reading after the sixth ode and stayed to the end, coming to
the late Liturgy rather than the early; very often on weekdays he read the
kathismata or the canon himself, not loudly but so clearly that every word
was heard through the church, reading simply and evenly without raising or
lowering his voice — read simply and distinctly, and do not wag your voice;
the divine word has its own power and does not need us to lend it
expression, which I count self-conceit, as if my reading could make more
impression than the sense of what the Church has appointed. Deeply and
sincerely devout, without idle speculation or doubt of the Church’s and
the Fathers’ teaching, he would say to laymen excusing their laxity: there
is nothing here for us to doubt, the holy Church teaches it, and we cannot
excuse ourselves by our clevernesses; read what the holy Fathers say, who
pleased God and were wiser than many of your learned wiseacres of today,
who only speculate and lead themselves and others into temptation and sin;
none of us will be justified by his deeds, and if you feel you are falling
short, do not justify yourself but humble yourself, own that you have
sinned and live badly, not by God’s word, and run to God’s mercy — do not
say, what does it matter; it matters very much: you knowingly departed
from the Church’s rule, so you are a transgressor and have not kept
Christ’s law, and are liable to what? to nothing less than the fire of
Gehenna; so repent and do not justify yourself, and do not dare say, what
does it matter — that is a double sin; you will not be greater than the
Church or above the saints, my dear sir — and so he spoke very often to
worldly people, not in the least constrained by a person’s rank: if we do
not speak, who will? do not press your convictions unasked, but if you are
asked and given cause to speak a word to their profit, there is nothing to
be shy or delicate about — say the thing as it is, respecting no person;
the Lord will require it of you, that you could have told your brother the
truth and kept him from sin and ruin and did not, out of empty, foolish
worldly complaisance; you cannot serve two masters — serve God and not man,
and if he is angry at you for the truth, what is that to you? it is bad for
him, not for us; we spoke the word of truth and did not flatter. On 24
August 1858, for outstanding services in the building up of the monastery,
Metropolitan Philaret raised him to archimandrite; by his labours five
churches were built and all consecrated by Philaret — the St Nicholas
cathedral in 1843, the church of Mary of Egypt in 1851, the Dormition in
1852, and in 1860 the “Joy of All Who Sorrow” and the skete church of Peter
and Paul; in 1866 came the founding and opening of the monastery’s people’s
school, which earned him the unending gratitude of thousands, and in 1869
he was made dean of the coenobitic monasteries of the Moscow diocese,
fifteen houses of monks and nuns from Kolomna to Borodino and Zosima’s
hermitage. On him the Saviour’s word was fulfilled, if any man serve me,
him will my Father honour: all his life he remembered his monastic calling
and measured every step by it — is this fitting for a monk, or
reprehensible? — kept the vows of chastity, non-possession and obedience
sacredly, loved monasticism, was deeply imbued with its spirit, and wishing
to be a true monk himself strove to plant that spirit not only at Ugresha
but in all the coenobitic monasteries under his deanery. Three
metropolitans of Moscow valued, respected and honoured him: under the
first he grew, took shape, ripened and proved a worthy worker of his time,
bearing the marks of that unbreakable spirit; to the second he stood as a
mature man, a sensible and experienced counsellor, a prop of the church
authority, a keen and watchful eye, a guardian and zealot of good monastic
tradition, of strict morals, spiritual purity and material non-possession;
the third at his accession found in him a ripened ear bowed to the ground
by its own fullness. His righteous death came on 17 August 1880 — the day
the Russian calendar keeps him — and his veneration began at once, crowds
of pilgrims coming to his grave, whose chapel was never empty of prayers,
sighs, griefs and joys; it revived with the monastery’s reopening in 1990,
and the Council of Bishops of the Russian Church, which had earlier
glorified him as a local saint of the Moscow diocese, numbered him among
the saints of the whole church and entered him in the calendar, where he is
kept also in the synaxis of the saints of Moscow.
