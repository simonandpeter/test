# Andronicus (Surikov)

Andronicus was born on 5 October 1885 in the village of Ogryzkovo in the
Volokolamsk district of Moscow province, the son of the peasant Ivan
Surikov, and was named Andrew at baptism. Schooled in the parish school,
in 1902 he entered the Luzhetsky monastery at Mozhaisk, where he laboured
in various obediences, chiefly at the kliros. In 1916 he was called to the
front and served as a private in the 52nd infantry regiment; in 1917 the
military authorities released him for field work, and in 1918 he was
demobilised, returned to the Luzhetsky monastery and was tonsured with the
name Andronicus. In 1920 he was moved to the Simonov monastery in Moscow
and on 20 December ordained hierodeacon; in 1924 he was awarded the double
orarion, and in 1928 was ordained hieromonk in the same monastery. When
the Simonov monastery was closed in 1929 he was moved to the Dormition
church at Krutitsy, where he served until 28 December 1930 — the day more
than three hundred clergy, monks, nuns and Orthodox laity were arrested
in Moscow, and he among them, charged with “active anti-Soviet activity
expressed in the organisation of illegal anti-Soviet brotherhoods and
sisterhoods, help to exiled fellow-thinkers, sermons of a
counter-revolutionary character, anti-Soviet agitation about religious
persecution by the Soviet power and the spreading of all manner of
provocative rumours”. Questioned on 31 December, he said: I have never
belonged and do not belong to any political party; I have no acquaintances
who are members of anti-Soviet parties; I keep up no acquaintances and
lead a closed life, so that I go to no one and no one comes to me. The
OGPU troika sentenced him to three years’ exile in the Northern region,
and he was sent to Pinega in the Arkhangelsk region and worked there
felling timber. At the end of his exile, in 1934, he settled at home in
Ogryzkovo, by then in the Shakhovskaya district of the Moscow region, and
on 27 November 1935 Bishop John (Shirokov) of Volokolamsk appointed him
psalmist of the Nicholas church of Kholmets in that district.

On 24 November 1937 the NKVD questioned the usual witnesses — the chairman
and secretary of the collective farm and the chairman of the rural soviet
— who said that on religious feasts the priest gathered believers for
talks and so undermined labour discipline, since the believers did not go
out to work that day; that at Pascha he had organised the singing of
church songs, Christ is risen, which came to be sung in the street of
Kholmets; that he complained that the Soviet power had strangled the
clergy with taxes so that there was nothing to live on, and told his
parishioners that his one hope was their help and support. On such
testimony a warrant was written on 27 November; the next day he was
arrested, put in the Volokolamsk prison, and questioned the day after.
The inquiry has evidence that you carry on counter-revolutionary agitation
against the Soviet power’s tax policy towards ministers of religion; give
evidence on this question. — It is true that I have more than once talked
among believers of the Soviet power taking large taxes from ministers of
religion; I consider these talks hostile to the Soviet power, but I
carried them on without any purpose. Several more questions carried
further charges, all of which he rejected, and the investigator demanded:
the inquiry has evidence that you discredit the Soviet power and praise
life under tsarism. — I have engaged in no discrediting of the Soviet
power and have not praised life under the autocracy. The same day the
investigator drew up the indictment; he was moved to the Taganka prison
in Moscow, and on 3 December 1937 the NKVD troika gave him ten years in a
corrective labour camp. He was sent to the 19th section of the
Baikal-Amur camp, and arrived while Stalin’s order for mass repressions
was still in force. In March 1938 the cultural-educational assistant, the
labour assistant and the head of the camp’s 188th column, secret informers
all three, sent reports to the plenipotentiary of the third section, and
on 9 March signed a character reference: Surikov holds that the
persecutions against him in 1930, and likewise now, are wholly unlawful,
since he had nothing to do with the government and order of the country
and does not wish to; he behaves secretively in the column and avoids talk
with members of the staff; well shod and dressed, he does not go out to
work, and when he did go out it was under great pressure from the staff,
which prisoner Surikov remained especially displeased with, voicing his
displeasure for all to hear. On 15 March the same men were questioned,
now as witnesses: the prisoners of Surikov’s barrack said they had been
taken for nothing, that honest, wholly innocent people had been made
counter-revolutionaries; Surikov said he had been taken only for being a
clergyman; by their conduct, the witnesses held, they worked on the other
prisoners, who did not go out to work. On 27 March a note on him was drawn
up and confirmed as an indictment without his once having been questioned
— the inquiry, the witnesses, the indictment and the charge were all done
in his absence — and on 31 March 1938 the NKVD troika sentenced him to be
shot. After the sentence he went out to general work with the other
prisoners for almost half a year more. He was shot on 22 September 1938 —
9 September of the old style, the day the Russian calendar keeps him, with
the synaxis of the saints of Moscow — and buried in an unknown common
grave, having learned his sentence, it seems, only just before. The life
is by Igumen Damascene (Orlovsky).
