# Theodore of Sykeon

In the land of Galatia is a village called Sykeon, under the city of Anastasius — Anastasiopolis, twelve stadia off, the first of the cities under Ancyra — and that village was Theodore's country. He was conceived by a wonder, in the days of the emperor Justinian the Great, of a mother named Mary and a father named Cosmas, a servant of the emperor; for on the night she conceived him his mother saw in her sleep a very great and shining star come down from heaven into her womb.

She told the vision to her mother Elpidia and her sister Despina, who lived beside her, and then went to a clairvoyant elder who kept silence in a hermitage some six stadia from the village. He taught her of a life pleasing to God, and said: the child conceived in you will be a great man indeed, not before men only but before God. The shining star, which the wise interpreters of dreams take for imperial glory, does not promise you power upon earth, but the glory of good works and of the gifts God will pour into him; for God wills to sanctify his servants in the womb, before their birth.

Theodosius, the bishop of Anastasiopolis, heard of the vision and judged of it the same way, that the child to be born would be a light to the world. Mary came back from the elder and lived by his counsel in chastity, without a husband, together with her mother and her sister; and when the time of her bearing was full she bore this servant of God, and at his baptism he was named Theodore, which is interpreted the gift of God — the name itself declaring beforehand what he would be to the world.

When the boy was six his mother wished to enrol him early in the army, and made ready a golden belt and costly clothes and the other things that rank required, meaning to go to Constantinople and make her son a soldier of the emperor. But the martyr George appeared to her in a dream and said: why have you settled on such a counsel for your son, woman? Do not labour in vain, for the heavenly king has need of him. She woke weeping, thinking the child would die soon; but the child grew.

The life that lies between is very long, and it is the work of his disciple Eleusius, whom Theodore named George in the monastic life. Eleusius's parents had been long childless and had asked Theodore's prayer; he took their girdles, blessed them, gave them back and foretold the loosing of their barrenness, and this son was born to them. They brought him to Theodore and gave him to God's service; he lived twelve years beside him, saw his unspeakable wonders himself, and wrote them at great length, and the page says it brings only a few of them.

At the last he was with the most holy patriarch Thomas, who before the hour of Vespers parted from the body rejoicing and went to the Lord. After the patriarch's death Theodore returned to his own monastery, and not many years later his blessed end drew near, when he had worked many great and glorious miracles and prophesied things to come.

Twice before his death he saw the great martyr George in a dream: first giving him a traveller's staff and calling him to go with him on a far journey, and then riding, and leading a second horse, and saying — Theodore, mount this horse and come after me. By this he knew that his passing from earthly things was near, and he told his disciples of it with joy, and the time of it too, after the feast of Pascha, which is when it came. As he was ending he saw the holy angels who had come to take him, and smiling with a bright face he gave his holy soul into their hands. His end was in the third year of the reign of Heraclius; the page gives no numbered year, and none is set down here.
