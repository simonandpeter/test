# Theodore of Sykeon

In the land of Galatia is a village called Sykeon, under the city of Anastasius — Anastasiopolis, twelve stadia off, the first of the cities under Ancyra — and that village was Theodore's country. He was conceived by a wonder, in the days of the emperor Justinian the Great, of a mother named Mary and a father named Cosmas, a servant of the emperor; for on the night she conceived him his mother saw in her sleep a very great and shining star come down from heaven into her womb.

She told the vision to her mother Elpidia and her sister Despina, who lived beside her, and then went to a clairvoyant elder who kept silence in a hermitage some six stadia from the village. He taught her of a life pleasing to God, and said: the child conceived in you will be a great man indeed, not before men only but before God. The shining star, which the wise interpreters of dreams take for imperial glory, does not promise you power upon earth, but the glory of good works and of the gifts God will pour into him; for God wills to sanctify his servants in the womb, before their birth.

Theodosius, the bishop of Anastasiopolis, heard of the vision and judged of it the same way, that the child to be born would be a light to the world. Mary came back from the elder and lived by his counsel in chastity, without a husband, together with her mother and her sister; and when the time of her bearing was full she bore this servant of God, and at his baptism he was named Theodore, which is interpreted the gift of God — the name itself declaring beforehand what he would be to the world.

When the boy was six his mother wished to enrol him early in the army, and made ready a golden belt and costly clothes and the other things that rank required, meaning to go to Constantinople and make her son a soldier of the emperor. But the martyr George appeared to her in a dream and said: why have you settled on such a counsel for your son, woman? Do not labour in vain, for the heavenly king has need of him. She woke weeping, thinking the child would die soon; but the child grew.

The life that lies between is very long, and it is the work of his disciple Eleusius, whom Theodore named George in the monastic life. Eleusius's parents had been long childless and had asked Theodore's prayer; he took their girdles, blessed them, gave them back and foretold the loosing of their barrenness, and this son was born to them. They brought him to Theodore and gave him to God's service; he lived twelve years beside him, saw his unspeakable wonders himself, and wrote them at great length, and the page says it brings only a few of them.

At the last he was with the most holy patriarch Thomas, who before the hour of Vespers parted from the body rejoicing and went to the Lord. After the patriarch's death Theodore returned to his own monastery, and not many years later his blessed end drew near, when he had worked many great and glorious miracles and prophesied things to come.

Twice before his death he saw the great martyr George in a dream: first giving him a traveller's staff and calling him to go with him on a far journey, and then riding, and leading a second horse, and saying — Theodore, mount this horse and come after me. By this he knew that his passing from earthly things was near, and he told his disciples of it with joy, and the time of it too, after the feast of Pascha, which is when it came. As he was ending he saw the holy angels who had come to take him, and smiling with a bright face he gave his holy soul into their hands. His end was in the third year of the reign of Heraclius; the page gives no numbered year, and none is set down here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr's account is long where the Romanian is short. Theodore was born in the village of Sykea, or Sykeon, of Anastasioupolis, the first city of the province of the Ancyrans, the son of Maria — whom the page calls a harlot — and of Cosmas, a courier of the emperor Justinian. His birth of fornication, it says, did not hinder God from making him a most honourable hierarch and enriching him with strange signs and wonders. At school he got on well at his learning, and at ten he showed an inclination to the monastic life; and one night, when he was twelve, [George the Trophy-bearer](/saints/george-the-trophy-bearer) appeared to him and woke him and said, «Σήκω, Θεόδωρε, έφθασε ο όρθρος, πάμε να προσευχηθούμε» — rise, Theodore, matins has come, let us go and pray. Such was his reverence for George that every noon, leaving school, he climbed the neighbouring rocky hill where George's shrine was, the saint himself leading him in the shape of a young man.

He took the monastic way young, with the blessing of Theodosius, bishop of Anastasioupolis, and a little later was ordained deacon and priest; he went at once to the Holy Places and received the monastic habit at the monastery of the Most Holy Mother of God of Choziba. Then he came back to his own country and settled for good in the chapel of St George, building himself up with fasts and sleeping on the ground, with vigils and psalmody, and receiving from God a river of gifts against unclean spirits and against sicknesses of every kind. His mother, being carnally minded, left him, took her share of the property and married David, a man of the imperial guard at Ancyra; but her aunt Despoinia, her mother Elpidia and his own sister Blatta would not be parted from him, and watched his virtuous life and tried to imitate it as far as they could.

After the death of Timothy, bishop of Anastasioupolis, the people of the city, clergy and laity, went to Paul, metropolitan of Ancyra, and asked him to make Theodore their bishop. He would not accept it on any terms, so the Christians resorted to force: they brought him out, set him on a litter and carried him off. At his consecration somebody saw an enormous radiant star come down from heaven and stand over the church, flashing and lighting the city and the country round. He reached Anastasioupolis with Amiantos, bishop of Kinna, by whom he was enthroned, and from then on he shone continually like the sun, with the divine gifts of healings, with the strictness of his life, and with all the virtues and good works.

He wished to visit Jerusalem a second time, and there he venerated the precious Cross, the Tomb of the Lord and all the holy things of the region, and the monasteries nearby. But a thought troubled him and persuaded him at last not to go home but to live a hesychast's life in one of the monasteries there — he thought he had fallen outside the monastic measure by taking on the spiritual charge of a diocese, and the vexatious affairs of it grieved him. So he went to the Lavra of St Sabbas and lived in the cell of a struggling monk called Andrew. But one night George appeared to him in his sleep and gave him a staff and said, «Σήκω και περπάτα, διότι πολλοί άνθρωποι λυπούνται, γιατί απουσιάζεις. Δεν είναι επιτρεπτό να εγκαταλείψεις την Επισκοπή σου και να ζεις εδώ» — rise and walk, for many people are grieving because you are away; it is not permitted that you abandon your diocese and live here.

So he took leave of the fathers of that monastery and set out home. Reaching the parts of Galatia, near the monastery of the Dryinoi, he charged them to tell nobody who he was, for those there did not know him; but his fame went about all the same, and many came to the monastery to get his blessing. From there he returned to Anastasioupolis, to everyone's joy — and then, having resolved to resign and follow the hesychast's road, he met Paul of Ancyra and begged him to accept his resignation. Paul would not; and after a sharp discussion between them they decided at last to send word to Cyriacus, patriarch of Constantinople, and put the matter to him. Cyriacus, at the emperor's urging, ordered the metropolitan of Ancyra to grant the request, and to give him besides the omophorion of the see so that he might keep his rank, since he was a holy man and was leaving the diocese having done no wrong.

So he came to the region of Heliopolis and shut himself away in the church of the Archangel at Akrina, very near the village of Pidros. In that same season he received letters from the emperor Maurice and the patriarch Cyriacus urging him to visit Constantinople and bless them; so he went to the God-guarded city, where he preached the word of God and healed many. He returned to Galatia, but visited Constantinople a second time, in the year 610, under the patriarch Thomas, at whose death he was present; and having been honoured by the patriarch Sergius he came back to his monastery and went on with his God-loving life. He fell asleep in peace in the year 613.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović fills in the childhood the other calendars read for this folder leave out. His birthplace was the village of Сикеа in Galatia, for which he was called Сикеот. Already as a boy of ten he gave himself to hard fasting and to prayer through the night, after the example of an elder named Stephen who lived in his house. His mother Maria was a rich widow and meant to settle her son in the military calling; but [George](/saints/george-the-trophy-bearer) appeared to her in a dream and told her that Theodore was appointed to the service not of an earthly emperor but of the heavenly one. George appeared to Theodore himself many times after that, either to teach him or to save him from some danger the malicious demons had put him in, and he had several visions of the Most Holy Theotokos besides.

His labour, the entry says, surpassed in its severity the labours of every living ascetic of his time: he tormented his body with hunger and thirst, with iron fetters and with standing all night at prayer — all so that he might bind his soul with love to God and make it wholly mistress over the body. To Theodore's love the merciful Lord answered with love, and gave him great power over evil spirits and over every sickness and torment of men, so that he was spoken of everywhere as a wonderworking healer.

For his great purity and spirituality he was consecrated bishop of Anastasiopolis, and that too against his will. He spent eleven years in the episcopal service, and then begged God to free him from it so that he might give himself again to the labour he loved; and he returned to his own monastery, where in old age he gave up his soul to the Lord for whose sake he had willingly suffered much. He died at the beginning of the reign of the emperor Heraclius, about 613 — the year this folder already held, which the entry's „око 613. год.“ agrees with, so nothing was changed by it.
