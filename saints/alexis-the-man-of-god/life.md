# Alexis the Man of God

In the reign of the emperors Arcadius and Honorius there lived in old Rome a devout nobleman named Euphemian, first among the great men of the city and very rich, who kept three thousand servants and set three tables in his house for widows, for the poor, for strangers and for the sick, eating himself at the ninth hour with monks from abroad. His wife Aglaida was barren and prayed for a child; God remembered her, and she bore a son, whom they baptised Alexis.

The boy learned grammar, rhetoric, the books of the Church and the divine Scripture, and, weighing the vanity of the world, resolved to give up the passing goods of this life in order to inherit the eternal ones; he began to wear a rough garment secretly on his body. When he came of age his parents betrothed him to a maiden of the imperial house and crowned him in the church of Saint Boniface. That evening he gave his bride his gold ring and his costly girdle, wrapped in a purple kerchief, told her to keep them until God's grace should do something new, and left her.

He changed into poorer clothes, took some gold and precious stones, went secretly out of the city by night, and sailed to Laodicea; from there he travelled on to Edessa in Mesopotamia, where the image of Christ not made by hands was kept. He sold everything he had brought, gave the gold to the poor, put on a beggar's old coat and sat among those who ask alms in the porch of the church of the Mother of God, fasting on a little bread and water, communing every Sunday, and passing on what alms he was given to older beggars. His father's servants, sent to search for him, gave him alms and did not know him.

After seventeen years the sacristan of the church saw in a vision the icon of the Mother of God telling him to bring the man of God inside, since his prayer rose like fragrant incense before God; and when he could not find such a man, the voice told him it was the beggar who sat at the church door. Being known now and honoured, Alexis fled the city and took ship for Cilicia, and a storm carried the ship instead to Rome. He met his own father on the road at dinner-time, bowed to the ground, and begged a corner of his courtyard. Euphemian, hearing a stranger speak of exile, remembered his son and wept, and gave him a small hut before the doors of the palace and food from his own table.

There he lived another seventeen years and no one knew who he was: the heir and master of the house was mocked as a vagrant by its servants, who struck him on the face, pulled his hair and poured slops over his head, and he bore it in silence. Opposite his hut was the window of the room where his bride stayed with his mother, and he often heard the two of them lamenting, the one her widowhood and the other her lost son. When God revealed to him the day and the hour of his death he asked for paper, ink and pen and wrote out his whole life, with the tokens by which his parents alone could know him.

On a Friday, after a voice from the altar of the church of the Holy Apostles had called the people to seek out the man of God and at last named Euphemian's house, Alexis was found dead in his hut, his face uncovered and a paper held fast in his right hand. The emperor and Pope Innocent came, and at their asking the hand gave up the paper; Aetius, the archivist of the great church, read it aloud, and at the words about the ring and the girdle his father knew him. The crowd could not be kept from the body, so the relics were left a week in the great church for all to venerate, and then laid in a coffer of marble and emerald, out of which fragrant myrrh flowed.

The page dates his departure to the sixteenth calend of April — that is, 17 March — in the year 5919 from the creation of the world and 411 from the incarnation of the Word, Honorius reigning at Rome in the days of Pope Innocent, and Theodosius the Younger holding the empire at Constantinople.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and tells the same story. saint.gr sets his birth at Rome under the emperors Arcadius (395–408) and Honorius (395–423), names his father Euphemianus, a senator who set out three tables in his house every day for the orphans, the widows and the poor strangers, and his barren mother Aglais, to whose prayer he was given. It has him hand back the gold ring and the belt to his bride on the wedding night and take ship for Laodicea in Syria and then for Edessa in Mesopotamia, where he gave away his money and even his clothes and sat seventeen years in the narthex of the church of the Mother of God as one of the poor, fasting the week through and eating a little bread after Sunday's communion.

Two turns of the account are the Greek page's own. The Mother of God appeared in a dream to the church's sacristan and asked that the man of God be brought inside; and when he had been led in with every honour, Alexis slipped away for Tarsus and the church of the Apostle Paul, where he would be unknown, but a violent wind carried the ship to Rome instead, and he understood that the Lord meant him to come home. Seventeen more years he lived in his father's house unrecognised, fed from the leavings of his father's table and mocked by the servants, giving his own food away.

saint.gr adds what became of the relic. His head was given to the monastery of Agia Lavra at Kalavryta in 1398 by the emperor Manuel Palaiologos, and when the Ottomans burned that house in 1585 two monks saved it; portions are kept at Akraiphnio in Boeotia, at Esphigmenou on the Holy Mountain, at Kykkos in Cyprus and at the Lavra of St Alexander Nevsky in St Petersburg.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same menologion day and opens on the thought that the ways by which God leads those who wish to please him are various. In the reign of the emperor Honorius there lived at Rome a high imperial dignitary, Euphemianus, very well regarded and very rich, who with his wife Aglaida led a life pleasing to God; rich as he was, he sat down to table only once a day, and that after sunset. Alexis was their only son, and when he was grown he was compelled to marry. That same night he left not only his wife but his father's house, took ship, and came to the city of Edessa in Mesopotamia, where the famous image of the Lord Jesus was, sent by the Lord himself to king Abgar.

Having venerated that image he put on a beggar's clothes and lived as a beggar in that city for seventeen years, praying without ceasing in the porch of the church of the most holy Mother of God. When he became known there as a man pleasing to God he feared the glory of men and went away, taking ship for Laodicea; but by God's providence the ship was carried off course and sailed as far as Rome. Taking that for the finger of God, Alexis resolved to go to his father's house and to carry on his life and his struggle there unrecognised. His father did not know him, but out of mercy let him live in a shed in his courtyard, and there Alexis passed another seventeen years on bread and water alone, ill-used by the servants in various ways and bearing it all to the end.

When his end drew near he wrote out a paper, gripped it in his hands, lay down and breathed out his spirit, on 17 March 411 — the day and the year this folder already records. Then came a revelation in the church of the Holy Apostles, a voice which said in the presence of the emperor and the patriarch: seek out the man of God. A little after it was revealed that this man of God was in the house of Euphemianus. The emperor came there with the pope and the whole retinue, and after long questioning they learned that the beggar was he; and going into his shed they found him dead, but his face shining like the sun. From the paper his parents learned that this was their son Alexis, and his bride, who had lived thirty-four years without him, that this was her husband. Immeasurable grief took hold of them all, and afterwards they were comforted, seeing how the Lord had glorified the one who pleased him: many sick people were healed by touching his body, and fragrant myrrh flowed from it. His body was buried in a coffin of marble and emerald, and the Prologue says his head is in the Holy Lavra in the Peloponnese.

The Russian church keeps him on the same menologion day, and the Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery gives his story twice over on one name page, in two readings that agree in substance. Both have him born at Rome to a senator and his wife who had long been childless; both have him married at his parents' wish to a girl they chose, and both turn on the one gesture — alone with her on the wedding evening he gave her back his ring, told her to keep it and that the Lord would order a new life for them by his grace, and went away in secret.

The end is the same in both and the detail is this calendar's own. He was living unknown as a beggar in his own father's house; a voice in the church sent them to look for the man of God there, and when they came he was already dead, his face shining with a light not of this world and a scroll held fast in his hand. The emperor and the pope knelt and asked him, as though he were living, to open the hand, and he did; the writing was read out, and his father, his mother and his wife came to the body weeping. It was set in the middle of the square and the whole city came, and the mute spoke and the blind saw. The two of them carried it into the church themselves.

He was buried in the church of Saint Boniface on 17 марта 411, which is the day this calendar keeps him on, and his relics were found in 1216. His life, the page adds, was from of old one of the best loved in Rus. The day page prints two troparia and a kondak for him and all three are copied here.
