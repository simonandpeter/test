# Cyril of Jerusalem

Cyril was born of Orthodox parents and brought up in the right faith, and became a monk in the days of Constantine the Great. Under Constantine's son Constantius, when the blessed Maximus, archbishop of Jerusalem, departed this life, Cyril was raised to his throne, and showed himself a skilled defender of the apostolic dogmas, opposing with zeal the heresies of Arius, of Macedonius and of Manes. For that he was hated by the heretic bishops, and above all by Acacius, archbishop of Caesarea in Palestine, at whose hands he was at last driven out.

In his days a great sign appeared over Jerusalem: at the third hour of the fiftieth day the honourable Cross shone above Golgotha brighter than the sun and stretched out as far as the Mount of Olives. Cyril wrote of the wonder to Constantius, counselling him to the right faith, for the emperor had fallen away into the error of Arius. Acacius rose against him on two grounds: that the saint reproved his heresy, and that he would not submit to him — for Jerusalem had been laid waste and brought low, and its see was held under the metropolitan of Caesarea.

Cyril held that the see of Jerusalem, the mother of all the Churches, should come before Caesarea, and that Acacius, judged a heretic by the local council at Sardica, kept his rank by imperial power and not by the canons. Acacius found his pretext in a famine. All the people ran to Cyril for food; he spent everything he had on them, and when the famine dragged on he began to sell the church goods — the gold, the silver, the costly hangings — and bought grain with the price. Word went round that a woman had been seen dancing in the city in a sacred vestment which a merchant had sold her, and which the archbishop had given him to sell.

Whether the thing was true or not cannot be shown; but Acacius took it as a lawful ground, gathered his synod, and judged Cyril worthy of deposition for selling holy things to dishonour. He drove him out of the borders of Jerusalem. Cyril went to Antioch and then to Tarsus, to the blessed bishop Silvanus, whom he corrected in some points of doctrine; and Silvanus would not obey Acacius's letters demanding that he expel him, knowing him to be Orthodox, holy, a teacher, and driven out without cause. At the local council of Seleucia, where a hundred and fifty bishops gathered, Acacius demanded that Cyril be excluded before it began; many would not agree, so he left for Constantinople, and worked on Constantius until the emperor condemned Cyril to exile.

When Constantius died and Julian the Apostate took the empire, he at first undid all his predecessor's judgements and released the exiled Orthodox bishops, so Cyril received his throne again. Then Julian renounced Christ and gave the Jews leave to build a new temple in the place of Solomon's, paying for it from the public taxes. Cyril said to his people, as a prophet, that the words of Christ would be fulfilled and no stone left upon another, and prayed that God would break the plan; and in one night a great earthquake overthrew not only what had just been laid but Solomon's old foundations with it, and scattered them like dust.

When day came the crowds gathered and marvelled, and when the Jews thought to set to work again, fire fell from heaven and burned all their tools; and the next night the sign of the Cross appeared on their clothes and could not be washed out. After that wonder Cyril, whose prophecy had been fulfilled, was driven from his throne a second time. He returned when the lawless emperor perished, and was exiled a third time when the Arians grew strong again under Valens. After Valens died and Theodosius the Great received the empire, Cyril came back to his see with honour, sat on it eight years undisturbed, shepherded the Church of Christ well, left writings useful to her, and rested in the Lord.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and dates the whole course of his life, which doxologia did not. saint.gr has him born in Palestine, probably in the year 313, at Jerusalem; ordained presbyter by Maximus III, bishop of Jerusalem (333–348), whose memory it puts on 9 May; and succeeding him on the episcopal throne at the beginning of 348, either because the Arians had driven Maximus out or because he had died. At first, the page says frankly, Cyril was indifferent to dogmatic «fine distinctions» and carefully avoided the word ὁμοούσιος, consubstantial, which is why the Arian metropolitan of Caesarea, Acacius, approved his election and consecrated him bishop — and why what then happened to him happened afterwards to [Meletius of Antioch](/saints/meletius-of-antioch) as well, whose memory the page puts on 12 February. From the first months of his episcopate his famous Catecheses showed him a defender of the decisions and definitions of the First Ecumenical Council.

There were two causes of the breach with Acacius: his dogmatic stand, and a dispute over the jurisdiction of the two sees. Jerusalem's Christian community had been scattered when the city was destroyed, and after the rebuilding the Christians there were few, so Caesarea, the capital of Palestine, was made the metropolis; when the Christians of Jerusalem grew in number the bishopric asked its old place back. The seventh canon of Nicaea in 325 laid down that the bishop of Aelia — that is, of Jerusalem — be specially honoured according to ancient custom, while the metropolis of Caesarea kept its own dignity, and the vagueness of that wording set the two men against each other. Acacius had the Arian emperor Constantius (337–361) behind him, and found his pretext in Cyril's having sold sacred vessels and votive offerings in a famine to feed the destitute: a synod at Jerusalem in 357 deposed him and put him out.

He was exiled to Tarsus in Cilicia and received by its bishop Silvanus, who refused Acacius's demand to break communion with him. Cyril asked for a larger synod, and the synod that met at Jerusalem in 359 restored and acquitted him — but Acacius went to Constantinople and had the Jerusalem decisions undone by another synod there in 360, which confirmed the deposition and the exile. He came back to his see in 361 under Julian the Apostate, who recalled the exiled bishops because they were the enemies of Constantius; after Julian's death on 26 July 363 the emperor Valens (364–378) exiled him again for eleven years, and he returned to Jerusalem when Valens died in 378. The Council of Constantinople of 382 praised his struggles, acknowledging «τὸν αἰδεσιμώτατον Κύριλλον ἐπίσκοπον» of the mother of all the churches, canonically ordained long before and having contended much against the Arians in various places. His chief work is the twenty-three Catecheses, delivered during Great Lent and Bright Week of the year 348 in the basilica of the Resurrection, to bring the catechumens into the foundations of the faith and to open the mysteries of the Church to the newly baptised; saint.gr calls their value incalculable and says no earlier work sets out so nearly the whole ritual of the Orthodox Church.
