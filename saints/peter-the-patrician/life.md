# Peter the Patrician

saint.gr gives him a court career before it gives him a cell. His father Constantine was a patrician and a general of the emperor, so Peter had the means to study philosophy and the other sciences in the best schools; he married, and after his father's death became Domestic of the Schools and patrician himself, under the emperor Nikephoros I. The page sets his life in the years of the emperors Constantine and Irene, naming 780 as the point of reference.

On a failed campaign against the Bulgars Peter was taken prisoner with fifty other nobles. It is said, the page goes on, that [John the Evangelist](/saints/john-the-theologian) appeared to him, freed him and went with him as far as his own country. Peter despised what the world then offered him, went up to Olympus and became a monk beside the venerable [Joannicius](/saints/joannicius-the-great).

Thirty-four years later, when his holy elder had died, he came back to Constantinople in 847, and built himself a small hut beside the church of Euandros that he had put up himself. In that hut he lived eight years in asceticism and in a way pleasing to God, and died in peace in the year 854.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 1 July, the same menologion day the Greek calendar keeps him on, and it is one short paragraph that agrees with the Greek page nearly throughout and differs from it in the year. He was a nobleman of Constantinople and a commander in the time of the emperor Nikephoros, it says. In one war with the Bulgars the emperor Nikephoros was killed and Peter taken captive with fifty Greek commanders and princes and thrown into prison, and out of the prison he was wonderfully freed by John the Evangelist. Then he despised all the glory of the world, left wife and son, and withdrew to Mount Olympus.

There, as a monk and the disciple of Joannicius, he struggled thirty-four years. After the death of his wife and his son he settled in Constantinople, where he spent eight more years in fasting and prayer, and reposed in the Lord in the year 865, in the seventy-seventh year of his life. The Greek page gives the year as 854, so the folder’s date now reads 854 or 865, widened to hold both books rather than deciding between them; the seventy-seven years are an age and not a date, and nothing is subtracted from either year to reach a birth. The wife and the son, and their deaths as the reason he came back to the City, are this book’s and not the Greek page’s.
