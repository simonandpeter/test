# Stephen the New

Stephen was a Constantinopolitan. The life doxologia prints says he was born in the city in the first year of the reign of Anastasius — the later, right-believing Anastasius, not the one before Zeno — in the year six thousand two hundred and twenty-two from the creation of the world, which the page reckons as 714. His father was no man of rank or of wealth, but one of those who lived in sufficiency and fed himself by the work of his own hands. Stephen bore the name of the first martyr, and his biographer, writing forty-two years after the martyrdom, says he gathered the story from men who loved the truth and from the saint's own disciples, going about and collecting it out of a long lapse of time as a bee gathers from many flowers.

Still a boy, he was brought over from Chalcedon to the mountain of the God-bearing father [Auxentius](/saints/auxentius-of-bithynia) — cold, dry, higher than all the summits about it and, as the life says, all but touching heaven. There the elder John, who kept Auxentius's place on the mountain, took the shears from his hands and tonsured him at sixteen and clothed him in the angelic habit. He had already given what he had to the poor, and he came up with his mother and one of his sisters, the other having been tonsured before that in another monastery of Byzantium.

When John died, the people round about ran up from the hills and the valleys like thirsty deer to kiss him at his burial, and Stephen took his place, the life says, as a new Elisha who had received Elijah's mantle on Carmel. He shut himself into a cave at the head of the mountain, where the rock ends above a deep ravine, and worked there like a bee in a hive at the honey of the virtues. But a city set on a hill cannot be hidden: many were kindled by his manner of life, and the mountain itself became a city and a wonderful common life.

In the year 754 after the birth of Christ, the fourteenth of the reign of Copronymus, the enemies of the holy icons gathered a council of three hundred and thirty-eight bishops of the East, with no patriarch among them but Constantine, the false patriarch of Constantinople. Stephen would not sign its decree. Soldiers came up to the mountain and took him down with blows, and he was shut up in the monastery of Philippicus by the sea at Chrysopolis, whence word of everything was sent to the emperor.

The council's men were sent to him there, and he was carried in by two others, his feet closed in iron fetters. Asked how he could reckon them heretics and set himself above emperors and bishops, he answered with Elijah's word to Ahab, that it was not he who troubled Israel. Constantine of Nicomedia kicked him in the face as he sat on the ground, and others trod on his breast, and he gave them the other cheek as the Gospel commands. Offered his signature or his death, he said that whatever living blood was in him should be poured out for the holy icon of Christ.

He went on to anathematise any who would not venerate the Lord in his icon according to his humanity; the bishops rose in shame and shut him up alone, and told the emperor that the man had beaten them, being high in strength, in speech and in boldness before death. The emperor wrote out his banishment to the island of Prokonnesos in the straits of the Hellespont. He stayed seventeen days without food at Chrysopolis, sending back the food the emperor sent him. There doxologia's page breaks off, in the middle of the story of the sick abbot who sent to ask his prayers; it gives no year for his martyrdom, and none is written here.
