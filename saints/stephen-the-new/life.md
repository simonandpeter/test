# Stephen the New

Stephen was a Constantinopolitan. The life doxologia prints says he was born in the city in the first year of the reign of Anastasius — the later, right-believing Anastasius, not the one before Zeno — in the year six thousand two hundred and twenty-two from the creation of the world, which the page reckons as 714. His father was no man of rank or of wealth, but one of those who lived in sufficiency and fed himself by the work of his own hands. Stephen bore the name of the first martyr, and his biographer, writing forty-two years after the martyrdom, says he gathered the story from men who loved the truth and from the saint's own disciples, going about and collecting it out of a long lapse of time as a bee gathers from many flowers.

Still a boy, he was brought over from Chalcedon to the mountain of the God-bearing father [Auxentius](/saints/auxentius-of-bithynia) — cold, dry, higher than all the summits about it and, as the life says, all but touching heaven. There the elder John, who kept Auxentius's place on the mountain, took the shears from his hands and tonsured him at sixteen and clothed him in the angelic habit. He had already given what he had to the poor, and he came up with his mother and one of his sisters, the other having been tonsured before that in another monastery of Byzantium.

When John died, the people round about ran up from the hills and the valleys like thirsty deer to kiss him at his burial, and Stephen took his place, the life says, as a new Elisha who had received Elijah's mantle on Carmel. He shut himself into a cave at the head of the mountain, where the rock ends above a deep ravine, and worked there like a bee in a hive at the honey of the virtues. But a city set on a hill cannot be hidden: many were kindled by his manner of life, and the mountain itself became a city and a wonderful common life.

In the year 754 after the birth of Christ, the fourteenth of the reign of Copronymus, the enemies of the holy icons gathered a council of three hundred and thirty-eight bishops of the East, with no patriarch among them but Constantine, the false patriarch of Constantinople. Stephen would not sign its decree. Soldiers came up to the mountain and took him down with blows, and he was shut up in the monastery of Philippicus by the sea at Chrysopolis, whence word of everything was sent to the emperor.

The council's men were sent to him there, and he was carried in by two others, his feet closed in iron fetters. Asked how he could reckon them heretics and set himself above emperors and bishops, he answered with Elijah's word to Ahab, that it was not he who troubled Israel. Constantine of Nicomedia kicked him in the face as he sat on the ground, and others trod on his breast, and he gave them the other cheek as the Gospel commands. Offered his signature or his death, he said that whatever living blood was in him should be poured out for the holy icon of Christ.

He went on to anathematise any who would not venerate the Lord in his icon according to his humanity; the bishops rose in shame and shut him up alone, and told the emperor that the man had beaten them, being high in strength, in speech and in boldness before death. The emperor wrote out his banishment to the island of Prokonnesos in the straits of the Hellespont. He stayed seventeen days without food at Chrysopolis, sending back the food the emperor sent him. There doxologia's page breaks off, in the middle of the story of the sick abbot who sent to ask his prayers; it gives no year for his martyrdom, and none is written here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 28 November, and saint.gr carries the end of the story doxologia.ro broke off. It names his parents John and Anna, says he was brought up by them in the best Christian way and was well educated, and that he was afterwards made abbot on the famous mount of Saint Auxentios. When the war on the holy icons broke out he not only refused the imperial orders but called the iconoclast emperors heretics; denounced to Constantine Copronymus, who hoped that his own presence would break Stephen's mind when he had him brought before him, he did the opposite and reproved the emperor sharply to his face. Copronymus shut him in prison and after some days ordered his death.

They stoned him and struck him with heavy clubs, and one strong blow to the head ended his life, in the year 767. His body was thrown into the sea, and devout Christians who found it where the waves brought it ashore buried it with the honour it deserved. A piece of his relic is kept at the monastery of Dionysiou on the Holy Mountain. The same page keeps beside him on this day the ascetics Andrew of Blachernae and Peter of Olympus, the venerable-martyr Anna, and the martyrs who suffered with him, of whom it names Basil and John of the Legatarioi.

The Russian calendar keeps him on the same day with a line of three words and a year, «Прмч. и исп. Стефана Нового (767).», and names his city on the name page behind it: «Стефан Новый, Константинопольский». What it adds to this folder is a service and a company. Its day page prints four hymns under his name, two troparia and two kontakia, all now stored here in Church Slavonic; the first troparion has him strive first as an ascetic on the mountain and then arm himself for the contest, slaying the iconoclast emperor with the sword of faith, and the second kontakion praises him precisely for honouring the painted depiction of the Master and of his Mother.

The company is the separate line that follows his on the same day: «Мчч. Стефана , Василия , Григория , другого Григория , Иоанна и иных многих (VIII).» The one life the calendar carries for them says they suffered for the honouring of the icons together with him, were shut up in the prison with him, and were put to death after his own end. The corpus already held one of the five, and this day adds the other four named; the rest the calendar numbers and does not name.

The Охридски пролог begins him before his birth. As Hannah the mother of Samuel once prayed, it says, so did his mother pray to God to give her a son; and praying so one day in the church at Blachernae, before the icon of the most holy Mother of God, a light sleep took her, in which she saw the most holy Virgin bright as the sun and heard a voice from her: „Иди, жено, с миром, према молитви твојој имаш сина у утроби твојој.“ — Go, woman, in peace; according to thy prayer thou hast a son in thy womb. And she conceived indeed, and bore this holy Stephen.

In his sixteenth year he took the monastic habit on the mountain of [Auxentius](/saints/auxentius-of-bithynia), facing Constantinople, from an elder the book names Јован, from whom he learned divine wisdom and the ascetic life; and when that elder fell asleep in the Lord he stayed on the mountain, keeping a strict rule and adding labour to labour, and his holiness drew him many disciples. When the emperor Constantine Copronymus persecuted the icons, more fiercely even than his unclean father Leo the Isaurian, Stephen stood out as a zealous defender of the veneration of the holy icons; and the senseless emperor took in every foul slander against him and laid plots himself besides, for no other purpose than to break him and put him out of the way.

He was exiled to the island of Proconnesus, then brought back to Constantinople, put in irons and thrown into prison — where three hundred and forty-two monks met him, prisoners brought from various countries and flung in for the honouring of the icons, who kept the whole church rule in that prison as though they were in a monastery. The wicked emperor condemned him to death. He foresaw his own death forty days ahead and took leave of the brethren.

The emperor’s servants dragged him out of the prison and hauled him through the streets of Constantinople, beating and tearing at him and calling on all who were for the emperor to throw stones at «the emperor’s adversary»; and one of the heretics struck him on the head with a piece of wood, and he gave up his spirit. As the first of the martyrs suffered at the hands of the Jews, the book says, so did he at the hands of the iconoclast heretics. He suffered in the year 767, this glorious soldier of Christ, in the fifty-third year of his earthly life — and those two figures agree with both years this folder already held, the birth of 714 from the Romanian calendar and the death of 767 from the Greek, which until now came one from each and had never been confirmed together.
