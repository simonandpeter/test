# Theophanes the Myrrh-streamer

Theophanes lived in Cyprus in the years of the island's occupation by the Venetians, between 1489 and 1571. Of his parents, his family in the world and his schooling saint.gr says nothing is known. What is known is little but important, it adds, because it comes from a contemporary who was an eyewitness of the exhumation of his relic: Stephen Lusignan, a Dominican monk and vicar of the Latin archbishopric of Cyprus, in his Chorography of the island of Cyprus, published first in Italian in 1573 and then, more carefully, in French in 1580.

He loved Christ beyond measure, left the world, became a monk, and lived in the monastery of the Manganon at Nicosia, dedicated to Saint George — a Byzantine house which Helen Palaiologina, wife of John II of Cyprus, restored in 1453 to shelter the clergy who came to the island after the fall of Constantinople, and which the Venetians pulled down in 1567 while building the new fortifications of Nicosia. His life as a monk was exemplary: as far as his manner of living goes, the biographical notice in that work says, no one could find anything to blame. Though he hid his virtues in obscurity and humility, the Lord made him known, and led the bishops of the day to elect him, against his will, Archbishop of Cyprus. Because the Latin archbishop was installed at Nicosia, he bore instead the title bishop of Soleas and governor of the Greeks of Nicosia — the Greek bishops under Venetian rule not being allowed to live in the great towns where their sees were — and that second phrase shows the authority the Venetians granted him over some of the internal affairs of the enslaved Greeks.

How long he stayed in that office is not known. An unexpected and strange incident led him to submit his resignation, which was accepted only with difficulty. One day, the French text of the Chorography says, he reproved his oikonomos — the highest church office after the bishop's — for some fault; and the oikonomos, an exceedingly proud and insolent man, slapped him in front of the people. The bishop, seeing so grave and unhappy an act against his person and holding himself wholly unworthy of the place he held, went to the Latin archbishop and laid his bishopric in his hands. He begged with tears that the resignation be accepted; the Venetian authorities at first refused, out of the respect they had for him, and after his insistence agreed. He did not grow angry, and asked neither vengeance nor punishment for the man who had struck him.

Once the resignation was accepted he satisfied his inward wish for the life of stillness. He went far from the noise of the cities to a monastery at Mesa Potamos in the mountains of Troodos, where, according to Archimandrite Nikodemos, exarch of the metropolis of Kition at Limassol, he was also abbot for a time; and afterwards he went up and settled in a cell some eighty-seven metres above the house, so that the place took the name «the abbot's monastery». There he lived alone a life of holiness, and after his falling asleep the Lord gave him the gift of wonderworking, as the monks of the monastery bear witness. The page's head line dates his death to 1550, and its couplets rejoice that the fold of the Forerunner has him as its divine protector.
