# Sava of Serbia

He was Rostislav — Rastko — in the world, a son of the Serbian king Stephen Nemanja and of Anna, daughter of the Byzantine emperor Romanus. From his early years he was constant at the services and had a particular love for icons.

At seventeen he met a monk from Mount Athos, left his father's house secretly and made for the monastery of Saint Panteleimon. His father, learning where he was, sent his retainers under a trusted voivode and wrote to the governor of the district that if his son were not given back he would make war on the Greeks; the voivode was ordered never to take his eyes off the boy. During the evening services, when the soldiers had fallen asleep after wine, Rostislav was tonsured — in 1186 — and sent his parents his worldly clothes, his shorn hair, and a letter.

He then set about persuading his powerful parents to take up the monastic life themselves, and his father and he practised asceticism together at Vatopedi. On Athos they founded the Serbian monastery of Hilandar, which received its name by imperial grant, and there Sava was ordained deacon and then presbyter. His mother Anna became a nun.

The Romanian calendar's life sets him in his father's reign: Stefan Nemanja was named voivode of Raška by the emperor Manuel I Komnenos in 1167, rebelled with Hungarian and Venetian support, was forced to surrender and taken prisoner to Constantinople in 1172, and came back to power in 1183 as a Byzantine subject. Rastko was the third son, born in 1170 or 1175, after Vukan and Stefan, when his mother could bear no more children and the two of them had begged God for another with fasting and tears. The same page counts father and son together as the founders of Hilandar, and Sava besides as one of the founders of Vatopedi, Karakallou, Xeropotamou and Philotheou, whose donations to the Athonites are well remembered.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, as first archbishop of Serbia and founder of the holy monastery of Hilandar, and dates his death to 1236. saint.gr puts his life at the end of the twelfth century and the beginning of the thirteenth, and makes him the second-born son of Stephen I Nemanja, ruler of Serbia — Neeman, as the page says the Byzantine sources call him — and of the princess Anna; his name in the world was Resko. From a small child he had a great love for the Christian virtues, and at seventeen he went to the Holy Mountain, where with his royal father's consent he became a monk at the monastery of Vatopedi and was renamed Sabbas. The page's account of what followed is longer than what is set down here; it ends with his death in the year 1236, his relics found incorrupt, and their burning at Belgrade by Sinan Pasha in 1594. Over him it prints the couplet «Φωστήρ εδείχθης φωτίσας την Σερβίαν, / Έργοις λόγοις τε Σάββα των Σέρβων κλέος» — thou wast shown a luminary, enlightening Serbia by works and words, O Sabbas, the boast of the Serbs.
