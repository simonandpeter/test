# Agathopodes

In the reign of Diocletian and Maximian, the heathen emperors of Rome, there were at Thessalonica two churchmen pleasing to God, Agathopodes and [Theodulus](/saints/theodulus-companion-of-agathopodes). Agathopodes was a deacon, old in years and very wise, adorned with grey hairs; Theodulus was a reader, young and fair of face, living in purity and without blame, the son of honourable Christian parents and the brother of Capito, Metrodorus and Philostorgius, all of them right-believing.

When the emperors raised a persecution and their orders went out that men should bow to idols made by men's hands, the order came to Thessalonica too, and the instruments of torture were set out in the theatre for those who would not obey. Many of the faithful fled and hid; some ran gladly to the torments; others, weak and afraid of the threats of death, went over to the heathen and ate of the idol sacrifices, loving this empty life more than the deathless one. These two neither fled nor hid, but stayed in the house of God, praying day and night for the Church that stood in danger and waiting to be taken.

The soldiers learned of it, seized them and threw them into prison, and Faustinus, the governor at Thessalonica, sat in judgement over them. He sent Agathopodes away first in order to catch the young man alone, and spoke to Theodulus in a friendly way; then he sent Theodulus off and called Agathopodes and told him that Theodulus had already promised to sacrifice. Agathopodes saw the trick and answered that he too would gladly offer sacrifice with Theodulus — to the true God and to his Son Jesus Christ, for it was a sacrifice of sweet savour to that God that Theodulus had promised.

Then, shaking his head a little, he asked whether those were gods whom the craftsman shaped out of perishable stuff in the likeness of a man; gods whom men made with their own hands and then senselessly decreed to serve as their betters; gods who, if anyone wished to throw them down and break them, could neither resist nor defend themselves, who see nothing with their eyes, walk nowhere with their feet, and have no feeling in them at all. The officials standing by feared that the other Christians brought for trial would be strengthened by his words, and had the two taken back to prison.

In the prison they prayed quietly and joined the others shut up there for all kinds of causes; at midnight they were strengthened by visions and woke rejoicing, calling on Jesus. They washed their hands and faces with clean water, bowed head and knee to the ground, and prayed with one voice to God the maker of all, who set the unresting course of the heaven, gave the earth its creatures and the deep its fish and the air its birds, and who, when our race had fallen into lawlessness, did not let it perish but sent his Only-begotten from the heavenly throne. Those bound with them for murder and fornication threw off the fear of bodily death and fell at their feet asking forgiveness; and the people outside broke the locks and came in to listen.

Eupsychius, the head of the court of Thessalonica and a ready servant of the devil, ran to tell the governor that many would leave the gods if those two were left bound where they were. At the questioning the governor asked whether it was not right to obey the commands of the emperors who rule the world, and Theodulus answered that what the Master of heaven and earth commands must be obeyed first, and of what temporal emperors command only what is just and not against the heavenly Maker.

The governor had the young man stripped for torture while the crier called on him to sacrifice, and Theodulus said that he had taken the clothes from his body but could never take his faith in his God. He was ordered to bring the Christian books to the court and refused to put the divine gifts into a hand he knew to be treacherous; he was led out to the place of execution to frighten him, and seeing the sword over him gave thanks to God and said he came to him gladly. Agathopodes, asked what manner of life he had, answered: the same as Theodulus — by birth we are apart, but in faith and in manner we are one, and as far as we differ in kin, so far we are joined in spirit.

At the third hearing they answered nothing but that they were Christians and willing to suffer everything for the name of Christ, and the governor, with a sorrowful face, gave sentence that Theodulus and Agathopodes, who would not sacrifice to the gods, should be drowned in the sea. The night before, being of one soul and one mind, they had both been shown the same dream of a ship and of their end, and had known that the martyrdom of the sea was the gift given to them both.

The soldiers bound their hands behind them and heavy stones to their necks and put them in the boat, and a crowd of friends, neighbours and acquaintances gathered, some lamenting and others calling them blessed. The governor, pitying them, sent out a man of note called Fulvius to advise them that they need only offer incense and would escape death; and they did not stop calling on Jesus Christ and denying the unclean idols. They threw Agathopodes in first, and he looked up to heaven and cried aloud: see, with this second baptism all our faults are washed away, and we go to Jesus Christ clean. Then Theodulus after him.

The sea took their bodies and in the same hour put them out on the shore with the waves, loosed from their bonds and their stones and shining; and those who knew them took them and buried them with honour. Not long after, Theodulus appeared to them in white with a shining face, telling them to share out his goods to the destitute, the orphans and the widows. They ended on the fifth day of the month of April.

The Greek calendar keeps the two of them on 4 April, and saint.gr adds the shape of their life together before the trial. It says they came from Thessalonica and lived in the first years of the persecutions; Agathopodes was the elder and Theodulus the younger, and Christian love joined them like brothers. They studied the Scriptures together, worked for the spreading of the Gospel, and wore themselves out in the service of their suffering and destitute brethren.

One night both saw the same dream: that they were travelling in a ship, that a storm came up suddenly and broke it in two, and that they were saved out of it and climbed a mountain whose summit reached to heaven. The next day the dream came true. They were arrested and understood that waves of death were waiting for them; the governor Faustinus called on them to deny Christ, and both confessed him with courage, so they were thrown into the sea. The page's couplet for him makes the same point: «Πρῶτος θάλασσαν εἰσιὼν Ἀγαθόπους, / Ἔργοις ὑπῆρχε Χριστόπους Θεοδούλῳ» — first into the sea, Agathopodes was by his deeds a Christ's-foot for Theodulus to follow.
