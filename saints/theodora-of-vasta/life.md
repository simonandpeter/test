# Theodora of Vasta

Theodora came from the Peloponnese, mother of saints and heroes, and so is
called by some Theodora the Peloponnesian; Arcadia and Messenia seem the
likeliest regions of her birth. She lived in the ninth century — the tenth,
by others — in the years of the Byzantine empire, to parents poor and
obscure but loving God, who handed on to their children the faith in
Christ; from a small child Theodora, more than her brothers and sisters,
had a particular love and bent for divine things, loving God so much that
she wished to give her whole life to him, gaining day by day what the holy
Fathers call the love of Christ. Growing up she resolved to live in a
monastery and cultivate her love for Christ there — but, strangely, she
chose not a convent but a men’s monastery: she presented herself at the
monastery of the Panaitsa, on the border of Arcadia and Messenia, as a
man, with the name Theodore — why, cannot be said for certain; perhaps she
wanted to vanish entirely from those who knew her. In the men’s monastery
she soon became an example of patience, obedience and humility, virtues
that led her step by step to a spiritual progress the abbot and her
fellow-monks recognised; and the fathers, admiring the character and the
gifts that marked “Theodore” out, entrusted him with the monastery’s
outside business — a service, the synaxarion remarks, always given to monks
or nuns experienced in the spiritual life. At that time a terrible famine
fell on the wider Peloponnese, so that the people and the monastery were in
danger of starving, and all the fathers looked to “Theodore” as the one
person who could help in so hard a case; and indeed he went round many
Christian houses to support them and, if it could be done, to find
something for the brotherhood too. Then something terrible happened: a
woman let fly a grave accusation against him — the monk, she said, has left
me pregnant. The news spread fast, and a wicked slander was already built.
The pregnant woman’s parents climbed to the monastery in anger and roughly
ordered “Theodore” to come with them; the “monk” denied the charge but did
not refuse to follow, and was tried summarily, found guilty and given the
last of penalties, death by beheading. Though the uncovering of her body
would have proved her innocence, she chose to carry the weight of the
slander. The village of Vasta in Arcadia was fixed as the place of
execution; the executioner led him there, “Theodore” following as a
speechless lamb, and a little later Theodora’s soul flew up to heaven, to
the place prepared for the Church’s monastic virgin-martyrs. The
executioner and his helpers, who had beheaded the martyr, saw the body
uncovered, repented and asked God’s forgiveness. The wonder became known
everywhere; the abbot and the brethren came lamenting to the place of her
martyrdom, and glorifying God buried her body in their monastery — or, as
others hold, at the place itself. It is said that before her beheading she
asked God that her body might become a church, the hair of her head trees,
and her blood a river; and indeed on the roof of the church founded at
Vasta in Arcadia seventeen trees have grown, standing paradoxically on the
roof itself, a witness that where God wills, the order of nature is
overcome. A detailed life of the saint was written by Theophilos,
metropolitan of Gortyna and Megalopolis, and the synaxarion notes that her
story has a good deal in common with that of Theodora of Alexandria, kept
the same day. The Greek and Romanian calendars keep her on 11 September.
