# Olga of Kyiv

At the end of the dark night of idol-worship that lay over the land of Rus, doxologia begins, blessed Olga rose like a morning star before the daylight of faith in Christ. She was of a famous family, great-granddaughter of Gostomysl, who ruled in great Novgorod before the princes of Rus and at whose counsel Rurik and his brothers were called in to take the great rule; and she was born in the village of Vybutsk, which lies near the city of Pskov, though the city did not then exist. She was brought up in the worship of idols but taught well by honourable parents, and was of a very sound and whole mind.

Rurik's son Igor, grown to his youth and out hunting near Vybutsk, could not cross the river and called to a boatman to ferry him over; and looking at the rower he saw that this was no young man but a girl of very beautiful face — Olga. He was wounded in the heart at the sight of her and began to speak lewd and flattering words to her, and she, understanding what moved him, answered him not with anger but with reason, and he was put to shame; and she became his wife.

Oleg, his kinsman and tutor, died, and Igor ruled the whole land of Rus alone, with great wars against the peoples around him and a campaign as far as Constantinople; afterwards he lived at peace. His end came when, going about his cities to collect the usual tribute, he came to the Derevlians and remembered their old ill-will, and was killed. Olga, hearing in Kyiv of her husband's murder, wept for him bitterly with her son Sviatoslav, and all the people wept with her.

The page tells her revenge at length: the Derevlian envoys who came to offer her their own prince in marriage and were buried in a pit dug for them by night in the princely court outside the city; the funeral feast she went to hold for her first husband at the place where he was killed; and the campaign of the second year, when she took her son Sviatoslav with her, urging him to avenge his father, beat the Derevlians and drove them into their capital Korosten, and at last took the city by the birds she had asked as tribute, sending back doves and sparrows at dusk with burning sulphur tied to them, so that each went to its own nest and the city caught fire in a thousand places at once.

When the time came in which God willed to light the blinded Rus with the light of the faith, He chose to begin that enlightening, the page says, through a weak woman's vessel, to the shame of the men hardened in heart. Olga took some of the noted boyars and courtiers of Rus and went to Constantinople by ship with much wealth, and was received with great honour by the emperor and the patriarch, and there she learnt the Christian faith, listening every day to the divine words.

The emperor, then a widower, wished to marry her, overcome by her beauty and moved too by her good sense, her fame and the greatness of her lands. She asked that he himself should be her godfather at the font, saying that otherwise she would not be baptised at all and they would answer to God for her soul; and he agreed, and she was baptised, and the patriarch communicated her and blessed her, saying that she was blessed among the women of Rus, who had left the darkness and sought the true light. Then the emperor spoke again of marriage, and she answered, How could you take your own daughter from the font to wife? — a thing forbidden by the Christian law and by the pagan. You have outwitted me, Olga, he said.

She was called Helen at her baptism. Going to the patriarch for a blessing for the road, she asked him to pray for her, returning to a country where her son lived in pagan error and all the people were hardened in the old uncleanness. The cross he gave her carried the inscription that the land of Rus was renewed for life in God by the holy baptism which blessed Olga received. Coming to Kyiv the new Helen began like a sun to drive out the darkness of idolatry, built the first church there in the name of Saint Nicholas at Askold's mound, and turned many of the people of Kyiv to Christ; her son Sviatoslav she could not bring to the truth at all.

When Sviatoslav left her in Kyiv with his sons Yaropolk, Oleg and Vladimir and went off against the Bulgarians, she taught her grandsons the faith. He came back in haste against the Pechenegs who had fallen on Kyiv, drove them off, and bowed to his mother, who was ill; and when he meant to go away again to the Bulgarians she wept and held him. He promised her everything she asked except that he would not receive the faith. Three days later, come to her last weakness, she received the holy Mysteries and gave herself wholly to prayer.

Blessed Olga, called Helen from her baptism, died on 11 July. She had lived forty-two years in marriage after her virginity, and was twenty when she married; in the tenth year after her husband's death she was granted holy baptism, and lived fifteen years after it, so that all the years of her life were nearly ninety. Sviatoslav and her grandsons, the boyars and all the people wept for her, and she was buried with honour after the Christian manner. The page gives no year for any of this.

Her grandson afterwards took up her relics with the metropolitan Leontius of Kyiv, whole and unharmed and full of a sweet smell, and moved them with great honour into the church of the Most Holy Mother of God, and set them in the open for those who came to them in faith. There was a little window in the church wall above her tomb, the page ends, which opened of itself to anyone who came to her relics with undoubting faith, so that he saw them from outside and saw a wonderful brightness coming from them and was healed of whatever sickness he had; but to one who came with little faith it did not open, and he saw only the tomb, though he went into the church, and got no healing.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 11 July, and gives the years the Romanian page did not. saint.gr says she was queen of Russia and was renamed Helen through holy baptism in the year 957; that it is hard for anyone to describe her tireless efforts for the Christian enlightenment of the Russian people, and that she did everything so that the Russians should come to know the truth of the Gospel of Christ. When she once came with her retinue to Byzantium, the page says, she met a warm reception for her struggles on behalf of Christianity. In the middle of that struggle she gave up her last breath, on 11 July of the year 969.

The Serbian church keeps her on the same 11 July, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović heads her not by the name this folder carries but by the name she was given at the font: „Св. Јелена.“, Saint Helen. Its first clause says why — the great Russian princess, before her baptism called Olga. It gives her as the wife of prince Igor, baptised at Constantinople by the patriarch Polyeuctus, and a great zealot of the Orthodox faith in Russia; and it puts her repose in 969, which is the year this folder already held.
