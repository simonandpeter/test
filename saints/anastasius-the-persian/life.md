# Anastasius the Persian

The life begins with the taking of Jerusalem by Chosroes, king of Persia, when the wood of the Cross was carried off into Persia among the spoil; and it says that the glory of Christ then rose over Persia like the sun, because of the wonders worked by that wood in its captivity. Anastasius was a Persian by birth, from a village called Rasnun within the borders of a district called Razeh. His name in his Persian heathenism was Magundat, and he was the son of a magus named Vava, a noted teacher of the magic art, who taught it to his son thoroughly while he was still young.

Grown to manhood, Magundat was enrolled with other young men in the army and served King Chosroes in the royal city. Hearing what was said of the wood of the Cross, he could not rest for asking about it, wanting to know how a piece of wood could have such power; and when Christians told him that it was the Cross on which the Son of God was crucified for the salvation of mankind, he burned to know that Son of God altogether, and asked question after question: how God came down from heaven, how he became man, why he was condemned to the Cross, and by whom.

He was serving, with a brother of his own, in the company of a Persian general named Sain, which came as far as Chalcedon; and when Heraclius fell upon the Persians and Sain turned back, Magundat left the company and his brother with it, wanting rather to be poor and unknown among Christians than rich and honoured among men who did not know God. At Hierapolis he lodged with a Persian who was a Christian and a silversmith, learned his trade, and asked to be taken to holy baptism; but it was put off for fear of the Persians. Seeing the martyrdoms painted on the church walls and hearing what they were, his heart burned the more, and he went up to Jerusalem to be baptised there, and was given the name Anastasius.

He was tonsured a monk by the spiritual father who had received him, and the life says he was humble, gentle, a lover of work, doing every monastic obedience in the kitchen, the bakehouse and the garden without slackening. Taken afterwards for his confession of Christ, he was kept in prison by night and brought out in chains by day to carry stone with the other prisoners; and when the news reached his monastery the brethren were filled with joy, and the abbot sent one monk after him to serve him and to see his end and bring the account back.

He was led in irons into Persia and shut in a prison in a city the page calls Vithsalia. The prisoners condemned with him were taken out of the city to the riverbank and strangled one by one with a rope about the neck, and after each one the soldiers asked Anastasius whether he would not rather obey the king and live and take gifts and honour. He lifted his eyes to heaven and gave thanks, saying that this death was light to him. So he was strangled last of all, on the twenty-second day of January; and to show the king that he was dead the soldiers cut off his head and carried it to him, and threw his body to the dogs with the others, which the dogs would not touch. The monk who had followed him came back after two years with the martyr's mantle, and a young monk possessed of a devil was healed when the abbot put that mantle on him; and the abbot then sent brethren into Persia who brought back the relics.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and is exact about where his relic now is: the head and part of the holy relics, saint.gr says, are at the Roman Catholic monastery «Tre Fontane», the Three Fountains, at Rome. Its heading gives the year of his death as 628. Its apolytikion puts his two crowns side by side — «Την πλάνην αφέμενος, την των Περσών νουνεχώς, τη πίστει προσέδραμες», having wisely left the error of the Persians he ran to the faith, and having shone in asceticism he contended above nature — and its kontakion for the day is sung to him together with [Timothy](/saints/timothy-of-ephesus), the disciple of Paul, calling him the wise Anastasius who shone out of Persia like a star.
