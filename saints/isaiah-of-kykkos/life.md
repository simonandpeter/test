# Isaiah, founder of Kykkos

One of the many famous monasteries of Cyprus is Kykkos, dedicated to the
Panagia Eleousa, which keeps the wonderworking icon of the Mother of God
that tradition says the evangelist Luke painted — and it is there through
the efforts and the wonderful persistence of an old hermit, Isaiah. Where he
was born, who his parents were and what schooling he had is not known; what
is known is that about the eleventh century he lived in a cave in the hills
where the great monastery now stands, and that, kneeling at prayer, he used
to hear a strange bird sing over and over: Kykkou, Kykkou, the hill will
become a monastery, a golden Lady will go in and never come out — which he
heard and could not understand.

About the year 1100 the Byzantine governor of Cyprus, the duke Manuel
Boutoumites, went up from Nicosia to a village of Marathasa for the summer,
as the governors did, and one day out hunting wild sheep lost his companions
and wandered for hours in the forest. Tired and out of temper he came on the
cave and a man in rags at its mouth, asked who he was, and got no answer —
the hermit, not wanting to be known, moved off. The duke took it as an
insult, called him an old wretch, beat him and kicked him to the ground; and
Isaiah, weeping, told him quietly that he was a servant of Christ and that
his Lord would repay him. The duke rode away and thought no more of it, went
back to Nicosia when the summer ended, and within days fell into a lethargy
that paralysed every limb. Then he remembered the hermit’s words, and prayed
in tears for forgiveness; God gave him back his health, and the next day he
set out for the mountains with his servants to ask the old man’s pardon. That
same night the Mother of God came to Isaiah in a dream and told him the duke
was coming, that he would offer to make amends with whatever the hermit asked,
and what to ask for: the icon Luke had painted, which was kept in the palace
at Constantinople.

So it went. The duke thought the thing all but impossible — the icon was among
the emperor’s greatest treasures — but promised to try if the hermit came
with him, and the two sailed for the City. Isaiah lodged in a monastery he
knew rather than in a great house; the duke went to the emperor Alexius again
and again to report on Cyprus and never dared raise the icon; and after a
while the hermit could not bear the crowded city and asked to go home. The
duke, grieved, had two icons painted for him, Christ enthroned and the Holy
Trinity in the form of Abraham’s hospitality, with the Mother of God, Isaiah
and Boutoumites himself along the foot, gave him these and money and his
leave, and promised to do the impossible. Back in his cave Isaiah dreamed the
same dream again and heard a voice telling him not to grieve, and without
waiting built a church of the Holy Trinity, set the two icons in it, and put
cells round it for a monastery. Then the emperor’s eldest daughter fell ill
with the duke’s own sickness; Boutoumites asked for the icon, was promised it
if she recovered, and she did, and the emperor did not keep his word. Then
the emperor fell ill himself, and thought to send Cyprus a copy — until the
Mother of God came to him in his sleep and reminded him of his promise. He
had the imperial ship made ready, the icon put aboard with a devout
hieromonk to be its abbot, and money for a fine church, all to be handed by
the duke to the monk Isaiah to do with as he would. When the ship put in on
the coast of Tylliria the old man came down from the hills of Kykkos on
joyful feet, with the duke and thousands who had left their villages at the
news, and there the duke gave him the icon and the money. He built the
monastery, set the icon in its church, lived out his life there, and died in
peace.
