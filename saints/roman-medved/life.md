# Roman Medved

Roman Ivanovich Medved, archpriest and confessor, was born in Kholm province
on 1 October 1874. With a theological education he gave himself to pastoral
service, trying in everything to follow the example of his spiritual father,
John of Kronstadt, who in 1900 married him to Anna Nevzorova, who also
became the Kronstadt pastor’s spiritual daughter; the same year he was
ordained priest, and served first at the church of the Exaltation of the
Cross in Chernigov province, in 1902–1903. From 1907 he served selflessly as
rector of the Admiralty cathedral of St Vladimir at Sevastopol, and as
protopresbyter of the Black Sea fleet had great authority among the
sailors, with fifty ships’ priests under him; to dispel the revolutionary
“romance” in the fleet he published a pamphlet on the temptations of
revolution and the foundations of Orthodoxy, by his preaching ended the
unrest of 1912 on the battleship St John Chrysostom, and there confessed
the sailors condemned to be shot. On the eve of Christmas 1918, though, a
sailor whom he had caught stealing church money, and who had become
chairman of the revolutionary committee in the February revolution, had him
sentenced to be shot; his devoted spiritual children warned him, and he got
away to Moscow. There for a time he cared for the parish of St Basil’s,
orphaned by the shooting of [John Vostorgov](/saints/john-vostorgov), until
the church was closed, and then at St Alexis in Glinishchevsky lane, where
in those hard years he built a community of deep spiritual life: a
Brotherhood of Zealots of Orthodoxy in honour of Alexis, metropolitan of
Moscow, with at least two hundred standing members, which ran public
lectures at which he too spoke, exposing the false ideals the new power was
pressing on the people. In May 1921 he was arrested and accused of leading
an organisation with anti-Soviet political aims, publishing anti-Soviet
literature and spreading an antisemitic leaflet, “Who is our government”;
under an amnesty the case against the brotherhood’s leaders was dropped and
he was freed before trial. For all his failing health he stayed calm,
approachable and angelically meek; the famous Moscow confessor Alexis Mechev,
comparing their communities, said: you have a hospital, and I only an
out-patients’ clinic. In the thirties he was arrested and his church
destroyed; in 1931 he was sentenced to ten years in the camps at Solovki,
where he worked as a watchman and then a book-keeper and went on writing to
his spiritual children, and where the disablement he suffered shortened his
term. He came back in 1936 and settled at Maloyaroslavets, where despite
grave illness — tuberculosis of the bone and a broken hip — he went on with
his pastoral work, and shortly before his death took the rasophore as
Joseph. He died on 26 August 1937 — 8 September of the new style — and was
buried in the town cemetery; his relics were found on 3 August 1999 and
carried to Moscow, to the church of the Protection on Lyshchikova hill. The
Jubilee Council of Bishops of August 2000 numbered him among the new martyrs
and confessors of Russia; the Russian calendar keeps him on 26 August
(Julian) with the hieromartyr Victor and the martyrs Demetrius and Peter,
and the finding of his relics on 21 July.
