# John the Merciful of Alexandria

Doxologia's life makes him the son of Epiphanius the governor, a man of Cypriot stock, brought up from his youth in good learning, with the fear of God rooted in his heart as the beginning of wisdom. Grown to full age, he was pressed by his parents to take a wife, and had children by her. Then he was bereaved first of his sons, and afterwards his wife followed them; and the page reads this as God's own will, that John should be loosed from the bondage of the flesh and receive the spiritual life.

Left free, he gave himself the more diligently to prayer and to whatever pleased God, and above all to mercy towards everyone who suffered need and poverty, so that he was honoured not only by his equals but by the emperor. When the throne of the Patriarchate of Alexandria stood without a shepherd, the emperor Heraclius raised him to it, and he bowed, unwilling, to receive the ordination.

His first care was to purge the flock of the heresy then being sown by one Peter, surnamed Fullo and the Fuller, a false patriarch of Antioch, who had dared to add words of blasphemy to the Thrice-Holy Hymn — «Sfinte Dumnezeule, Sfinte Tare, Sfinte fără de moarte, Care Te-ai răstignit pentru noi, miluiește-ne pe noi» — as though the Godhead had suffered in our Lord Jesus Christ. Having put that blasphemy away, he turned to the commandments of God and to the good of the poor.

At the beginning of his shepherding he called the stewards of the church and told them to walk the whole city and write down his lords. When they asked who his lords were, he answered that those they called poor and needy were his lords, for they could bring him to the eternal dwellings and give him every kind of help towards salvation. They wrote down seven thousand five hundred of them, found in the streets and in the poorhouses, and he appointed to each a daily ration out of the churches' goods.

When the Persians warred upon Syria and burned the Holy City of Jerusalem, carrying off the honourable wood of the Cross and many Christians into captivity, he sent ships with wheat and with gold to ransom the prisoners. Because not everyone could come to him freely, he set aside two days in the week, Wednesday and Friday, and stood in the doors of the churches with certain honourable men, hearing each man's complaint and judging the quarrels that arose between brothers.

If a day passed in which nobody had come or asked anything of him, he went home in tears, saying that the humble John had gained nothing and had brought nothing to God for his own sins; and his friend the blessed Sophronius would comfort him, saying that he ought rather to rejoice, for his sheep were living in peace, without persecution and without quarrel, like the angels of God.

He was buried in the city of his own country, in the house of Tychon the wonderworker, between the bodies of two bishops already lying there; and the page says that when they went to lay him beside them, those holy bodies moved as though alive and drew apart to make room for him in the middle, and all who were present glorified God. The life ends with a woman who could not for shame confess a grave sin, and gave it to him written and sealed. Five days later he died, the letter unopened and nothing said of it.

Thinking her sin now known to everyone, she came to his grave and cried out to him as to a living man for three days, until on the third night he came out of the tomb with the two bishops and gave her back the sealed paper. She found her own seal whole, and opened it, and her writing was erased; in its place stood the words «Pentru Ioan, robul Meu, s-a șters păcatul tău» — for John My servant, thy sin is blotted out. The page gives no year for his birth or for his death.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 12 November, and gives his birthplace and his own words. saint.gr has John the Merciful born at Amathus in Cyprus — which it glosses as today's Old Limassol — the son of the governor Epiphanios and of Eukosmia, and living in the years of the emperor Heraclius, which it dates to 615. When he grew up he married and had children, whom he and his wife brought up as truly Christian parents; but his wife and children soon died. He had a great fortune and many proposals were made to him to start a new family, and he refused them all, answering: I think I am a debtor to everybody — and I do not only think it, I am; for we Christians have solidarity with one another. Does not Paul say it? We are members one of another. Since, then, I have the means to give to my brothers, I am also obliged to give; that is why I work and will not stop doing it, and my property cannot stand higher than these debts of mine. For the brightness of his life he was made archbishop of Alexandria, where he shone as a spiritual lamp for many years of his patriarchate and worked many wonders; and because he dealt out alms to the poor open-handedly he was called the Merciful. He had become so venerable that even the idolaters respected him; and at the last he gave up his blessed soul to God in peace in the year 620.

The Russian calendar's life adds the Cypriot beginning and the shape of the almsgiving. He was born on Cyprus in the seventh century into the house of a nobleman the page names «Епифаний»; he married at his parents' wish and had children, and when his wife and his children died he became a monk, a strict faster and a man of prayer and of brotherly love. When the patriarchal throne of Alexandria fell vacant the emperor and the whole clergy begged him to take it, and he carried the office as a pastor, teaching his flock and driving out of Alexandria the heresy of a Monothelite of Antioch the page calls «Фуллон».

At the start of his patriarchate he had the poor and destitute of the city counted, and there turned out to be more than seven thousand of them; all of them he fed daily and free. Twice a week, on Wednesday and Friday, he sat on the porch of the patriarchal cathedral and received everyone who came — settling quarrels, helping the wronged, giving alms — and three times a week he went round the hospitals. When the Persians sacked and burned Jerusalem and carried off a multitude of captives, he set aside the greater part of the church treasury to ransom them.

He never refused a man who asked. One beggar who had been given six pieces of silver on the road changed his clothes, overtook the patriarch and asked again, and was given six more; when he asked a third time and the servants moved to drive him off, John ordered twelve given him, saying «Не Христос ли искушает меня?» — is it not Christ who is testing me? He had his own coffin made and forbade the workmen to finish it, telling them to come to him on every feast day and ask whether it was time.

Illness made him leave the see at the last and withdraw to Cyprus, and on the ship a shining man appeared to him in sleep and said «Царь царей зовет тебя к Себе». He reached Amathus, the city he was born in, and there died in peace. The day page carries a troparion and a kontakion to him, and they are now in this folder with the English typed here.

The Ohrid Prologue gives him the longest entry of this day, a family and two scenes. He was born on Cyprus into a princely family, the son of a prince, and brought up from childhood as a true Christian; at his parents' insistence he married and had children, and by God's providence both the children and the wife passed out of this life before him. Famous for his mercy and piety, he was chosen patriarch of Alexandria in the reign of the emperor Heraclius, and governed that church ten years as a true shepherd, keeping it from the heathen and from heretics.

He was a pattern of meekness, mercy and love of men, and the entry gives one of his sayings: if you want nobility, he said, do not look for it in blood but in virtue, for that is the true nobility. All the saints were distinguished by mercy, it says, but he had given himself wholly to that one wonderful virtue.

Two scenes carry it. Serving the Liturgy once, he remembered the words of Christ about leaving your gift at the altar if you remember that your brother has something against you, and remembered that one of the clergy standing there in the church bore him ill will; he left the holy gifts at once, went to that cleric, fell at his feet and begged forgiveness, and only when he was reconciled with the man did he return to the altar. Another time, on his way to church, a poor widow met him and began to tell him of her misery; his attendants grew weary of the length of the woman's complaint and told the bishop to hurry on to the service and hear her after it, and he answered them: and how will God hear me now, if I do not hear her? He would not move from the spot until he had heard her complaint to the end.

When the Persians fell upon Egypt he took ship to get away from the trouble, fell ill on the voyage, and reaching Cyprus died in his own birthplace in 620. That year is inside the 616-to-620 interval this folder already holds and is not a reason to close it. The entry carries his relics further than any page the folder cites: first to Constantinople, then to Buda and Pest, and at last to Pressburg.
