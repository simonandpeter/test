# John the Merciful of Alexandria

Doxologia's life makes him the son of Epiphanius the governor, a man of Cypriot stock, brought up from his youth in good learning, with the fear of God rooted in his heart as the beginning of wisdom. Grown to full age, he was pressed by his parents to take a wife, and had children by her. Then he was bereaved first of his sons, and afterwards his wife followed them; and the page reads this as God's own will, that John should be loosed from the bondage of the flesh and receive the spiritual life.

Left free, he gave himself the more diligently to prayer and to whatever pleased God, and above all to mercy towards everyone who suffered need and poverty, so that he was honoured not only by his equals but by the emperor. When the throne of the Patriarchate of Alexandria stood without a shepherd, the emperor Heraclius raised him to it, and he bowed, unwilling, to receive the ordination.

His first care was to purge the flock of the heresy then being sown by one Peter, surnamed Fullo and the Fuller, a false patriarch of Antioch, who had dared to add words of blasphemy to the Thrice-Holy Hymn — «Sfinte Dumnezeule, Sfinte Tare, Sfinte fără de moarte, Care Te-ai răstignit pentru noi, miluiește-ne pe noi» — as though the Godhead had suffered in our Lord Jesus Christ. Having put that blasphemy away, he turned to the commandments of God and to the good of the poor.

At the beginning of his shepherding he called the stewards of the church and told them to walk the whole city and write down his lords. When they asked who his lords were, he answered that those they called poor and needy were his lords, for they could bring him to the eternal dwellings and give him every kind of help towards salvation. They wrote down seven thousand five hundred of them, found in the streets and in the poorhouses, and he appointed to each a daily ration out of the churches' goods.

When the Persians warred upon Syria and burned the Holy City of Jerusalem, carrying off the honourable wood of the Cross and many Christians into captivity, he sent ships with wheat and with gold to ransom the prisoners. Because not everyone could come to him freely, he set aside two days in the week, Wednesday and Friday, and stood in the doors of the churches with certain honourable men, hearing each man's complaint and judging the quarrels that arose between brothers.

If a day passed in which nobody had come or asked anything of him, he went home in tears, saying that the humble John had gained nothing and had brought nothing to God for his own sins; and his friend the blessed Sophronius would comfort him, saying that he ought rather to rejoice, for his sheep were living in peace, without persecution and without quarrel, like the angels of God.

He was buried in the city of his own country, in the house of Tychon the wonderworker, between the bodies of two bishops already lying there; and the page says that when they went to lay him beside them, those holy bodies moved as though alive and drew apart to make room for him in the middle, and all who were present glorified God. The life ends with a woman who could not for shame confess a grave sin, and gave it to him written and sealed. Five days later he died, the letter unopened and nothing said of it.

Thinking her sin now known to everyone, she came to his grave and cried out to him as to a living man for three days, until on the third night he came out of the tomb with the two bishops and gave her back the sealed paper. She found her own seal whole, and opened it, and her writing was erased; in its place stood the words «Pentru Ioan, robul Meu, s-a șters păcatul tău» — for John My servant, thy sin is blotted out. The page gives no year for his birth or for his death.
