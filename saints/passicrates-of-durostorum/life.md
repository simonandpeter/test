# Passicrates of Durostorum

Passicrates and [Valentine](/saints/valentine-of-durostorum) were Christian soldiers serving under the governor of their place, a man the life calls Avsolan; doxologia prints one account for the two of them, opening at Rodostol, a city of Moesia, while the calendar line for the day names them of Durostorum. Seeing the people caught in the deception of the idols, and Christians who feared the torments running away and hiding, the two confessed openly and with boldness that they were Christians and cursed the lifeless idols.

Brought before the judge and pressed to offer incense, Passicrates ran up to the idol of Apollo that stood there, spat in its face and said that such was the honour that god deserved. He was loaded with heavy iron chains and thrown into prison, and the life says he wore them as though they were an emperor's gold ornaments, rejoicing that he had been counted worthy to carry such things for Christ. Valentine was brought in with him, and the two were set again before the governor.

His own brother Papian came there. Papian was a Christian, but fear of the torments had made him sacrifice, and he begged Passicrates in tears to offer incense as he had done and to pretend for a time, so as to escape. Passicrates drove him away and called him unworthy of his family, because he had departed from the faith in Christ. Then he ran alone to the shrine, put his hand into the fire, and said to the governor that this mortal body burns, as he could see, while the soul, being immortal, takes no notice of visible torments.

Valentine, questioned, answered in the same words, and both were condemned to the sword. As they were led out of the city Passicrates' mother followed, urging her son as a mother urges a son to go to his death without fear, for he was young and she was afraid he would falter; and so their heads were struck off. Passicrates was twenty-two years old and Valentine thirty, and the mother took up their bodies with joy and buried them with honour. The page gives no year.

The Greek calendar keeps the two of them on the same day and gives them ages and a year. Passicrates and [Valentine](/saints/valentine-of-durostorum) came from Durostorum in Lower Moesia — a region of the Balkan peninsula, the page explains — and were Christian soldiers at the time when the prefect was Aulozanes. Because they could not bear the worship of the idols by the heathen, they made a very sharp remonstrance to those who believed in them and confessed Christ true God; and they were arrested and brought before the governor, who urged them to offer sacrifice to Apollo.

Passicrates refused, and they bound him with chains and threw him into prison. There his own brother came to him and advised him to offer at least a little incense, so as to save himself; but Passicrates drove him away, and accepted with joy the governor's sentence that he be beheaded. Valention, when he was asked whether he agreed with what Passicrates believed, answered that he too held to his Christian faith and would never sacrifice to lifeless idols. Then both were beheaded — Passicrates at twenty-two years of age and Valention at thirty — in the year 297.

The Serbian church keeps the pair on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović names them both in its heading — „Св. муч. Пасикрат и Валентин.“ — and tells the scene at the trial. When the judge was urging [Passicrates](/saints/passicrates-of-durostorum) to offer sacrifice to the idols, and urging the martyr's brother Papian to the same — who had fallen away from Christ out of fear of the torments — then Passicrates put his hand into the fire and cried out: „тело је смртно и огњем сагорева, но душа је бесмртна и не осећа видљиве муке!“ — the body is mortal and burns in fire, but the soul is immortal and does not feel visible torments. **His mother supported and encouraged him greatly**, to hold out to the end. He was beheaded with [Valentine](/saints/valentine-of-durostorum), and both passed over into the kingdom of Christ.

What the entry adds to this folder is the whole of that scene: the hand put into the fire and the sentence shouted over it, the brother **Papian** who had apostatised out of fear and whom the judge was working on beside him, and the mother who held him to it. **Papian has no folder in this corpus**, so he is named here without a link and without a row; the mother the entry does not name.

**The year is where this book and saint.gr part, and neither folder's dates were touched.** The entry closes „око 228. год.“; saint.gr gives 297 and gives it with both their ages — «σε ηλικία, ο μεν Πασικράτης 22 ετών, ο δε Βαλεντίων 30, το έτος 297 μ.Χ.» — which is where the birth years 275 and 267 these two folders carry come from. A death in 228 cannot stand beside a birth in 275, so **widening the interval to hold both readings would make each folder say its man died before he was born**; and narrowing or displacing a sourced reading is not a reader's act either. So the dates stand as they were, the disagreement is recorded here and in the batch's commit message, and the choice between them is the author's.
