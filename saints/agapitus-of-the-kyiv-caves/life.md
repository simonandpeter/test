# Agapitus of the Kyiv Caves

Agapitus came to the great Lavra of the Caves burning to become a monk in the days of the elder [Anthony](/saints/anthony-of-the-caves), who tonsured him after the appointed time of testing. From him Agapitus learned the service of the sick and their healing with roots, with herbs and with prayer. If any brother fell ill he would leave his cell and his quiet and run to him, watch beside him, help him in whatever he needed, and nurse him until he was wholly well; and if the illness was long and the sick man lost patience and began to complain, he comforted him with many words and exhortations to endure.

Following his spiritual father's example in humility, he was granted by God the gift of healing, curing every sickness by prayer and by giving the sick to eat of the same roots he lived on himself. Word of him spread through all the land of Kyiv, and a great multitude of the sick began to come to the monastery for healing from him.

There lived then at Kyiv an Armenian physician, famous because he could see any disease, cured a great part of them, and told the sick how long they had left to live; if a man was past hope he would not undertake him. Once a gravely ill nobleman was brought to the monastery, and the Armenian foretold he would die in eight days; Agapitus prayed over him, gave him roots from his own dish, and the man was healed at once. Hearing of it, the physician's heart filled with envy and he began to accuse him before the people of imposture.

Another time the physician's servants brought in a sick man he was certain would die, for he had given him poison in the guise of medicine. Agapitus, seeing how far gone he was, brought healing roots quickly and forced them into his mouth, then fell face down and prayed hard for the poor man, and before long the dying man was full of life and rose from his bed. Blinded with fury, the physician told his servants to contrive a way to poison Agapitus himself; they came in the guise of devout pilgrims and gave him the poisoned drink, telling him it was fruit syrup. He drank it and suffered nothing.

Not long after, the prince Vladimir Vsevolod Monomakh, who was then at Chernigov, fell ill. The Armenian laboured hard and could do nothing, and the prince came to the point of death; so he sent word to John, the abbot of the Lavra of the Caves at that time, to send the saint to Chernigov. Agapitus said humbly to the abbot: if I go to the prince to heal him, I shall have to go to everyone else who has the same need; all men are equal before God. He asked a blessing not to go where human glory waited for him, having promised the Lord to avoid it to his last breath, and rather to be sent to some hidden place until the temptation passed — but if the abbot would send him to Chernigov in any case, he would obey.

The abbot, admiring his humility, did not press him, and at last the messengers were given a few healing roots; the prince took them and was well at once. Full of joy and gratitude he sent much gold in reward, which Agapitus cast away as nothing, asking that the whole of it be given to the poor. My son, he told the nobleman who brought it, I never take payment for a healing, for these are not done by my power but by the power of Christ.

Some years later Agapitus himself fell ill, and the Armenian physician visited him, pretending concern for his health, and asked with what herbs he treated his own sickness; then he said he was certain the saint had no more than three days to live, and gave his word that if he did not die in three days he would change his life and become a monk. The saint answered in anger: is this your art — instead of saving, to foretell death? Heal me if you can; and if you cannot, why do you tell me I shall die in three days? Know that the Lord has told me He will take me to Himself in three months.

Meanwhile a sick man had been brought to him from Kyiv, and Agapitus gave him some herbs; hearing the physician's mockery he turned and asked him to eat of them too. I cannot, father, the man said, for in these days we keep a strict fast and today I may eat nothing. And of what faith are you? I am an Armenian. Armenian, cried the saint — how did you dare come in here, and defile my cell with your heretical presence, and touch me? Get out, shameless man and heretic. And the physician went away ashamed.

Agapitus lived three months more, as the Lord had told him, and on 1 June fell asleep in peace, and was buried with honour in the cave of the venerable Anthony beside the other ascetics of the community. Afterwards the physician came to the abbot and told him that Agapitus had appeared to him the day before and said: you promised to renounce your heresy and become a monk; if you do not keep that promise now you will lose your soul, for God is not mocked. And indeed he passed over to the Orthodox faith, stayed in the monastery and became a monk, giving his whole life to the healing of his own soul's wounds and to unceasing prayer to the great Physician, Jesus Christ.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and adds the name of a patient. Agapitus lived in Russia about the eleventh century, saint.gr says, and lived the ascetic life in the Great Lavra of Kyiv in the days of the venerable Anthony. He was indeed beloved of God, the page says, playing on his name, and so God counted him worthy of the gift of wonderworking; his fame spread everywhere and crowds of the faithful came to him to be healed, and for that reason they called him the physician. Among the others he healed Vladimir Vsevolodovich, prince of Chernigov, who afterwards became grand prince of Kyiv with the surname Monomachos. Agapitus fell asleep in peace, and his holy relics are kept in the cave of Saint Anthony.
