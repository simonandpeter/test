# Dionysius the Rhetor

Dionysius the Rhetor and his disciple [Mitrophan](/saints/mitrophan-disciple-of-dionysius) lived towards the end of the sixteenth century and the beginning of the seventeenth, and doxologia says plainly that it is not known when or where either of them was born. Dionysius was tonsured at the famous Stoudios monastery in Constantinople, where Mitrophan was living too.

The two left the monastery and went to the Holy Mountain for a better spiritual life, settling first at a cell near Karyes, where they gave themselves to prayer, asceticism and watchfulness; and afterwards, wanting more stillness, they moved into a cave in the rocky and deserted region of Little Saint Anne. The page says here that they left before the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople, which its own dating of their lives does not allow, and the corpus records the page's words rather than resolve them.

Dionysius wrote a number of spiritual works, which are kept in the library of the Saint Anne skete. Among the papers preserved there with them, in a manuscript called the Kouvaras, is an account Mitrophan wrote down of something he heard while hearing confessions in the village of Isvoros: a grieving man whose three children had all died was shown, in a vision in which his spirit was taken from him, his children resting and rejoicing in a place of light, and when he came to himself he ate, and told the story gladly to those with him.

Dionysius fell asleep in the Lord on 6 October 1606, and his disciple followed him not long after; but the two are honoured together on 9 July. The page gives no account of his death and no place of burial.

The Greek calendar keeps the two on the same day, 9 July, and saint.gr adds the place. In the skete of Little Saint Anna on the Holy Mountain — Mikragianna, a dependency of the Great Skete of Saint Anna lying between Saint Anna and Katounakia on a rocky slope — Dionysius and his disciple [Mitrophan](/saints/mitrophan-disciple-of-dionysius) were the first known inhabitants, come from a dependency of the monastery of Stoudios at Constantinople in search of a quiet and remote place. Lettered, honoured by the Great Church with the office of Rhetor, he drew many people to himself and worked wonders for many, so that his repose was entered as something out of the ordinary in codices of two Athonite monasteries. He was a first-rate calligrapher and writer, and a book of varied matter in his own hand survives at Saint Anna under the title «Κουβαράς».
