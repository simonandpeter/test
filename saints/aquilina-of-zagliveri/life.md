# Aquilina of Zagliveri

Aquilina lived and suffered in the small town of Zagliveri near Thessalonica
in the second half of the eighteenth century, when the Turks were pressing
the Orthodox of Greece to Islam on any pretext; she was born, it is thought,
in 1746, and the house where she lived and died still stands at the edge of
the town. Her mother’s name is not recorded, but her part was beyond price:
she fed her daughter the true faith with her milk, and went on guiding her
when the father failed. He killed a Turkish neighbour in a quarrel, and
since by Turkish law a Christian who killed a Muslim could wash out the
crime only with his blood or with his faith, he accepted Islam to save his
life, the shame of his family and of the little Christian community, and a
trophy to the Turks, who pressed him constantly to bring his wife and
daughter over too — leave my daughter to me, he would answer; she is mine,
and I will make her a Turk when I choose. Her mother, as if she foresaw what
was coming, gave all her care to her only child and prayed every day that
God would enlighten and strengthen her not to follow her father. When
Aquilina was eighteen the Turks demanded that he keep his promise, and he
tried, and found her immovable: am I as faint in faith as you, she asked
him, to deny my Creator and Maker, the Lord Jesus Christ, who bore the cross
and death for us? no — I am ready for any torment and for death for the love
of Christ. He went to the Turks and said, I could not persuade my daughter;
you try. Men were sent to bring her before the court. Her mother begged her
to be brave and firm — now, beloved child, the hour has come for which I
have prepared you every day; go bravely to the sufferings that are yours to
bear, and do not deny Christ — and Aquilina answered in tears, do not fear,
mother, I will be firm, and God help me; only pray for me. The Turks led her
away, her mother following and repeating, do not deny Christ, be firm, do
not be afraid, until she was kept out at the door of the court. Bound and
brought before the judge, she was asked whether she renounced her faith, and
answered with nobility and directness, no, I will not renounce my faith or
my Lord Christ; a Christian I was born, and a Christian I will die. The
pasha had her outer clothes stripped off, and she was tied to a post and
beaten long with rods; then they tried flattery and gifts, and a rich and
powerful Turk offered her his son in marriage if she would turn, and she
refused as bravely as before. They beat her again until her shift was torn
and she stood naked under the blows, and the judge taunted her — are you not
ashamed to stand naked before so many? renounce your faith, or I will break
your bones one by one — and she answered, if I deny Christ, what am I to
believe in? what is the faith of such as you to me, dead men rotting while
you live? They beat her with rods until she was half dead, her face covered
in blood and her white body red with it, her flesh torn and her bones
broken, and when they saw that her end had come they untied her from the
post. A Christian who had watched carried her to her mother’s house. Her
mother, seeing her only daughter so torn, embraced her trembling and,
fearing that she might have weakened, asked her through tears, my child, did
you deny your faith in Christ? — and Aquilina, hardly able to form the
words, answered, what are you saying, mother? could I have gone against all
you taught me and not kept the confession of my faith? Her mother raised her
hands to heaven and glorified God, and Aquilina gave her spirit to Christ
and received the martyr’s crown, and a fragrance rose from her body that
filled the streets of the town for many days. It was 27 September 1764. The
Turks ordered her buried in the Turkish cemetery by the mosque, but a light
like a star came down from heaven and stood over her coffin, and three
Christians of the town whose names tradition keeps, Tsoplas, Kalimeris and
Bouklas, buried her secretly in a safe place and swore never to tell where,
that her relics should not fall into Turkish hands; a fine church was built
to her at Zagliveri, and for nearly two and a half centuries no one knew
where she lay. Then, in a grave illness, she appeared to Metropolitan
Nicodemus of Ierissos, in whose diocese Zagliveri lies, and told him that
her relics were buried behind the altar of the church of the Taxiarchs
Michael and Gabriel at Ossa, and on 22 May 2012 Metropolitan John of
Lagkadas presided at their finding. The Greek calendar keeps her on 27
September, and the Romanian calendar the same day; the Russian church
calendar prints her life on 27 September of the old style, 10 October, and
notes that she is not in the present Russian menologion.
