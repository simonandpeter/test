# Artemas of Cyzicus

Cyzicus, says the life, was an old and famous city in the part of Asia called Lesser Mysia, on the sea of the Hellespont that divides Asia from Europe, next to the Troad through which the feet of the teacher of the nations, the apostle Paul, had passed; and it was from Paul, the chosen vessel, that the city received the seed of the word of God. Bright as it was with the light of the faith, it had at first few Christians, because of the emperors who reigned before Constantine the Great and who sent out dreadful orders and pagan governors to force all who confessed the name of Christ to worship idols, and to punish with torment and death those who would not.

Many hid themselves then, fleeing into the mountains and the desert, and others lived among the pagans and kept their faith secret; but those who loved Christ more confessed His name openly and gave themselves up of their own will. Such, the life says, were nine brave men, unmoved in the faith as the mountain of Sion and burning with zeal for God like Elias, whose names are these: [Theognis](/saints/theognis-of-cyzicus), [Rufus](/saints/rufus-of-cyzicus), [Antipater](/saints/antipater-of-cyzicus), [Theostichus](/saints/theostichus-of-cyzicus), Artemas, [Magnus](/saints/magnus-of-cyzicus), [Theodotus](/saints/theodotus-of-cyzicus), [Thaumasius](/saints/thaumasius-of-cyzicus) and [Philemon](/saints/philemon-of-cyzicus). They were from different places and countries, and they came together at Cyzicus.

Caring nothing for the terrible imperial orders or the fear of the tyrants, they glorified Christ and preached Him with all boldness as the one God, maker and holder of all things, and they rebuked the Greek error that honoured demons instead of God, the creature instead of the Creator, the dead instead of the living. Seeing such boldness, the servants of the devil seized them like wolves taking sheep, bound them and brought them before the governor of the city.

They stood before the pagan rulers with firm souls, and were worn down with many kinds of torment and punishment, thrown into prison and brought out and tortured again, to make them deny Christ and sacrifice to the idols. They never departed from the Lord, and rebuked and shamed the rulers for their empty worship; so after further torments their heads were struck off, and their bodies were buried in that same place. The page gives no year, and says only that it was before the reign of Constantine the Great.

Some years later, when Constantine had been enlightened in baptism and the persecution had ceased, the Christians of Cyzicus took the nine bodies up out of the ground, found them uncorrupted, laid them in a new reliquary and built a church in their name. Many healings followed at the relics — a well-known man was freed of dropsy by touching the reliquary, and whoever was held by any illness was healed at a touch — and seeing them, a multitude of the unbelieving people turned to Christ, until almost the whole city had received the faith, broke its idols and raised churches in their place.
