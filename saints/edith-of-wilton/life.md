# Edith of England

Edith — Eadgith — was born in 961, the natural daughter of King Edgar by
Wulfrida, a noble lady he had ravished, for which he did seven years’
penance at Dunstan’s hands; after his wife’s death he begged Wulfrida with
great importunity to marry him, and she constantly refused and took the veil
at Wilton, where she was soon chosen abbess. Her daughter she brought up in
that community, rescued from the world before she had any taste for its
deceitful pleasures, so that the Roman Martyrology says of her that,
dedicated to God in a monastery from her tender years, she may be said
rather not to have known the world than to have left it; her tender heart
was always open to God because always a stranger to any other love, and
Wulfrida improved it with constant lessons of Christian perfection and the
examples of the saints, which Edith repaid with an admirable docility. She
was professed very young, the king her father’s consent being got with much
difficulty, and united Martha’s active life with Mary’s contemplation:
though her greatest delight was to hear her heavenly Bridegroom speak to her
heart in silence, she often gave up that pleasure to serve him in his
distressed members, fed the poor, nursed the sick and dressed their foulest
sores, preferring the lepers to the king’s children; her abstinence and
austerities were wonderful, she wore hair-cloth next her skin, and she
showed her devotion to her crucified Bridegroom by the constant use of the
sign of the cross. At fifteen her father pressed her to govern three
monasteries, so great were her virtue and discretion judged, and she humbly
refused all superiority and stayed in her own house under her mother; soon
after, Edgar died, and after the death of his son Edward the Martyr the
nobles of the martyred king’s party asked her to leave her monastery and
take the throne, and she preferred humility and obedience to a crown. She
built the church of St Denis at Wilton and asked Archbishop Dunstan to its
dedication; he was seen to weep exceedingly at the mass, and told afterwards
why — that Edith would shortly be taken out of this world into the regions
of everlasting light, while we, he said, shall still sit here below in
darkness and the shadow of death. Forty-three days after that solemnity, on
16 September 984, at twenty-three, she reposed in the Lord; Dunstan, who had
attended her in her last illness, buried her in the church of St Denis, and
William of Malmesbury in the early twelfth century says her feast was kept
with great devotion. Butler’s sources are her life in Capgrave, Malmesbury’s
books on the pontiffs and the kings of England, and the Bollandists. The
Greek synaxarion keeps her on 16 September, spelling her Edithac, with the
line that it has no details of her life.

*After Alban Butler’s Lives of the Saints (1866), volume IX, September — [St. Editha, or Eadgith, Virgin](https://www.bartleby.com/lit-hub/lives-of-the-saints/volume-ix-september/st-editha-or-eadgith-virgin), read 6 September 2026; the Greek synaxarion’s line (saint.gr, 16 September) was all this entry had until then.*
