# Seraphim (Romantsov)

Seraphim — John, the son of the peasant Roman Romantsov — was born on 28
June 1885 in the village of Voronok in Kursk province, finished the parish
school, and in August 1910, having buried his parents, came to the Glinsk
hermitage, where he was set to the bakery and a year later to the
cooperage. Sent to the kitchen and told, like every newcomer, to go to an
elder, he went and went and grew sad, and a fellow novice noticed it —
what is it, Vanya, why so glum? — and took him to his own elder, whom John
liked at once; go to your elder, that one said, and tell him everything as
it is, and if he lets you go in peace, come to me; and the first elder
said only, such things happen; since your soul is not in it, go — I want
nothing but your good; go in peace. The elders knew how to keep the peace
of a soul, their own and their neighbour’s. Taken into the army in the
First World War, he was wounded in 1916, recovered and came back to
Glinsk, was tonsured in 1919 with the name Juvenal while the revolution
raged — there was still time to learn the one needful thing, to pray —
and was ordained hierodeacon in 1920 by Bishop Paulinus (Kroshechkin).
When the hermitage was closed he went to Sukhumi and the Dormition
monastery at Dranda, which survived as a farming co-operative until 1928;
there in 1926 Bishop Nicon ordained him hieromonk and tonsured him into
the schema with the name Seraphim. In old age he remembered those years
living two together in the mountains, sharing the housework and never
letting the prayer stop — when one fell silent the other took it up under
his breath; fervent prayer, he said often, guarded me in every hard
circumstance of my life. The hermits were driven from the mountains and
their huts wrecked; in 1930 he went to Alma-Ata as watchman on an apiary,
was arrested the same year, and was sent to dig the White Sea canal. From
1934 to 1946 he was in Kirghizia, at Toktogul and then Tash-Kumyr, and
made himself a summer cell out of a rock jutting over a spring with a
wattle hut built onto it, the rock serving for table and seat; the
villagers fed him, and he came down to them, mostly by night, to serve the
vigils of Sundays and feasts, to confess and to preach, and wintered in a
pious family’s lean-to hidden behind the henhouse, with a stove, a bed and
a table.

By 1946 he was in Tashkent and could serve openly in the cathedral, and
there, it seems, he heard that Glinsk had reopened; on 30 December 1947 he
returned to it and was made its confessor — go to the confessor, was the
sentence everyone heard who crossed the threshold of the Glinsk of the
fifties and sixties, to which people came from every end of the country,
and Father Seraphim settled where each one stayed and how they helped the
house if they stayed past three days. He gave the whole of his strength,
his time and his life to the care of the brethren and the pilgrims; he had
seen too much in his day to be surprised by any sin, could feel for
everyone and still reminded everyone of the work to be done on oneself,
and to make that work a rule he copied out whole passages of Ignatius
(Brianchaninov), Theophan the Recluse and John of Kronstadt and had them
copied again by anyone who wrote a clear hand, choosing what each
correspondent needed and adding a few words of his own. His day was full
to the brim; rarely was he not asked for, not knocked for, not waited for
on the creaking stair to his round cell in the tower, and he would look
into a brother’s cell at exactly the needed moment as if by chance — a
pilgrim who had been hurt by her own confessor and was slipping out of the
gate before four in the morning met him and heard, where are you going?,
and went instead to the church, where the morning prayers were already
being read. Such things were not written down or even wondered at; he
never fussed and never lost his temper, knew how to see without seeing
what was of no use to a soul, and spoke briefly the one thing needed: to
a man worn down by writing false reports and invented percentages, your
own will not pass you by; to a monk back from town and indignant at the
shamelessness of the young — how long ago did you get back? three days?
and you still remember all that? He hated pious empty talk, the save-you
and forgive-me and bless-me said without measure or meaning; he went to
Moscow on the house’s business in a raincoat with his cassock tucked up,
by ordinary train, in a corner seat with his head bowed as if dozing, an
old man who looked like a beekeeper; and he gave advice that could be
carried out — to a nurse on night shifts who grieved that she could not
rise to pray at night, read a few short prayers and go to sleep; do not be
troubled, God asks nothing beyond one’s strength. The inner life of the
house rested on three pillars, the council of elders: the superior
[Seraphim (Amelin)](/saints/seraphim-amelin), himself, and
[Andronicus (Lukash)](/saints/andronicus-lukash). His letters, which those
who have them keep, teach nothing of his own and nothing new, only the
fathers: without humility there is no salvation; take every sorrow without
indignation, murmuring or despair, for to our time sorrows and illnesses
have been left in place of elders, and the Lord picks for each the
circumstances that are useful to him just now; inward agreement to accept
what God allows is the obedience possible in any life; and — as he
sometimes signed off — let everyone read this.

In 1961 Glinsk was closed again and the brethren scattered. He went to the
country he knew, first to Ochamchira, to a parishioner of the church at
Ilori, then to Sukhumi, where he helped the cathedral clergy, confessing
above all, and where more and more people found him; as at Glinsk he found
each a roof, and sent them on pilgrimage to the holy places of old Iberia
that the Iberians themselves had mostly forgotten — Shio-Mgvime, Sighnaghi
where Nina lies, Komany where Chrysostom died, the Iberian mountain —
going himself while he had the strength. With a monk of the closed house
who had caught the world’s pride at being a persecuted monk he was severe
in confession, and the severity sobered rather than crushed; the young who
rushed at feats he held back toward the surer virtues of patience,
humility and obedience, and warned above all against visions and
revelations, which flatter self-love and end in delusion; he blessed the
Jesus prayer to those who asked, reminding them that its heart is
repentance, and for those not inclined to fight their pride prescribed the
publican’s prayer first. He served vespers and matins wherever he could,
and a hut or a city flat became a church for the stillness in it; he sent
food and money by trusted, silent people to monasteries the local people
had forgotten. Andronicus came often from Tbilisi, where he lived in the
house of Metropolitan [Zenobius (Mazhuga)](/saints/zenobius-mazhuga),
and the two elders held the scattered Glinsk brotherhood together. In his
ninetieth year Metropolitan Elijah of Sukhumi, later Patriarch Ilia II,
made him archimandrite — Patriarch Alexy I had made him igumen in 1960 —
and he kept his clear mind to the end. In December 1975 he was taken ill
at the vigil and for two weeks read the Jesus prayer aloud from his bed,
communicating daily; in full consciousness he saw many of his brethren in
the spirit singing the stichera of the Mother of God, and sang himself in
a failing voice, taste and see that the Lord is good, alleluia — what I
prayed for all my life and sought has now opened in my heart; my soul is
so filled with grace that I cannot contain it. His last words were, now I
shall die. On 31 December he closed his eyes, and on 1 January 1976 he
died in peace, the canon at the parting of the soul read by the igumen
Isaiah, once a Glinsk novice; his body lay three days in the cathedral of
Sukhumi, and on 4 January, the Sunday before Christmas, Metropolitan
Elijah buried him, calling him a true Christian, a humble labourer and a
grace-filled man of prayer. He was buried in the Michael cemetery of
Sukhumi. On 25 March 2009 the Holy Synod of the Ukrainian Church resolved
on his canonisation, and on 21 August 2010, at Glinsk itself, he was
glorified together with Seraphim (Mazhuga) and Andronicus (Lukash), his
relics having been brought from Abkhazia with Patriarch Kirill’s blessing;
on 30 November 2017 the Council of Bishops of the Russian Church
glorified him for the whole church, with his memory on 22 September — 9
September of the old style — in the synaxis of the Glinsk fathers.
