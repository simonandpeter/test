# Nicholas Velimirovich

Nicholas Velimirovich was born on 23 December 1880 in the village of Lelić, the feast of Saint Naum of Ohrid, the eldest of nine children of devout Serbian parents, Dragomir and Katarina; all his brothers and sisters died during the Second World War. He was schooled at home, then at the monastery of Ćelije and the gymnasium at Valjevo, and in the summer holidays would climb the bell-tower of Ćelije and stay there the whole day long, reading and praying.

Turned away from the military academy as too frail, he entered the seminary of Saint Sava in Belgrade, where besides the ordinary subjects he read Shakespeare, Voltaire, Nietzsche, Marx, Pushkin, Tolstoy and Dostoevsky; his favourite author was the Montenegrin writer Njegoš, and his seminary dissertation on him astonished his teachers. He was sent abroad to study, took a doctorate at Berne in 1908 on faith in the Resurrection of Christ as the foundation of the dogmas of the apostolic Church, prepared another at Oxford, and defended a second doctorate at Geneva the same summer on the philosophy of Berkeley.

Healed in 1909, remarkably, of dysentery, he became a monk with the name of Saint Nicholas, was ordained priest on 20 December of that year, and soon afterwards archimandrite. When the First World War began he was sent on an official mission to England to win the British government's support for the Serbian people; Cambridge gave him an honorary doctorate and Glasgow another, and in the late summer of 1915 he carried his preaching to the United States. An angel, the life says, told him in a dream there that he would one day return and gather the scattered Serbian parishes of America into a diocese of their own.

On 12 March 1919 the Synod raised him, at thirty-nine, to be bishop of Žiča, the historic see of [Sava](/saints/sava-of-serbia), the first archbishop of Serbia; in 1921 he was moved to the diocese of Ohrid and Bitola, to help knit the Serbian and Macedonian churches together. He preached in American universities and churches, raised funds for those the war had struck, founded the American diocese of the Serbian Church, taught at the Saint Sava seminary in Libertyville and was dean and rector of Saint Tikhon's in South Canaan, and wrote the four volumes of the Prologue of Ohrid, besides homilies, prayers, missionary letters and a life of Saint Sava.

He had fought for the canonisation of [John of Kronstadt](/saints/john-of-kronstadt), and his own disciple, the theologian Justin Popović, later laboured for his. In 1941 he was arrested with his Patriarch, Gavrilo Dožić, and held by the Gestapo in German prisons and in the camp at Dachau until 1945. He did not stop writing there: the beatings, the gallows, the crematoria and the gas chambers were to him the proof of how far a man can fall when God goes from him, and he said that Europe had been a square lit by thousands of candles while she followed Christ, and that human desire and human wisdom had blown them out.

He fell asleep in the Lord in a foreign country in 1956, aged seventy-six, while praying in his modest cell at Saint Tikhon's Orthodox Seminary in America. In 1991, twenty-five years after his repose, his relics were brought home and laid for veneration in the monastery he had founded in his native village of Lelić. The Serbian Orthodox Church canonised him in 2003.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the fifth of March, not the eighteenth, and saint.gr adds what the Romanian page passes over. It gives his birth as the twenty-third of December 1880 in the village of Lelić in central-western Serbia, the first of nine children of the pious farmers Dragomir and Katherine; weak in body and constitution, he showed from childhood his quickness of mind, his great love for God and the Church and his inclination to the monastic life. Despite the family's great poverty he studied at the theological school of Belgrade, and was made a doctor of theology at Bern in 1908, a doctor at Oxford in 1909 and at Halle in 1911; he knew seven languages, Greek among them. He was tonsured a monk and ordained presbyter at the monastery of Rakovica near Belgrade in December 1909, having vowed, when he lay gravely ill of dysentery, that if the Lord healed him he would dedicate his whole existence to him for life.

Between 1915 and 1919 he was sent to America and England to help and strengthen the much-suffering Serbian people; in 1919 he was elected bishop of Žiča in central Serbia, and in 1920 he was moved to Ohrid, where he built up an immense missionary, pastoral, social and charitable work. For all his learning and his many gifts, the page says, he was marked by simplicity of manner, by kindness and love, and above all by humility; the study of the Fathers and his dealings with the fathers of the Holy Mountain enriched him, and under his writings and his guidance the people were reborn and monasticism flowered. In 1941 the German occupation authorities arrested and confined him, and in 1944 sent him to the concentration camp of Dachau, where he suffered dreadful tortures: the servant of the Lord bore the marks of martyrdom on his body, which had become one wound, and there was no skin left on his back or on the soles of his feet.

After his release, in May 1945, he no longer wished to return to his own country, whose regime then held him an undesirable person. So he went to America, and with his health broken carried on Christ's charitable and missionary work: he taught at the priestly school of the monastery of St Sava at Libertyville in Illinois, and from 1951 settled at the Russian monastery of St Tikhon in Pennsylvania, guiding the monks and directing the theological seminary of the house. Difficulties never discouraged him; he felt the presence of divine Providence in his life keenly, and it gave him strength, courage and joy. His prayer was unceasing and flowed like a river of paradise, and he wept easily, with tears of repentance, entreaty, intercession and doxology. Praying on a Sunday morning in the year 1956 in his humble cell, as he prepared to serve the Liturgy, he fell asleep in peace; his body was returned to Serbia in 1991.
