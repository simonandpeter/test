# Nectarius of Vryoula

Nectarius, called Nicholas at his baptism, was from the village of Vryoula in the diocese of Ephesus in Asia Minor, and he kept camels. He grew up in a poor but devout Christian family, and when he was seventeen his father died; having no trade or skill or business of his own he could not live by himself, and he took service with a rich Muslim, looking after his camels, as six other Christian lads did. When plague broke out in the country of Ephesus they sheltered in the villages round about with other camel-drivers, poor Muslims.

Alone and far from the townspeople, the young Christians began to think that no Orthodox Christian would come out of the disaster alive, and that only they, the camel-drivers, would be spared, along with their masters. We have lost our parents and have nothing left, they said among themselves; let us take the faith of the men we live with, so that we may live. So they left the Orthodox faith and submitted to Islam.

When word came that the plague was over, Nicholas and his companions went back to the city, and he was astonished and glad to find that his mother had survived. But the moment she saw him dressed as a Muslim she said, Do not come near me. What are these clothes I see you in? He turned his back as if it were nothing to him, and she went on: Go away from here quickly. I did not give birth to a Muslim. I gave birth to Nicholas, the Orthodox Christian. Go, and let me never see you again. He said nothing; but her words went into his heart like arrows, and he turned to Jesus Christ and prayed Him, fountain of mercy, to bring him back to the straight road.

He sailed to Constantinople and went on to Romania, which he had been told was an Orthodox country where he might find refuge; but Romania was not the place for him, and for reasons the page says are not known he felt his soul in greater danger there, and went back to his uncle at Smyrna. Christians who knew his state took him to a monk of the Holy Mountain, who counselled him and told him to go to Athos, where he would find his salvation. There an elder helped him grow and taught him to pray; and he began to long to give his life for the Christ he had denied, but his spiritual fathers would not release him to be a martyr, and told him to wait and to grow strong in the faith.

He went then to the venerable Stephen at the skete of Saint Anne, who received him gladly and asked him to stay; and in time, having grown in faith, he was tonsured with the name Nectarius. As a monk he lived in prayer and ascetic labour, perfecting himself as much as he could, and kept asking leave to be a martyr, and none of the elders thought him truly ready. At last the fathers who had prepared him gave him their leave and their blessing, and he left the Holy Mountain with Hadji-Stephen and returned to his native town.

Three days before Ramadan he came before the kadi and said in Turkish that he was by birth an Orthodox Christian named Nicholas; that as a young and senseless boy he had been tricked by Husein Agha with promises of cattle and money and fine clothes and choice weapons, and had denied his most sweet Jesus Christ, the true God, and become a Muslim; and that when he grew up he found the promises were lies and left that place. The kadi told him he was very young and rash, that he would suffer torments he could not bear for such boldness, and that he should go and think.

He spent the night in prayer, and on the next day, a Sunday, came again and said that he had thought as he was told, and confessed that he had been tricked like a foolish child, and that now he was grown he wept for his sin, and that he trampled their deceiving faith underfoot as a lying and beguiling thing. This time the kadi was furious and cursed him and sent him to the vali; the vali being away, he came before the bulukbaşi, who saw how young and handsome he was and spoke to him gently, and heard the same story, and sent him to prison in chains. In the prison he became the teacher of the other Orthodox prisoners in the faith, and spent the rest of his time in prayer.

The leading Muslims of the place went in the meantime to the vali and told him it was not good that the young man should live, since he had shown himself a rebel and shamed their faith by his obstinacy. The vali handed Nectarius over to them to be taken to the kadi and sentenced, and they dragged him before him; and the sentence given, he was taken at once to the place of execution, where an Arab executioner was waiting. To frighten him the man struck him several times with the sword without much hurting him, and Nectarius stood untroubled; at last he struck hard and took off his head.

His body was thrown into a dry well, with stones and other things piled over it so that the Orthodox should not reach it. His icon is in the church of the holy martyr George at Nea Ionia outside Athens, where the faithful keep his feast every year. So Nectarius the camel-driver of Vryoula, in Ephesus in Asia Minor, suffered martyrdom for the love of Jesus Christ on 11 July 1820. The page names its book: Fr Nomikos Michael Vaporis, Witnesses for Christ: the lives of the Orthodox New Martyrs under Turkish rule, 1437–1860, in the Romanian of Constantin Făgețan, Sophia, Bucharest, 2002, pages 380 to 386.
