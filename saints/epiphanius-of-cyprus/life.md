# Epiphanius of Cyprus

Epiphanius was a Phoenician, from the country about Eleutheropolis, some three stadia from the town. His father worked the land and his mother's trade was the weaving of linen; there were two children, Epiphanius and a sister, Calitropi. The father died when the boy was about ten and the mother was left to feed them both. The life doxologia prints is the long one of his disciple Polybius, who speaks throughout in the first person; what follows is a part of it only.

It opens with a donkey. The family had one beast and it was vicious, and the mother sent Epiphanius to sell it in the market so that they could buy food. In the market the animal grew orderly and mild. A Jewish merchant named Jacob asked whether it was for sale, and learning that the boy was of his own people warned him that they should set an honest price and take no sin upon themselves, for he who blesses is blessed and he who curses is cursed. At that Epiphanius was afraid and refused to sell him a beast with a vice, and told him why — his father dead, his mother and sister hungry. Jacob marvelled at the answer, gave him three pieces of silver as a blessing and told him to keep the donkey if it mended and to put it out if it did not. On the road home the beast threw him and he lay on the ground unable to rise, and a Christian named Cleobius felt the hip where it had struck him, signed him three times with the cross, and he stood up unhurt.

A year later, having no living from their little land, his mother placed him with Tryphon, a Jewish teacher of the law who had property in the village and had known the family. Tryphon took him as a son, taught him the law and the Hebrew learning, and meant to give him his only daughter. The daughter died, then Tryphon, and the whole estate came to Epiphanius; his mother died also, and he brought his sister into the house. Going out to the village he met a Christian named Lucian, a monk who wrote a fine hand and earned his bread by copying, giving away what was left over.

Lucian's words humbled him. He brought the monk into the house, showed him all that was in it and said: all this is mine, and I wish to become a Christian and to live the monastic life; this is my sister; what do you command? Lucian answered that he could not live monastically while he held all that: he should marry his sister and give her what she needed, and then he would be able. Epiphanius asked to be made a Christian first, and Lucian said he could not do it without the bishop's knowledge.

The bishop sent word that Lucian should teach him the faith. As Epiphanius came to the outer doors of the church, the shoe fell from his left foot on the first step, and as he went on the other fell from his right, and both lay outside the threshold; he did not stop for them but went in barefoot, with such eagerness did he come to God. The bishop, watching from his seat, saw his face glorified and a crown set upon his head, and after the Gospel baptised him and his sister, Lucian standing as their father in the holy illumination.

After the seven days in the bishop's house Epiphanius gave a thousand pieces of silver and his sister into the keeping of the holy virgin Bernice, who had charge of other virgins; he sold everything else and gave it to those in need, keeping forty pieces to buy the divine and life-giving books, and went out of the city with Lucian, who had made himself a monastery of ten monks who lived by copying. He was sixteen years old when he became a monk. Under Hilarion, who was second in that house and took its leadership when Lucian died, he learned the Scriptures.

The life then runs very long: a journey into Persia where he confronted the king and his magicians and raised a dead youth at the palace gate; healings, exorcisms and disputations with philosophers across Phoenicia, Egypt and the upper Thebaid; the driving out of a lion from a desert place; and years in the monastery of Spanidrion.

When the bishops met to choose a bishop they remembered him, and sent a young monk named Polybius to see whether he was still at Spanidrion and to tell nobody. Epiphanius saw the errand through him at once, told him that lying is an evil thing, and had him send his beast back to the priests. He that leads led Epiphanius instead to Cyprus. There the old bishop Papus, who had been told in a flash of light and a voice to go down to the market and find a monk buying grapes whose face was like the Prophet Elisha's, had him held at the altar and ordained him deacon, then priest, then bishop, all in one service. Epiphanius wept; and Papus said that he was being made to look a fool by telling it, but that he had been sent.

He grew very old in that office. Before the end he was drawn to the imperial city. Eudoxia, wife of the emperor Arcadius, and Theophilus, archbishop of Alexandria, were working the guiltless banishment of [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom), and Theophilus had written to Epiphanius many times that John shared the ideas of Origen; Epiphanius gave way to him at once, not knowing that Theophilus hated the man. In the city he ordained a deacon in one of the monasteries that would not receive John, and letters passed between the two bishops; and the empress heard that they were at odds.

Then Eudoxia called him and said: the whole empire of the Romans is mine, and all the priesthood of the churches under my empire is yours; make bishop whomever God shows you, and put John out of the Church. Epiphanius answered that if John were charged and convicted of the heresy she spoke of and would not confess that he had erred, he was unworthy of the priesthood and they might command what they would; but if they wished to drive him out for an insult to themselves, Epiphanius would not agree to it — for it is the nature of emperors to be insulted and to forgive, since they too have an Emperor in heaven who forgives them. She threatened to open the temples of the idols and make the last things worse than the first, and wept with bitterness. He said: daughter, I am guiltless of this judgement.

Word ran through the city all the same that Epiphanius had gone in to the empress and banished John, and his own disciple Isaac left him for it and hid in a monastery; three days of persuasion brought him back. Each bishop wrote the other a hard word — John, that Epiphanius would not sit again upon his throne; Epiphanius, that John would not reach the place of his exile. Polybius sets all this down, he says, so that nobody may reproach Epiphanius for it: could a man who had done so much good come down to such a depth of malice? Asked his age by the emperor Arcadius on the day they took ship, Epiphanius answered that he was a hundred and fifteen years old less three months, that he had been made bishop at sixty, and that he had been fifty-five years less three months in the episcopate.

He died at sea. A dead man aboard the ship was carried to him and laid at his feet, and at the touch of them rose; and fear fell on everyone in the ship. When they came to Constantia, to the place called Dianeutirion, the sailors went round the city crying to the men who lived there to come down to the sea and take the honoured relics of their father Epiphanius, for he had finished his human life. Darkness fell over the whole city, and the people went down to the shore holding one another by the hand and making great lamentation, and the country people came in weeping along the road. Three blind men of the district called Stornichi set out with nobody to give them a hand, and one of them, Prosexius, prayed: holy one of God, venerable Epiphanius, make us see, that we too may venerate your honoured relics. They saw at once, came into the city, venerated the relics and told what had happened to them on the way.

The relics were taken from the ship and laid in the church with much honour, and wonders were worked at them, the sick being healed of whatever illness held them. After ten days bishops, priests, abbots and a countless people from the whole island gathered and buried them with honour in that church.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same civil day and puts years and a bibliography around him. saint.gr has him born in 310 to a very poor family of Jewish farmers at the village of Vesandouke near Eleutheropolis in Palestine, with one other child in the house, his sister [Callitropos](/saints/callitropos), whom the same day keeps; a Cypriot popular tradition, the page adds, has him born instead at Kalopanagiotis in the Marathasa. Orphaned at ten, he was drawn to Christ by two monks famous for learning and asceticism, Lucian and Hilarion, and seven days after his baptism he settled his sister in a women's monastery and left for the desert of Palestine.

He was made bishop of Constantia in Cyprus in 367 — reaching the island, the page says, when a storm drove the ship that was carrying him to Palestine there — and in 381 he sat with four other Cypriot bishops at the Second Ecumenical Council. He died at sea on 12 May 403, returning from Constantinople to Constantia, after thirty-six years as bishop, and Leo VI the Wise later brought his relic to Constantinople. Of his writing the page names the Panarion, the Ankyrotos, On Measures and Weights, and the treatise on the twelve stones of Aaron's vestments; of his wonders, the healing of the Persian king's daughter, the raising of a Persian nobleman's dead child, and the cure of the emperor Theodosius the Great of a paralysis of the legs.
