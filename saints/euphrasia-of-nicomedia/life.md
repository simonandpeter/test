# Euphrasia of Nicomedia

Euphrasia was of the city of Nicomedia, in the reign of Maximian, of a well-known family, beautiful, of good manners and a faithful servant of Christ. Taken by the idolaters, she was pressed to sacrifice to the demons, and when she would not she was cruelly beaten and bore it manfully; and then she was handed over for outrage to a barbarian, who took her away to his house, while she prayed without ceasing to her most pure Bridegroom, the Lord Christ, to keep her virginity.

When the man had shut himself in the chamber with her, she asked him to wait a little and promised to give him a herb which, carried about him, would let no weapon of his adversaries touch him — for she was said to be a worker of charms. He said she should give him the herb afterwards; but she answered that such a herb cannot be pulled up by women, only by unwedded virgins, and that unless a pure virgin shows it, it does nothing at all. So he let her go, and she gathered some herbs in the garden and brought them to him.

He asked how he was to know that what she said was true, and she laid the herbs on her own neck and told him to strike at it with all his strength. Believing her, he took his sword to the head of the holy virgin and swung it hard and cut it off; and then, knowing that she had made a mockery of him, he gnashed his teeth — but to no purpose, says the life, for the wise virgin went pure to her Bridegroom Christ, leaving a wonderful pattern of chastity, choosing rather to die than to lose her virginity.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and tells the same story. saint.gr says she came of Nicomedia in Asia Minor and lived in the years of the emperor Maximian, whose reign it dates 285 to 305; that she was of a distinguished family and was marked out for her chastity and her upright character; and that when she was denounced as a believer in Christ and told to deny him and sacrifice to the idols, she stayed firm and unshaken. Its account of the end is the one the epigram over her turns on — «Ψεύδει σοφῷ φυγοῦσα σαρκὸς τὴν ὕβριν, Ἀθλεῖς ἀληθῶς ἐκ ξίφους Εὐφρασία»: by a wise falsehood she escaped the outrage of the flesh, telling the man set over her to strike her neck to prove what she had promised him, and he, believing it, raised his sword and struck the harder. So her plan succeeded: her head was cut off by the executioner's sword, but she kept her purity and received the crown of witness.
