# Nina (Kuznetsova)

She was born on 28 December 1887 in the village of Лаль in the Arkhangelsk province — now a town, the calendar says, in the Vyatka region — the only child of a police constable, Алексей Кузнецов, and his wife Анна, who loved her beyond measure. They dreamed of marrying her, but from childhood she loved nothing but prayer, monasteries and spiritual books. Her father, seeing where she leaned, decided that salvation along the ways of family life would not be good for her and that it would be unwise to stand in the way of what she wanted; he gave her a barn, built shelves in it with his own hands, and began buying her spiritual books.

So she came to have a rich library, and there was no greater comfort for her than reading. She knew many prayers by heart and read the Psalter from memory, and she began at that time to take in wanderers and the destitute.

In 1932 the authorities arrested her parents, who were already old; they did not survive the hardships of confinement and died soon after. The officers meant to arrest her with them, but during the arrest the shock of it struck her with paralysis, and from then on she could barely walk and had almost no use of her right hand — when she had to make the sign of the cross she always helped herself with the left.

Had the illness not come she would have been sentenced too; as it was they held her a month in the prison at Kotlas and sent her home, and for the same reason left her the house and everything in it. She used them as well as they could be used. It was a large house with an enormous kitchen where twenty could sleep on the shelf above the stove and five on the stove itself, and the big room was filled with people, mostly women whose husbands had been arrested and whose property had been taken. They all came to her and found shelter and food; she settled them down for the night herself, which her illness made no easy thing.

After the monastery at Коряжма was closed at the beginning of the revolution its brethren moved to Лальск, and a monastery of twelve men formed there. Under the church, in what had been a storeroom, they laid a stove, cut two small windows and partitioned the place into two cells; they lived there and served in the town cathedral, keeping the monastic rule whole both in their lives and in the services. There were no monasteries left in the Russian north, and here was one.

Its superior was the abbot Павел (Хотемов), who had walked to Kyiv as a boy with the village women and understood at the relics of the venerable fathers in the caves what the monastic way was, and who prayed every day for the women who took him. When that house too was closed in 1928, part of the brethren — among them Павел and the treasurer Нифонт — found shelter in her house.

She kept the monastic rule strictly. She slept four hours and stood up with the monks at prayer at two in the morning without fail; she never drank tea or milk, never ate sugar or anything good, and her whole daily food for many years was rusks soaked in water — this in a house where the samovar never left the table and people sat around it eating and drinking, where the yard was full of horses because travellers stopped there too, needing neither payment nor directions, since anyone would point out the house of the constable's daughter. She never sat at the table but on a block of wood in the corner by the stove, and she never slept in a bed: she lay in the corner under the washstand, pulled the blanket over her head with her crippled hands, curled up and slept.

She was at every service, settled somewhere in the choir and pretending to sleep; but the moment anyone faltered she gave voice and read what came next, because she knew the services by heart. The abbot's sight was poor, and he would open the door from the altar and ask her from there which readings of the Apostle and the Gospel were appointed, and she answered at once and was never wrong.

In the early thirties the authorities ordered the cathedral at Лальск closed, and she wrote decided letters to Moscow, gathered and sent delegates, and acted so firmly and so persistently that they had to give way and return it.

At the beginning of 1937 the NKVD arrested the priest Леонид Истомин, the psalm-reader Андрей Мелентьев, the churchwarden, the singers, many of the parishioners and the last priests still at liberty. On 31 October 1937 they arrested her, and found no charge against her: they kept her half a month in the prison at Лальск, asking nothing and charging nothing.

They pressed many people to bear false witness and only one agreed, the deputy chairman of the village soviet, who testified that she had organised the meetings that wrecked the closing of the church in the summer of 1936 and again in August 1937, collecting signatures and giving her own house for the purpose, and that she had at once begun petitioning for the arrested psalm-reader. On that testimony she was charged in the middle of November and questioned, and she did not admit herself guilty before the Soviet power.

What to do with a cripple whose very keeping in prison was awkward, and whose standing among the people made it frightening, they did not know; the next day after the questioning she was sent to the prison at Kotlas, and on 23 November 1937 a troika of the NKVD sentenced her to eight years in a corrective labour camp. She was sent to one of the camps of the Arkhangelsk region and did not stay long there: she died in the camp on 14 May 1938. She was numbered among the New Martyrs and Confessors of Russia at the Jubilee Council of Bishops in August 2000.
