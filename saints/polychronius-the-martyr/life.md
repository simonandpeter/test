# Polychronius

Polychronius was born in the province the Romanian life calls Gamfanita, the son of a ploughman named Vardenie, who taught him his letters and sent him to school with the other children. Water was far from the house, and at the boy's prayer a well of very good water sprang up in his father's yard; the life says he had great understanding and great self-restraint even then.

Grown, he joined the men who worked the vines and went to Constantinople. He worked with the other labourers, but ate and drank only every second or third day, so that the owner of the vineyard marvelled at him, gave him a sum of gold and sent him home with the words: go to your house and pray to God for me. The man kept the saint's hoe for love of the faith, and the life says many miracles were worked through it.

With the gold Polychronius built a church. He was present at the Council held at Nicaea as a reader, and was afterwards made deacon and priest. When the great Constantine died the teaching of Arius began to take hold of many, and Polychronius held firmly to the right faith and worked always to strengthen it. The heretics, eaten by envy, found him standing at the altar, fell on him without warning and cut him down with their swords, mingling his blood with the holy blood, and so sent him against their own will as a sacrifice to God.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 7 October, and gives him a life the Romanian line did not. saint.gr makes him a vigorous cleric of the fourth century, out of the province of Gamphanitis, son of a farmer named Vardanios who saw to it that his son was taught letters as well as the faith; while the father tended his vines the son tended the Lord's vineyard, and he was a formidable debater who more than once shamed the pagan philosophers. He went up to Constantinople, was made deacon and then presbyter, and did not stop teaching.

When Constantius, the Arian-minded son of Constantine the Great, became emperor, Polychronius stayed with those clergy who were unshaken by the exiles and persecutions the heretical emperor imposed; and the Arians took it on themselves to be rid of him. One day, while he was serving, a band of them burst into the church and cut his throat upon the holy table. The same calendar keeps his ten fellow contenders on the same day — [Parmenias](/saints/parmenias-companion-of-polychronius), [Polyteleios](/saints/polyteleios-companion-of-polychronius), [Elymas](/saints/elymas-companion-of-polychronius), [Mokios](/saints/mokios-companion-of-polychronius), [Chrysotelis](/saints/chrysotelis-companion-of-polychronius), [Maximus](/saints/maximus-companion-of-polychronius), [Luke](/saints/luke-companion-of-polychronius), [Abdias](/saints/abdias-companion-of-polychronius), [Semnios](/saints/semnios-companion-of-polychronius) and [Olympiadas](/saints/olympiadas-companion-of-polychronius) — and says of them only that no details of their lives are known.

The Russian calendar keeps him on its own 7 October, «Мч. Полихрония пресвитера (IV)», which agrees with the fourth century the Greek page gives, and its index of names heads him «Полихроний, пресвитер, сщмч.». The life behind the line fills the story out in a direction the Greek does not go.

He was a farmer's son, brought up to love work and in Christian piety. Grown up, he left his parents' house for Constantinople and went to labour in one of the rich vineyards there, and the owner of the vineyard marvelled at the young man's industry and ascetic life. For his good work he was given a great deal of money, and with it he built a church; soon afterwards he was ordained presbyter. By tradition, the page says, he took part in the acts of the First Ecumenical Council. He was killed by heretics — Arians — in the sanctuary of a church, in the fourth century.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 7 October, and names him by the same obscure province the other three books name — he was born in the eparchy of Agathanis, which the Greek page calls Gamphanitis and the Romanian life Gamfanita — of peasant parents. As a young man he hired himself out in the vineyard of a certain man of Constantinople, and even as a hired labourer gave himself day and night to fasting and prayer. His master, seeing a life angelic in its purity and self-restraint, marvelled at him and paid him far more money than he had earned; and with it he built a church.

On one point this book tells the story differently. At the time of the Council of Nicaea he was a reader, and it was the zeal he showed there in pleading the cause of Orthodoxy against the Arians that led to his being ordained priest — where the Romanian life makes him deacon and then priest after the Council and the Greek has him ordained at Constantinople, this account knows no diaconate and makes Nicaea itself the cause. Afterwards those same spiteful heretics fell on him out of revenge in the church itself and cut him to pieces; and so, the book ends, this great defender of the truth and the purity of Orthodoxy suffered, and received the crown of glory from his most glorious Lord.
