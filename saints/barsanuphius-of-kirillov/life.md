# Barsanuphius

Barsanuphius — Basil Pavlovich Lebedev — was born in 1871 in the village of
Starukhino in the Belanino district of Borovichi uyezd, Novgorod province,
one of a psalmist’s eight children; his father Paul died when he was seven
and the eldest child fourteen, and their mother Agrafena, left without
means, would not have known the next day what to feed them had a kind man
not sent a sack of rye flour. He early knew want: taken into the Borovichi
church school at the state’s expense, with nothing from home, he often had
before lessons a piece of bread and a cup of cold water — which bred in him
not envy but abstinence, a hardened will, mercy and compassion. Free from
childhood of worldly amusements, his greatest happiness was to go with his
mother among the pilgrims of the plain Russian people to the elders, most
often to the hermitage the people called the Forgotten Parents, in a deep
pine forest near his village — an old chapel of the great martyr Paraskeva
beside a cemetery whose names were forgotten, where in the second half of
the century the hermit Peter, whom the pilgrims called Petrusha, lived a
strict life, prayed with those who came and heard their troubles; the
journeys, the prayer in the forest and the talks with the hermit went deep,
and when he died early in the new century a path was always trodden to his
grave. At the Novgorod seminary his habit of work and his freedom from the
passions of youth won him the affection of his fellows, who called him
gently Vasya Lebedev. The teacher of the history of the schism, the priest
Demetrius Sperovsky — tonsured while Basil was in the fifth class, and later
archbishop of Staraya Russa — decided his path: he gave his free hours to
the old printed books, by the end of the fifth class far outstripped his
class in knowledge of the schism, and in the last class, while his fellows
discovered secular literature, sat up nights over the thick leather folios,
and in the class disputations sometimes took the Old Believers’ side so
successfully that the defenders of Orthodoxy were routed. At the final
examination Archbishop Theognost (Lebedev) questioned him for forty-five
minutes and, finding his knowledge so great, sent him for the summer to
Moscow to the archimandrite Paul of Prussia, superior of the Nikolsky
Edinoverie monastery, to be prepared for mission, and on his return he was
made assistant to the diocesan missionary for the Krestsy, Valdai and
Demyansk districts.

On 1 April 1895, Holy Saturday, he was tonsured Barsanuphius in the Meeting
church of the Antoniev Dymsky monastery — a rare thing for a seminary
graduate then — and the superior, Archimandrite Michael (Temnorusov), handed
him to the elder Anthony, whose word to him proved a prophecy: you have
chosen the road the Saviour himself walked on earth and left to his
apostles, the road of self-denying, disinterested preaching of the word of
God — a great road but not an easy one, not strewn with roses; with a staff
in your hand and a bag on your back you must walk the face of the earth as
the holy apostles did; a peasant’s smoky hut and a wayside inn await you,
privations, sorrows, troubles and misfortunes; your word will often come
back empty, people will read your holiest intentions the wrong way, and the
time may come when they drive you from their villages with reproaches and
cruel words, or even take up stones; but do not lose heart then, brother
Barsanuphius, do not fall helpless under the cross you have taken on your
shoulders; not one of the saints fell under its weight or fled the field,
and the Lord who gave them strength will give it you. On 9 April he was
ordained hierodeacon, on 30 July 1895 hieromonk, and made missionary of the
same districts, and gave himself wholly to the work for twenty-two years:
the schismatic villages lay hundreds of versts apart with no roads, but he
set himself how many times to visit each and kept to it, and it soon bore
fruit — the fanaticism of the schism ebbed, interest woke in the local
monuments of Orthodox antiquity, conversions grew more frequent, and in 1903
almost the whole village of Rovno in the Staraya Russa district came over.
He held the general talks and went into the schismatics’ houses to talk and
leave booklets; he learned the Edinoverie rite, its chants and its
pronunciation so well that he beat his opponents with their own weapons and
drew many of the old faith to those services; he never mocked the people’s
ignorance as some over-zealous missionaries did, seeing beneath it a burning
interest in the questions of faith and salvation, and shaped his talks to
leave neither gloating triumph on one side nor insult on the other but
sorrow for erring brothers and, in the others, a sense or at least a doubt
of their own rightness; his own speech took on the colour of the people’s,
and in that love and understanding of the people’s soul lay the secret of a
respect that grew even among the Church’s enemies. He went to the
all-Russian missionary congresses at Kazan in 1897 and Kiev in 1908 and the
Edinoverie congress at Petrograd in 1912, and, wise as a serpent and simple
as a dove, even got himself into the first all-Russian Pomorian Old Believer
council in Moscow in 1909. From 1908 he spoke regularly at the religious and
moral readings in Novgorod with Bishop Andronicus (Nikolsky) of Tikhvin and
the hieromonk Peter (Zverev) before a hall always full; on 10 February 1909
the Synod confirmed him diocesan missionary-preacher with the rank of
archimandrite, and that year he published his Talks with the Novgorod
Pashkovite-Baptist sectarians. The mission grew until it needed standing
courses for the clergy and then for devout laymen on the sects, the schism,
unbelief and living preaching, held from 1911 in almost every district with
his own work as the model, and the diocese’s first missionary congress met
on 4 September 1912; at the Tikhvin congress of December 1913, where the
missionaries answered questions till late into the night, a priest thanked
him in everyone’s name for his interesting, simple, heartfelt and living
lectures. He was the builder of many Edinoverie churches, in which after
their consecration he served solemnly by the old books, a preaching in
itself, and he built and founded a skete on the site of the Forgotten
Parents, attached to the Antoniev monastery of Novgorod, with churches of
the great martyr Paraskeva and of Anthony the Roman, where on Paraskeva’s
feast the crowds could not get into the churches and the vigil was sung
under the open sky; remembering his own poverty he helped poor seminarists,
and when the war came gave regularly for the wounded and for soldiers’
families.

The archbishop of Novgorod tried to have him made a vicar; he took not one
step to hasten it, but when Archbishop Arseny (Stadnitsky) had the see of
Kirillov prepared for him he saw in it God’s mercy — it did not tear him
from the mission, left in his charge, nor from his native Novgorod land. He
was named on 7 January 1917 and consecrated bishop of Kirillov, vicar of
Novgorod, on the 8th; in his word at the naming he said he knew well how
high a believing flock looks to its bishop, and knew from his missionary
years how sharply the Church’s enemies and many of her weak members watch
every act and word of a bishop, and how a fault seen in one of them is
carried by the enemies onto the whole Church — see what your Church does! —
so that fear and trembling troubled his soul, but did not bring him to
despair, since he had never in anything relied on his own strength, for I am
a man, and would rely here too on God’s mercy and help. He served in
Novgorod until the end of January and then went to Kirillov, where his last
period began: a severe ascetic life, and preaching through the churches and
monasteries of the vicariate, where the news of his coming drew crowds who
wanted the gracious word of the archpastor they had come to love. In the
troubles that followed the emperor’s abdication and the Bolshevik seizure of
power he called the people without cease to faith and to the life of the
Church, called children and adults alike to learn the truths of faith in the
house of God and condemned the driving of the Law of God from the schools,
and, seeing the Orthodox position worsen, founded at Kirillov a Brotherhood
of Orthodox men and women — for the raising of the religious spirit and
morals of the people, the keeping of the purity of the faith, the guarding
of the church’s holy things and property as the possession of the whole
believing people, and in particular of the local shrine, the
Kirillo-Belozersky monastery; by readings and talks, the religious teaching
of the young, every moral means in the spirit of the Gospel for the defence
of the persecuted Church, works of charity, the care of old churches, icons
and vessels, and the drawing in of true sons of the Church. That brotherhood
was the charge on which he died. Twenty kilometres away lay the Ferapontov
convent, whose priest [John
Ivanov](/saints/john-presbyter-martyr-1918-2-september) was arrested on 9
May 1918 after the parish drove out a commission come to inventory the
convent’s property, and whose abbess [Seraphima
(Sulimova)](/saints/seraphima-abbess-martyr-1918) was held from 12 May on a
charge of inciting the peasants; the bishop had the priest’s case
investigated and his innocence shown, and the dean petitioned, in vain. On
Saturday 14 September the bishop and the archaeologist Alexander Anisimov
were at the Goritsy convent looking at its antiquities, and the bishop
hurried back to be at the vigil and serve the Liturgy next day; three versts
along the road a post-cart with two Red Guards met them, who had already
been to the monastery and left a detachment there. Are you Barsanuphius? — I
am — and one sat facing him and the other behind the hood, in a heavy
silence he broke: are you the commissar? — no, an instructor of the Red
Guard; what have you arrested me for? — here is the paper: Bishop
Barsanuphius is to be arrested and taken to prison. Anisimov murmured that
arrests were commonplace now, and the bishop said, if they shoot me, what is
to be done. The hood was lowered as they entered the town; children crossed
themselves at the sight of the bishop under guard — look, they cross
themselves! — the guard behind laughed like a demon. He told his companion
to get down at the holy gate, and to the offer of linen or food answered,
send me the confessor, the hieromonk Adrian. At the holy gate of the
Kirillo-Belozersky monastery he was made to get down, prayed toward the
chapel at the gate and looked toward the town cathedral, and walked under
guard through the mud to the prison half a verst off, where he found the
abbess Seraphima and the other prisoners. They said that in such times an
arrest might end in shooting; I am not afraid of a violent death, he
answered, but I do not dare think the Lord would find me worthy of a
martyr’s end — and spent the night in prayer, singing psalms, some said, or
the whole vigil.

On Sunday 15 September 1918, about five in the morning, the bishop, the
abbess and four laymen — Nicholas Burlakov, Anatoly Barashkov, Michael
Trubnikov and Philip Maryshev — were led out of the prison toward Goritsy by
a squad of twenty executioners. The early Liturgy was beginning in the
monastery, and the bishop’s request to go in and communicate was refused;
passing the metochion of the Filippo-Irapsky hermitage he wished to cross
himself before the icon of Philip over its door and a guard struck his hand
with a rifle-butt, and when he quickened his pace they mocked him: do not
hurry, you will get to the kingdom of heaven in time. They went by the old
road of Cyril’s day along the shore of Lake Siverskoye, the bishop first in
his klobuk with his staff, the abbess almost beside him, the laymen behind —
he firm in the knowledge that the decisive hour had come, the hour of
release from earthly bonds to be with Christ, she not yet believing they
would be killed without trial and thinking they were being taken to Goritsy
for the steamer; a prayerful quiet and the peace of Christ came down on
their souls, until at the verst-post that read five versts to Goritsy and
two to Kirillov the executioners turned them off the road to the right, and
there was no more doubt. Here is our Golgotha, said the bishop; the abbess
swayed and he held her up — mother, take heart; you are a person of the
Church; we must go to death without fear, as to a wedding feast, with
gladness; the time will come when we shall be envied — and her weakness
passed; one of the condemned began to speak sharply of those who had
condemned them, and the bishop stopped him: after the Saviour’s example we
must forgive everyone everything; we must pass into the other life at peace
with all. They were stood facing Zolotukha hill with their backs to the
monastery, the bishop between Seraphima on his right and Trubnikov on his
left. Every evening after compline the abbess had bowed to the ground before
her sisters and said, forgive me, accursed; now she turned to the killers
and said quietly, forgive me, accursed — they took it that she called them
accursed, and fired and killed her; then five volleys one after another, and
all were dead but the bishop, who stood praying with his hands raised to
heaven, reading the prayer for the departing, and when he had said amen
heard an executioner shout, put your hands down! — I have finished, he said;
you finish too — and turned to the monastery, blessed it, and lowered his
hands, and a shot at point-blank range felled him. Merchants were made to
dig the grave; the bodies were laid in with great care, the bishop nearest
the monastery, then Burlakov, then the abbess, with Barashkov and Maryshev
at their feet. The bishop lay on his back with closed eyes, hands and feet
stretched out under his clothes, his klobuk on his head and the chain of his
panagia on his breast. Hearing the shots at dawn, many ran to the place, and
the Red Guards they met mocked them: run, run! yours has risen! in three
years he will turn up as relics! Twice the brethren got leave to dig up the
bodies at night and twice were stopped, once by Red Guards firing in the air
— we will shoot all the Volkovs; what is the executive committee to us! —
and once by a written ban; on the evening of 17 September, behind closed
gates, the retired bishop Misail (Krylov) sang the funeral for the murdered
in their absence, and Bishop Alexis (Simansky) of Tikhvin ordered the
bishop’s name into every synodicon of the Novgorod diocese. The Cherepovets
chairman’s answer to the diocese’s envoy was: allow you to carry the body to
the monastery? never! you will want to make relics of him next! The place
was honoured for many years by the Orthodox who came to pray there; in the
1960s the authorities, seeing that the veneration did not wane, destroyed
every sign of the grave and built farm buildings on it, and on 17 December
1998 a cross was raised on the place of execution. Bishop Alexis (Simansky)
of Tikhvin wrote to the Novgorod council that God’s will had been done
concerning Bishop Barsanuphius, that for his devout life, his zeal and
firmness in the monastic labour, his meekness and guilelessness and his zeal
for Christ’s Church the Lord had given him the greatest reward while still
on earth, to be counted among the chosen and crowned a martyr — his life was
honourable and his falling asleep with the saints — and served the memorial
Liturgy for him in the Sophia cathedral on the ninth day; the Kirillov
chairman told the diocese’s envoy that the bishop had been shot by a
punitive detachment from Cherepovets that came with a ready order, and that
the dead man had been terrible while he lived and was not terrible to them
dead. He was glorified as a locally venerated saint of the Vologda diocese
in 1999, and with those who suffered with him — the priest [John
Ivanov](/saints/john-presbyter-martyr-1918-2-september), the abbess
[Seraphima (Sulimova)](/saints/seraphima-abbess-martyr-1918), and the
martyrs [Nicholas Burlakov](/saints/nicholas-martyr-1918-2-september),
[Anatoly Barashkov](/saints/anatoly-martyr-1918), [Michael
Trubnikov](/saints/michael-martyr-1918-2-september) and [Philip
Maryshev](/saints/philip-martyr-1918) — was numbered among the new martyrs
and confessors of Russia by the jubilee Council of Bishops of August 2000,
for veneration by the whole church; the Russian calendar keeps the seven
together on 2 September of the old style, 15 September. The life is by
Igumen Damascene (Orlovsky). The calendar keeps him also on 26 January with
the new martyrs, and in the synaxes of the saints of Vologda and Novgorod.
