# Thalaleus the Physician

Thalaleus was from the parts of Phoenicia, from a village called Libanon Dasos, and of a distinguished family: his father Verouchios was a hierarch of the Christians. Raised in the teaching and counsel of the Lord, he learned the holy Scriptures, and when he was grown he chose to study medicine, as the craft most needed by men and most useful to them. He found a skilled and God-fearing doctor and learned it completely in a short time, being quick by nature, and he was a very devout young man.

His house stood open to every man, stranger or neighbour, rich or poor, but his mercy was greatest for the poor, whose weaknesses and pains he counted as his own. To spare them the walk he would take them on his own shoulders and carry them home, and there stand before them like a servant. He did not wait for those who needed him but went to them, and gave health as a gift, without silver and without presents; and out of humility he washed the sores of the sick himself. Living so, he was given the apostolic gifts as well, and healed every sickness by the name of Christ alone, without doctors and without plasters.

Numerian was then reigning, wholly given over to the error of the idols, and he had loosed a great persecution and sent his governors out with terrible orders. Tiberian, hearing that Thalaleus preached the name of Christ, had him brought and cruelly beaten, then let him go, taking him for a man out of his wits, and ordered him out of the province. He went into the parts of Cilicia and preached there under cover of his doctoring, until servants of the idols denounced him to Theodore, the governor of the coast by the Aegean, as a Galilean who deceived the common people with a lying craft.

Brought before him in the temple of Hadrian and asked his place, his name and his trade, he answered: my place is Phoenicia, I am the son of free parents, I am called Thalaleus, my craft is medicine, and by faith I am a Christian and a servant of Jesus Christ. Asked how he came there, he said he walked from place to place to preach faith in Jesus Christ and to wipe out the error of the idols. The governor ordered his ankles bored through and the man hung head downwards; but the soldiers, their minds changed by the power of God, bored a piece of wood and hung that instead, thinking it was he.

Seeing the wood hanging there and supposing himself mocked, the governor punished those soldiers and set others to beat the martyr with ox sinews. They wounded his whole body and could not move his faith at all, and he bore the blows as though he felt nothing. Asked whether he healed by sorcery or by the power of the gods, he said he was no sorcerer and healed by no god of theirs — deaf, lifeless, senseless idols — but by the name of Jesus Christ and the sign of the life-giving Cross, and told the governor to ask those who had been healed.

At last the tyrant had him thrown to four fierce lions to be torn apart, and God tamed them like lambs, so that they stood by the martyr fawning on him with their tails and playing. Ashamed and at a loss, the governor took up his pen, wrote the sentence that his head be cut off, threw it down in anger, rose from the court and fled. So Thalaleus was made perfect on the twentieth day of May and took the crown of martyrdom.

Many others, seeing his courage, his endurance and his miracles, believed in the Lord and died a martyr's death confessing Him — among them, the life says, the blessed man who had been Thalaleus's own teacher in medicine, a woodcutter named Asterius, the soldier Alexander, and Sterona, Philergius, Timothy, Theodoula and Macaria, and many more.
