# Jonah

The Romanian synaxarion's life of the prophet Jonah begins before his own memory does. It makes him the son of the widow of Sarepta - the boy who died as a child and was raised by the prophet Elijah, who stretched himself over the body three times and cried out to God until the soul returned. The child brought back from death would later spend three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, and the life reads the one through the other: the raising prefigured the fish, and the fish prefigured the Resurrection of Christ.

Grown, Jonah received the gift of prophecy, and the word of the Lord sent him to Nineveh, the great city, to preach its overthrow. He fled instead, taking ship in the other direction - reasoning, as the life has it, that if the city repented and was spared, he would be left a prophet whose word had failed. The storm, the lot that fell on him, the fish that swallowed him at the Lord's command: he prayed in its belly with his hands stretched out in the form of a cross, and on the third day it set him on the shore. He went then to Nineveh and preached, and the city repented in sackcloth from the king down, and God spared it - which grieved Jonah exactly as he had feared, until God taught him by the withered gourd that a city of thousands is worth more pity than a plant.

Ashamed, the life says, to live among his own people as the prophet whose prophecy God had revoked, he did not return home but took his mother into the land of Assyria and settled there among strangers. He buried her in Lebanon, and died himself in the land of Assyria, eight hundred years before the birth of Christ, and was buried in the cave of Kenez.

The Greek calendar gives his father as Amathi and his homeland as Gethhepher, of the tribe of Zebulun, and has him live under the kings Amaziah and Jeroboam; it was Jonah, it says, who by God’s prompting encouraged Jeroboam to the war against the ruler of Syria that ended in Israel’s victory and the restoring of its borders. It counts him fifth among the minor prophets, tells the flight and the great fish as the book does, and closes the story on the forty days’ fast by which Nineveh was spared, because repentance brings God’s goodness above his justice; and it says he died in the land of Saraar, near the oak of Deborah, and was buried in a cave.

The Russian calendar keeps him on 22 September old style and dates him to the eighth century before Christ. Its life calls him the successor of the prophet Elisha, says that his book, besides the mission to Nineveh to preach repentance (Jonah 3:3–10), carries prophecies of the fate of Israel, the Saviour’s sufferings, the desolation of Jerusalem and the end of the world, and closes on the Lord’s sign of Jonah: as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the whale, so the Son of Man would be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth, and the men of Nineveh, who repented at Jonah’s preaching, would rise in judgement against that generation (Matthew 12:40–41). A second life on the same calendar opens by naming his mother as the widow of Zarephath to whom Elijah was sent; the rest of it was not read.

The Ohrid Prologue, for the Serbian calendar, keeps him on the same day, more than eight hundred years before Christ, as the son of the widow of Zarephath whom Elijah raised from the dead, and says that his three days in the belly of the whale foreshadowed Christ’s three days in the tomb, and his deliverance from it the Lord’s resurrection; for the rest, it says, one should read his book. Its verses for the day are on Jonah and Nineveh.
