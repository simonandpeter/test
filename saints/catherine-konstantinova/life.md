# Catherine (Konstantinova)

She was born in 1887 at Savrasovo in the Solnechnogorsk parish of the Klin district of the Moscow province, the daughter of a well-to-do peasant, Gregory Konstantinov, who kept a joiner's workshop and a tea-house with a small shop beside it. In 1905 she entered as a novice the Skorbyashchensky monastery in Moscow, on Dolgorukovskaya street near the Butyrka gate, and lived there until it was closed in 1918, when she went home to Savrasovo. In 1919 her sister and her sister's husband died and left three small children orphaned; Catherine took them in and brought them up. All that time she lived outside the walls of a monastery but kept the whole monastic rule and tried to be in church as often as she could, earning her bread by quilting blankets and mending sacks for the collective farm.

In the years of the mass persecution the arrests were made not only by the security service but by the ordinary police, and the whole staff of the Solnechnogorsk district police station, their chief at their head, set about arresting the required number of enemies of the people and stopped at nothing to do it. On 23 February 1938 the chief of the station composed a report, given as though it rested on the evidence of witnesses, that Catherine Konstantinova had said: there they go running about again, some elections are wanted, what are they for, there are men in power already — let them govern for the time being, or else they are afraid of war. They have imprisoned everybody and now they are to elect new ones; there is nobody to elect, they are elected already, and they squeeze hard enough as it is. And he reported that she had talked in her own house about the young, that the communists gave them no education at all and the young would all perish like worms on a cabbage, that they had renounced the Orthodox faith, and when would the communists come to an end.

Almost as soon as the report was written the police arrested her and put her in the holding cell of their own station. Armed with the witness statements he had written himself, the investigator questioned her. The investigation had material showing that she had systematically carried on counter-revolutionary agitation against the communist party and the Soviet power, he said. She answered that she did not admit herself guilty of counter-revolutionary agitation, but that among themselves the nuns had shared their opinions, that it had been good under the tsar's power and was bad under the Soviet, and that she could not now recall what exactly they had said. There the questioning ended. On 11 March 1938 an NKVD troika sentenced her to be shot, and she was shot on 20 March 1938 and buried in an unmarked common grave at the Butovo range outside Moscow, which civil day is the Julian 7 March the calendar keeps her on.

The account does not stop there, and this is the one of the nine lives that does not. In February 1940 an inquiry was opened over two people who had been sentenced to ten years. Witnesses were called, and they testified that what the records of interrogation said they had said they had not said, and that what they had said was written down distorted. Other cases were taken up, and from them it came out that the Solnechnogorsk police had falsified the witnesses' evidence in almost all of them, after which they had not even needed to make the accused confess. The evidence in her own case had been invented, and when that was established the witnesses were questioned again. One of them said that under the Soviet power she had quilted blankets and mended sacks for the collective farm, that she was a believer and went to church every feast, and that he had never heard any counter-revolutionary activity or any anti-Soviet talk from her. A woman witness said that she was a believer and went to church often, but that she had never heard her say anything against the collective farms or voice discontent with any measure of the Soviet power, although they had worked in the farm together.

The inquiry confirmed in full that through the work of the Solnechnogorsk police some of those arrested had been shot wrongly and others had been given ten years. The chief of the station was reprimanded, two of his men were arrested for twenty days and dismissed, one more was dismissed and a fourth reprimanded. Two months later the NKVD administration set that decision aside and declared the sentence of Catherine Konstantinova to be shot correct.
