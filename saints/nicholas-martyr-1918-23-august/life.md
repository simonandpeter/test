# Nicholas (Varzhansky)

Nicholas was born on 25 November 1881 in the Ovruch district of Volhynia
province, the son of the official George Varzhansky. Like many natives of
the western provinces of Russia who had felt on themselves all the
refinement of the influence of sects and heterodoxy, he strove with his
whole heart towards Orthodoxy, understanding that outside it there is no
salvation and that if one chooses the service of the people for one’s
life’s work it must be bound up with the preaching of Christ’s word; so he
entered the seminary and then the Moscow Academy, which he finished in
1907 as a candidate of theology. In 1907 he married Zinaida, daughter of
the archpriest Neophytus Lyubimov, rector of the Resurrection church at
the Vagankovo cemetery in Moscow, a well-known missionary and preacher
with his own press for missionary and apologetic literature; common aims
drew him close to his father-in-law, and both men’s missionary and
teaching work and confession were crowned with martyrdom — Neophytus was
shot in Moscow in 1918. In 1908 he was made assistant to the
anti-sectarian missionary of the Moscow diocese and then Moscow diocesan
missionary; from 1911, without leaving the mission, he served in the
chancery of the Holy Synod and taught at the Moscow seminary, and in 1912
and 1914 was given the orders of Stanislaus and Anne of the third class.
Drunkenness, spreading widely among the workers at the start of the
century, drove people to despair and suicide, wrecked the family and the
foundations of the state, crushed faith and choked the good seed the Lord
had sown in hearts, and he went into battle with it like a brave soldier
of Christ: beyond the Semyonovskaya gate in Lefortovo, a workers’
quarter, he founded the Barnabas temperance society with its own premises
and its own church, inviting well-known professors to lecture to
audiences of up to a thousand, so that the zealous and self-denying
fighter for souls became well known among the workers — and the more the
workers freed from drink clung to him, the more he was hated by those with
an interest in spreading the vice. In ten years of unwearied missionary
labour he wrote and published some forty books and pamphlets against the
sects old and new, the Tolstoyans among them, showing from Tolstoy’s own
writings that he was in essence a pantheist denying God the Creator; and
one of the outstanding works of the time was his “Good Confession: an
Orthodox anti-sectarian catechism” of 1910, written, its preface says,
because several popular anti-sectarian courses had shown the crying need
of a catechetical textbook for everyone, one that set out a full
Christian world-view, showed that the saving Christian faith is one and
the heresies the ruinous inventions of Antichrist, gave a handy weapon
against the sectarians’ lies and was clear in exposition — there being
plenty of anti-sectarian books on single points, unsystematic collections
of texts, catechisms either indifferent to Orthodoxy or good but very
short, and learned works of little use to the people, but no popular
Orthodox catechetical textbook giving a Christian world-view. The five
thousand copies sold out in under ten months; the second edition of 1912
he dedicated “to the sincerely loved Orthodox workers of Moscow”, and in
its preface answered the one hostile review, an anonymous one in the
“Russian Banner” which called the book an “evil confession” and advised
tearing it up because it acknowledged the grace of God in the rite of
monastic tonsure: if the man who marries needs God’s grace and receives
it in the sacrament of marriage, does the seeker of chastity not need
God’s help, and not receive it in the rite of the angelic habit? I cannot
count any Christian rite graceless, or we should have “comedy acts” that
only depict something without giving grace or bringing a man nearer to
God and salvation — so I have thrown out not a line. Many wrote to thank
him: the deacon Anthony Romanenko that his manuals surpassed everything
written before and had given splendid results in his school, and the
outstanding missionary and confessor Bishop Sylvester (Olshevsky) of
Priluki that his excellent little missionary books had gone to the hearts
of his fellow-workers — God strengthen you to go on with your holy work.
He took an active part in the missionary congresses, including the fifth,
at the Bizyukov monastery in Kherson province in the summer of 1917; after
the February revolution and the coming of a godless government his work
grew ever harder. On 24 April 1917, at a lecture evening of well-known
missionaries and professors in the temperance society’s hall, with about a
thousand mostly working-class listeners, he took the chair, proposed a
prayer, and announced Professor Kuznetsov’s lecture on the unity of the
Church; the lecturer, touching the history of the Russian Church, called
the Orthodox to unite round it and remarked that the new social order
would hardly bring the people much good, and in the debate some spoke
their minds very frankly about events, with loud protests from the hall
which Nicholas rose each time to calm peaceably. The chairman of the
local soviet of workers’ and soldiers’ deputies, Stepanov, decided that
agitation for the old order was going on and sent for the militia; during
the fourth speaker an armed squad burst in, levelled revolvers at the
presidium — don’t move, you are under arrest — and Stepanov jumped on the
table and read a paper requisitioning the society’s premises for
cultural-educational purposes; amid cries that there would be shooting a
panic began, some crushing each other on the stairs while most went into
the neighbouring house church, knelt and prayed. The guards round
Varzhansky and Kuznetsov told them they had nothing against the lecture in
itself, but it distracted the people from their main business, the
revolution — you call the people into non-party church organisations, and
the people must do politics; when Russia is settled you may do this — and
when the prisoners remarked that the old regime had hindered freedom of
speech and of the Church and, strangely, this was being repeated now, the
guards who had just dispersed the audience at gunpoint answered, we are
doing no violence; all were soon released. At the Local Council of 1917 he
was secretary of the department for the external and internal mission and
church discipline. In October 1917 the Bolsheviks took the Kremlin,
shelling the Nicholas gate and damaging the wonderworking image of St
Nicholas over it; when 1 May 1918, which the new power ordered kept as an
international holiday, fell on Great Wednesday, the authorities had the
image covered with a red cloth reading “long live the May Day
international holiday” — and the image freed itself of the cloth. He
described it in a leaflet, the “Tale of the wonderworking image of the
holy hierarch Nicholas over the Nicholas gate in Moscow”, later held
against him: all night from Great Tuesday to Great Wednesday Red Square
was guarded and no one could approach; in the morning the whole red cloth
was seen to be torn so that the image was visible to all and, many
remarked, incomparably brighter than before, and it went on tearing in
strips and fell away entirely; first they tried to say the wind had torn
it, then printed that it had been hung already cut, though thousands had
seen it whole — however it was, the Lord did not permit the wonderworking
images to be covered in purple; the wonderworker Nicholas once saved us
from the Tatars and will save us now, if only we pray to him earnestly
and stand for the holy Orthodox faith and our holy things even unto
death. On 31 May 1918 he called at the flat of the archpriest
[John Vostorgov](/saints/john-vostorgov), where a search was in progress
with other guests present, was arrested there and put in the Butyrka, the
warrant being written only the next day. The charges were gathered from
the newspapers of 1917: an item of 14 June 1917 on the requisition
commission’s debate over the temperance society, where a deputation led
by the missionary Varzhansky had argued against requisition and its
supporters had called the society a hotbed of counter-revolution and open
pogrom propaganda, though the vote went against requisition — from which
the Cheka investigator concluded: a reactionary of the purest water, who
always carries on pogromist and Black Hundred agitation, his chief nest
the Barnabas temperance society. Questioned on 7 June, he said he was
thirty-seven, Moscow diocesan missionary and student of the sects, that
since the Academy he had done nothing but study the sects and preach
Orthodoxy — in preaching I never touched political questions except where
they meet church life; I knew Vostorgov as a student, when he was Moscow
diocesan missionary, being interested in religious questions, and what
parties he belonged to interested me little; the leaflet on the
wonderworking image I edited, for information and to sustain the people’s
religious feeling. Almost at once many people and bodies petitioned for
his release — the rector and board of the Moscow seminary, the union of
parish communities at the Transfiguration church, the council of united
parishes, the council of the Barnabas people’s temperance society, and
Patriarch Tikhon himself — and to all of them the investigators
Rozmirovich and Tseitlin had one answer: refused; on 14 August his wife
Zinaida asked the Supreme Revolutionary Tribunal for a personal meeting
with her husband, and was refused. On 20 June the investigator Kosarev
proposed that all the accused connected with Vostorgov, Varzhansky among
them, be shot; on 23 July the Cheka’s investigative collegium resolved to
close his case and file it; on 8 September the commission asked the
Central Executive Committee for leave to settle the cases out of court,
the case came back from the tribunal to the Cheka, and on 3 December 1918
the presidium of the collegium of the department for combating
counter-revolution sentenced him to be shot. On 6 June 1918, almost at
once after his arrest, an icon of the Mother of God “Seeker of the Lost”
had been sent to him in prison inscribed “we pray earnestly for our dear
passion-bearers”; learning of his sentence he sent it to his wife, writing
on the back: may the most pure intercessor, the Mother of Light, keep you
and shelter you under her motherly protection; pray, dear Zinochka, my
dove, to the Mother of God — she will cover your early widowhood and the
little orphans; forgive me, my dear, and pray for me. He was shot and
buried on the waste ground outside the fence of the Kalitniki cemetery in
Moscow, where his family set an inscribed iron cross, later broken, and
the place of the grave is lost. The life was compiled by the igumen
Damascene (Orlovsky); he was numbered among the saints in 2000, and the
Russian calendar keeps him on 23 August (Julian) with Bishop Ephrem and
the archpriest John Vostorgov, among the fathers of the Local Council of
1917–1918, and in the synaxes of the saints of Moscow and Volhynia and of
the new martyrs of Radonezh.
