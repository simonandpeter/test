# Eutychius of Constantinople

Eutychius came from the country of Phrygia and from a village called Divine, which he afterwards walled about with stone, building in it a great church in the name of the Forty Martyrs and setting there a community of men who kept the angelic life, so that the village should be divine in deed and not in name only; and by his care it became not a village but a strong town. His father was Alexander, a soldier, and his mother Synesia, the daughter of an honourable and virtuous priest, Hesychius, who served in the church of Augustopolis.

While Synesia carried him she saw herself one night, resting on her bed, shining in an unspeakable light, and was afraid, and wondered what it meant; and it was a sign of the spiritual light that was to be born of her, a lamp to many who were in the darkness of error. The child was baptised by that same grandfather, the presbyter Hesychius, and given to learning; and the sign of what he was to be showed in his childhood, for when the boys of his own age who studied with the presbyter wrote their names on the walls at play, calling themselves presbyters or other officers, this child wrote his dignity thus: Eutychius the patriarch.

At twelve he was sent by his parents and his grandfather to Constantinople for the higher learning, and there he went far in the outward wisdom and surpassed many; but he became convinced that the outward wisdom is not from above, as the apostle James says, but earthly and of the soul, and he turned from it. Afterwards he was an archimandrite in the country of Amasia.

In that time Justinian the Great reigned, and the bishops were called to the fifth ecumenical council. The metropolitan of Amasia was held by an illness and asked Eutychius the archimandrite to go to Constantinople to the council in his place; and as he made ready for the road he saw in a dream the finger of God stretched out over the firmament of heaven towards the mountain above his monastery. He woke wondering at it and did not understand; it meant the high honour of the patriarchate of Constantinople, to which by God's good pleasure he was to be raised.

When he came to Constantinople, the most holy patriarch Menas, who was old and near his end, saw him and said prophetically to his clergy that this man would be patriarch after him, and bade him lodge near at hand. The council had not yet gathered when Menas fell ill, full of days, and departed to the Lord; and while many of the clergy sought that honour from the emperor with gifts and go-betweens, the emperor, whose heart is in God's hands, was thinking of Eutychius, and set one of his counsellors, an honourable man named Peter, to keep the archimandrite of Amasia with honour and see that he did not slip secretly out of the city.

The emperor had a revelation about him too. All heard it and cried with one voice, He is worthy, he is worthy; and Eutychius was raised to the throne of the patriarchate in the fortieth year of his life. At the beginning of his patriarchate the holy fathers gathered at Constantinople and the fifth ecumenical council was held, at which were Vigilius the pope of old Rome, Apollinaris the patriarch of Alexandria and Domnus of Antioch.

When he had governed the Church of Christ well, the devil raised a persecution against him through heretics and their servants. After some years the emperor Justinian was won over by certain heretics, secretly deceived by them, and under the appearance of the right faith began to sow the heresy of the aphthartodocetae — those who held that the body of Christ, before his death and resurrection as well as after, was incorruptible and not subject to human sufferings. Eutychius stood against it.

After much contention on both sides the emperor, urged by the heretics and above all by the eparchs Aetherius and Addaeus, gathered an unlawful assembly of bishops of their mind, in the twelfth year of the patriarchate; and bringing lying accusations and witnesses against a man who was innocent and holy, they gave an unjust judgement and drove the good shepherd from his throne.

He returned to Amasia, and God worked many things through him there. A man of that city, Androgynus, and his wife had grief upon grief, for their children died before they were born; they came to him with tears, and he anointed them both with holy oil from the life-giving Cross, and the woman bore a son whom they called Peter, and after him another, whom the bishop told them to name John because it was in the church of Saint John that the Lord had heard their prayer; and both grew up and were their parents' heirs.

A presbyter of the church council brought him a son of fourteen, Nunechius, deaf and dumb, and the saint anointed him with holy oil and opened his hearing and loosed his tongue. A cleric named Cyril had a son of about five, likewise dumb and half dead, neither able to die nor to live, and he made him whole and speaking with a prayer. From the town of Zela they brought him a child of four, dried up, with nothing but bones and skin, who could take nothing but a little at his mother's breast, and he was healed under the saint's hands; and another child, the son of a craftsman of Amasia, was raised from the gates of death.

A woman from a village going to the city with her son of seven was met by the devil, who struck the boy in the legs so that he rolled on the ground and could not stand; his mother and the travellers carried him in their hands to the monastery, and the bishop healed him. From the women's monastery called Flavia, near Amasia, they brought him a young nun who could not receive the divine Mysteries: when the hour of communion came, fear and trembling fell on her and she cried out and turned and fled from them, and if they were given her by force she threw them up as an unbearable bitterness, for there was an unclean spirit in her.

A young man skilled in painting churches was at work in the house of Chrysatius, a citizen of Amasia, taking the old painting from the walls and setting holy icons in its place; and when he began to wipe out an old figure of the shameless Venus, the devil that was in that figure struck his hand with the cruel sickness that is called a cancer, so that it ran with matter and would not heal — and the saint healed it. He healed the hand of another young man, which the physicians had told him to cut off; and a man brought to him possessed, who was tormented every day.

When the saint signed that one with the Cross and anointed his forehead with holy oil, wounding the demon's power as with a spear of fire, the young man roared in a terrible voice and gnashed his teeth; and questioned, he confessed everything: I was a monk in the monastery of Saint John in the Acropolis, and I threw off the monastic order and went into the world, and knew a woman and sinned much with her, and God punishes me and I suffer what you see. The saint prayed earnestly for him. Another sick man he healed with a charge never to drink wine again; and a man who had sworn falsely at law and lost the sight of his eyes for a whole year came to him in repentance, confessed the sin, and was given back both healings.

In the Persian invasion many people gathered at Amasia from the towns round about that had been laid waste, and there was a very great famine; and when the stewards told him that they had nothing for those who came and not even enough for the monks, since the wheat and the flour were finished, he went in and prayed — and the loaves, shared out without ceasing, did not grow fewer but more, and the more was spent on those who came the more there was in the granary and the storeroom, so that strangers and household alike were fed through those days.

He had the gift of prophecy as well. Three years before the death of Justinian, Justin the emperor's nephew, then curopalates, was at Amasia on imperial service; and Eutychius took him aside and told him that, sinner though he was, he was a servant and priest of his God, who had made known to him that Justin would reign after his uncle's death. Justin gave thanks to God and asked the help of his prayers. Later he wrote in the same way to the count Tiberius, who was to reign after Justin, while he was in Syria: God has given you now a part of the guidance of the people, and without delay he will entrust the whole rule of the empire to you — which came to pass.

When Justinian died and his nephew Justin came to the empire, and John the scholastic, who had been made patriarch of Constantinople, ended his life, Eutychius returned to his throne after twelve years and eight months of banishment, to the unspeakable joy of the whole people, who came out to meet him by sea and by land crying, Blessed is he that comes in the name of the Lord; and he took his throne again on a Sunday.

He passed the rest of his days in peace, adorning the Church with right teaching and with miracles: a plague that fell suddenly he stilled with prayer, and healed the sick of it. Then, willing to pay the debt of nature himself, he fell ill at the feast of the bright Resurrection of Christ, and the emperor Tiberius came to visit him; and the saint prophesied to the emperor that he would follow him soon. He had sat on the throne four years and six months after his return from exile, and all the years from his birth were seventy; and he was buried with glory in the church of the Apostles, before the steps of the altar, where the relics of the apostles Andrew, Timothy and Luke lay. After his departure the emperor Tiberius lived only four months and three days and ended in peace, as the saint had said.
