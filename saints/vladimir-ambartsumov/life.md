# Vladimir (Ambartsumov)

He was born on 20 September 1892 at Saratov, the youngest child of a second marriage, and was brought up a Lutheran. His father came from Shemakha in the Baku province and was an honorary citizen of that town, one of the fellow workers of the man who founded the teaching of the deaf and dumb in Russia; his mother was a pious Lutheran girl who had come into the house to care for three orphaned children and in time became their father's wife. His daughter's memoir, which the calendar quotes at length, says that spiritual searching and an active religious life ran in both families.

He was a quick and daring boy. He once crossed the Volga at Saratov on the moving ice for a wager, and another time lay under a passing train for a bet. He loved the woods, knew the songs of the birds and could answer any of them, played the violin and the harmonium and sang well, and knew Greek, Latin, English and German. At school he fell in love with physics and especially with electricity, punched a round hole under a window with an electrical «пушка» of his own making, and gave his sister shocks until she ran from anything that glittered.

In 1911 he entered Moscow university, and in March 1913, at his mother's insistence, went to Berlin to study engineering. There he met the Christian student movement, whose work was the preaching of the word of God among the young in small circles studying the Gospel, and became an active member of it; probably he became a Baptist then. He said later: «Баптизм — это первый класс, но нельзя всю жизнь ходить первачком» — Baptism is the first form at school, but a man cannot go about in the first form all his life. He woke one morning in Berlin with the sense that he had to get home at once, and caught the last or the next to last train out of Germany into Russia before the war.

He finished his degree in physics and mathematics at Moscow, married in 1916 a girl he had met in the university's Christian circle, and in 1917 gave up a career in science to live by private lessons and work in the circle. The famine took the family to Samara at the end of 1919, where he set up about ten Christian circles of ten to fifteen people each and bought a house with the meetings in one half of it and his family in the other. He was arrested for that work in 1920, brought to Moscow, and released after five weeks on an undertaking not to leave. Back in Moscow he was elected chairman of the central committee of the Christian student circles. His wife died of food poisoning in May 1923, leaving him three children; her closest friend gave up her own life to raise them.

In 1924 the movement was forbidden, and most of its leaders submitted. He judged otherwise — that Christian teaching was needed in Russia precisely in such years — and went on illegally, in private flats, with dues and with congresses, the last of them in the summer of 1928. He escaped arrest once by accident, when the officer searching the flat he had slept in did not know that the chairman of the student movement was standing in front of him, held him all night and let him go at dawn. He walked the city from one friend's house to another and found the lights on in all of them, waited for the barbers to open, shaved off his beard and moustache, cut his hair and changed his spectacles for a pince-nez; after that he lived wholly underground, with no fixed address. His children prayed: «Господи, дай, чтобы папа опять был с бородой».

In the middle of the 1920s many Orthodox came among his friends, and a priest who had great influence on him; that priest baptised his children in 1925, and at the beginning of 1926 he was himself received into Orthodoxy. He was ordained deacon on 4 December 1927 in the cathedral at Glazov and priest on 11 December by the bishop of Izhevsk, and two weeks later was moved to the Moscow diocese as rector of the church of the Prince Vladimir in Starosadsky lane. Those who heard him said he served with a reverent exultation that took hold of the whole church and preached so that the young and the adolescent in particular were held; as a pastor, one of them wrote, he seemed to have been made first of all for them.

The Prince Vladimir church was closed in 1929. The priest of St Nicholas at the Straw Watchtower, whose flock he had looked after one summer, was arrested that October and died at Kem in February 1930, writing before his death to ask him to take the orphaned parish; he did, and served it with a priest who was himself shot at Butovo in September 1937. In the spring of 1931 the clergy were required to adopt the formula for commemorating the authorities that the provisional patriarchal synod had brought in, and to declare themselves on the Declaration of July 1927. He acknowledged the hierarchical authority of its author but doubted the canonicity of his standing as deputy locum tenens, and would neither deny it nor use the formula; so he went out of the active priesthood in September 1931 and took employment as head of a measurement group at a poultry research institute.

On the civil service he went on with the whole of the work. He guided his spiritual children, served in the houses of the closest of them, heard confessions, taught the young, and where he could not meet a man often he attached the inexperienced to those who were mature. Having been a disenfranchised man himself, he sought out the families of the repressed and organised regular help for them, giving away a large part of a good salary and holding his own children to the minimum: his daughter remembers a pair of rubber slippers somebody had given her being taken off her and sent to a family in need. He required of the better-off among his spiritual children that their help be regular, of an agreed amount and on a fixed day.

He was arrested on 5 April 1932 as an «активный участник Всесоюзной контрреволюционно-монархической организации «Истинно Православная Церковь»» and refused at the interrogation to name anybody: «Фамилии своих знакомых предпочитаю не называть, дабы не навлечь на них неприятностей». More than a hundred clergy and laity who did not commemorate the deputy locum tenens were arrested in the same case. He was sentenced in July 1932 to three years in the Northern region, and the sentence was then made conditional and he was released, on the petition of the Academy of Sciences where he was working. He went back to research, designed instruments and installations, and held certificates for his inventions.

He was arrested again in the night of 8 to 9 September 1937, at a house on the edge of a village outside Moscow. He and his son were sleeping in the shed and were talking, when the knock came, about whether the vestments had been hidden; the men followed the voices, found the vestments and produced the warrant. The investigator hunted for the holy vessels and the antimension — he had forgotten the word for it, and seized on every piece of silk asking «Где это?» The antimension was inside an old porcelain oil lamp on top of a cupboard and the vessels were in the things in the loft; the men started up the ladder to the loft, tired, and turned back half way. He asked the woman of the house to forgive him the trouble he had brought her, and she said she had known long since that he was a priest.

He was held in the Butyrka prison and interrogated on 20 and 21 September and 12 October 1937. The protocols are printed, and the calendar's own summary of them is that he named only the dead, or men beyond the reach of the authorities, or refused to answer, and said nothing that the investigators did not already know.

He admitted meetings he was confronted with and would not invent any beyond them; asked what he and another priest had talked of, he answered that they had spoken of the hard and abnormal position of the Church, that a state which had separated the Church from itself should not interfere in church affairs, and that men of the cult were sometimes innocently convicted of counter-revolution and sent to the camps. Asked his attitude to the Soviet power, he said: «Я по своим убеждениям заявляю, что советская власть есть явление временное, как всякая власть» — it is a temporary thing, as every power is.

The indictment was drawn on 2 November. On 3 November 1937 the troika of the NKVD administration for the Moscow region resolved that he be shot, and on 5 November he was shot at the Butovo firing range outside Moscow. The family were told he had been given ten years without the right of correspondence, and in 1956, on his son's enquiry, that he had died on 21 December 1942 in a camp of a kidney disease; they never kept that date. For many years his children and grandchildren prayed to the great martyr who helps men find what is lost, and in August 1989 the rehabilitation papers came; on 3 November one of his grandsons was called in and told that it was on that day in 1937 that the troika had sentenced him and on the fifth that he had been shot. He was numbered among the saints at the jubilee council of bishops in 2000.
