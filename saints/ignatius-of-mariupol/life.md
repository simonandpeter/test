# Ignatius of Mariupol

While still young he went to the Holy Mountain of Athos, where a close relative of his was living as a monk at the monastery of Vatopedi. Loving the monastic life with his whole heart, he put away all worldly vanity and was tonsured a monk with the name Ignatius, and later he was ordained priest. For his virtue he was called to shepherd a flock in a far place, under the heavy Tatar yoke, and in 1769 he was consecrated bishop of Gkotfei and Kefai — so the page spells the sees — in the Crimean peninsula.

He was a kind and tireless hierarch and won the love and respect of his flock, and for his devotion in service the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople gave him the title of archbishop and made him a member of the Holy Synod of the Ecumenical Patriarchate. His contemporaries describe him as an honest man, devout, upright, humble and blameless, a lover of vigils, gentle in appearance and angelic in bearing, with a good understanding of church affairs and with inborn gifts.

With these he became a new Moses for an enslaved flock. Weeping to God in prayer for a people held in bondage for years, and knowing that the Orthodox there were under threat of being destroyed in body and in soul, he resolved to bring the Orthodox Greeks out of the Crimea from under the Tatars into the Christian land of Azov, which was under Russian rule. He began secret talks with the Russian government and succeeded in having the Christians of the Crimea enrolled as Russian subjects.

After the Liturgy of 23 April 1778, in the cave church of the Monastery of the Dormition of the Mother of God, he urged the faithful to prepare for their departure out of the land of bondage and for a long humiliation. Trusted men carried word of the exact date to all the faithful of the peninsula; the Tatar authorities learned nothing of what was coming and so did not hinder the crowds from leaving. The people had to abandon their country, their houses and their holdings, their churches and their graveyards, and in June 1778 the long and difficult road began.

He took all that came with prayer, patience and gentleness, and used to say: "For the Church and out of the Church flows the blessing of heaven, and from it rises the success of all the works of men." Living in a stone cell he had raised six and a half kilometres from Mariupol, after a fortnight's illness, on 3 February 1786 he gave up his soul in peace to his Maker. He was buried in the church of Saint Charalampus in Mariupol, and his grave later became a place of pilgrimage, where the devout honoured with gratitude the spiritual father who had been their liberator.

In 1936, after the atheist regime was installed, the church of Saint Charalampus was destroyed; his grave was opened and his relics found, and they were later moved elsewhere. After Mariupol was freed from the Nazis the city was burned, and the relics with it — and so was fulfilled his own prophecy that his body would be burned together with the city. A small part of them was left unconsumed, and lies to this day for veneration in the church of Saint George in Mariupol. In 1977 the Ukrainian Orthodox Church recognised his sanctity and set his commemoration on 3 February.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and its page — the source doxologia's own life follows closely — puts one thing more plainly than the Romanian does: that he was born at the beginning of the eighteenth century on the island the Greeks call Θέρμη, which the page takes to be Kythnos of the Cyclades, called Thermia for its hot healing springs, and that he was of the known and pious family of Gezedinos. It gives his consecration as bishop of Gotthia and Kaphas in the Crimea in 1769, the title of archbishop and the seat on the Synod of the Ecumenical Patriarchate that were given him for his good service, and the date of the summons itself: after the Liturgy of 23 April 1778 in the cave church of the Skete of the Dormition he called the faithful to prepare for their exodus, and in June the march began, about fifty thousand Greeks leaving the Crimea behind their icon of the Panagia of Bakhchisaray. It adds that he was recognised a saint by the Orthodox Church of Ukraine in 1977.
