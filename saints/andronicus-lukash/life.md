# Andronicus (Lukash)

Andronicus — Alexis Andreevich Lukash — was born on 12 February 1889 in
the village of Luppa in the Lokhvitsa district of Poltava province, the
son of the peasant Andrew and his wife Aquilina, and was schooled in the
village parish school. In 1905, wishing to give his whole life to God, he
came to the Glinsk hermitage and asked its superior, the
schema-archimandrite [Joannicius (Gomolko)](/saints/joannicius-gomolko),
to receive him; strict, clever Father Joannicius tested the boy — you are
so young, and our obediences are heavy; how will you live? — and, seeing
his resolve, took him in. He served in the guesthouse, the laundry, the
kitchen, the garden, the apiary and at last the skete; his first elder was
Fr Aristocles (Veter), and when he left for Omsk in 1913 Alexis was given
to the strict ascetic Julian (Gagarin), who taught him to watch the
passions of the soul and to fight them with God’s help rather than act on
them; meek and humble from childhood, he gave himself wholly into his
elders’ will. In 1915 he was called up, served first at Perm and then at
the front, and was taken prisoner by the Austrians with five monks of his
platoon; three and a half years in Austria, where the prisoners were
scarcely fed and given the hardest work and many died, he prayed and took
every sorrow as from God’s hand and kept peace in his soul — we dug the
ground, he said later with his own meekness; one has to work anyway, and
God’s wonders went with us — and the guards, seeing how he worked, offered
him tobacco and marvelled that he neither smoked nor slacked. Back at the
hermitage in 1918 he worked at the mill and was made steward; he was made
a rasophore on 11 June 1920 and tonsured Andronicus in 1921, a day he
never forgot: remember the great day of your tonsure and the vows you gave
at it, he wrote to his children later; be true monks and you will never
sin. The closing of the hermitage he called a terrible event.

His strict life and unquestioning obedience drew the notice of Bishop
Paulinus (Kroshechkin), vicar of Kursk, who took him as cell-attendant and
in 1922 ordained him hierodeacon. Once in church, he told it, a woman came
to him in tears saying all the churches were shut and the bells had
stopped ringing, and he said, God willing, they will ring again — and for
those words he was sent to Kolyma in 1923 for five years. In exile he was
an orderly in the prison hospital, washing and tending the sick with such
love that the exiled Uzbeks called him mother; when the body of Bishop
Irenarchus (Sinkov) was brought in he washed it, begged the doctor for the
big coffin that had stood in the hospital for years, lined it with a white
sheet, made an omophorion of a towel, put his own cap on the bishop’s head
and a prayer-rope in his hands, and wrote to Bishop Paulinus that the Lord
had let him bury a bishop — for which Metropolitan Sergius, the locum
tenens, gave him a gold pectoral cross in 1936. Freed early by an amnesty,
he was again Paulinus’s cell-attendant, and in 1926, the bishop being then
of Mozhaisk, was ordained hieromonk in the church of the Resurrection at
Sokolniki in Moscow; a year later, gravely ill, he was given the schema,
keeping his name, for the venerable Andronicus of 13 June. In 1939 he was
sentenced and sent to Kolyma a second time: eleven months in prison
first, called out every night to be made to slander Bishop Paulinus, and
silent; stood at the wall and shot past, to frighten; the investigator
shouting that he would kill him, and once tearing the cross from his neck
and throwing it in the stove — what are you doing? I was baptised, my
mother gave me the cross, and you tear it off; struck senseless by a
hulking man who asked how long the investigator meant to bother with the
old man, and coming to in the hospital, saying he had fallen on a stone;
told to sit on a red-hot stove, and asking whether to take his boots off
first; stripped to his underclothes and shut in a man-sized box in the
corridor at fifty degrees of frost until he thought he would die there,
and led out unable to walk; asked again and again to sign papers, and
answering that he was illiterate and did not know what was written. In
the camp it was easier — no interrogations, only the riff-raff troubled
you if you fell among them; he was barrack orderly, respected by prisoners
and guards alike, and at the end of his term the camp commandant took him
into his own house to run it, where he hung up a small picture of the
Resurrection and prayed, and when reproached said he would go back to the
camp; the family loved him, the wife asked him about the spiritual life
and gave him money for the journey, and the commandant did all he could to
keep him.

On 28 September 1948 he returned to the reopened Glinsk hermitage, where
the superior and the elders met him with brotherly love, and in April 1949
Bishop Hilarion of Sumy made him dean and sacristan: he gathered the
brethren to the common labours — haymaking, wood, the garden — and was
always first at them, cheerful and quick, so that work with him was easy
and glad, and he did everything neatly and with love. The revived house
lived not in scarcity but in outright destitution, under the strict
Athonite rule Archimandrite Nectarius had restored — the services from
four in the morning, the Liturgy at nine, vespers at four, compline at
six, and little time to rest — and the brethren, most of them old men
worn by hard lives and many illnesses, rejoiced to pray, forgetting
themselves; he was their example. His soul, cleansed by many sorrows, was
full of the gifts of the Spirit; having borne all with generosity he had
kept the commandment to love his enemies and gained the greatest gift,
Christian love of neighbour, and walked always bent in humility; his
service record says he was marked by special humility, meekness and
industry, obedient and full of love. The brethren came to him first on the
business of their obediences, then to open their souls, and soon he was
the brethren’s confessor; Bishop Eustratius wrote that he had the deserved
respect of the whole house and spent all his free time in prayer. He took
no decision without prayer, prayed for his spiritual children day and
night, saw a man’s inner state without error, and led every soul to
salvation through faith in Christ; his teaching rested on the fathers, and
his notebooks gather sayings from the Paterikon and the Spiritual Meadow
with his own, which he copied out and gave to his disciples. On 5 May 1955,
with Patriarch Alexis’s blessing, Bishop Eustratius made him schema-igumen;
not only the brethren but the superior,
[Seraphim (Amelin)](/saints/seraphim-amelin), confessed to him, the
bishop wrote to him of the six years he had known him in the heavy office
of dean, filled with great zeal and as great profit to the monastery, and
in 1960 the Patriarch gave him the palitsa. When the hermitage was closed
in 1961 he moved to Tbilisi under the care of its former head,
[Zenobius (Mazhuga)](/saints/zenobius-mazhuga), metropolitan of
Tetritskaro, who loved and honoured him, and went on with his eldership in
the cathedral of Alexander Nevsky, where seekers from all over the
country came as once to Glinsk and the church was always full for his
sake; he healed the wounds of the suffering by his word and bodily
illnesses too by his prayers, and in 1963 was made archimandrite. His
letters to the Glinsk monks, part of his legacy, are full of tender
fatherly love and of strict, wise instruction: do not grieve — the Lord
knows hearts; call on Him with firm faith and He will always help; be
brave, and let your heart be strengthened amid vexing and sometimes
frightening temptations; Lord, You send me sorrows, send me patience too;
rejoice in the temptations allowed you, for by them spiritual fruit is
gained; pray often, not as I will but as You, Father; ask the Mother of
God, she never abandons anyone — entrust yourselves to her and she will
save you always and everywhere; with man it is impossible, with God all
things are possible; think of death every minute, for many lay down and
did not rise — you fall asleep, and it is eternity. Priests asked him
their questions, and his answers are on record: daily communion is
delusion, once a month with preparation and the cutting off of self-will,
save for a schemamonk, a sick monk or the priest of the week; priests
should confess more often — we confess people and do not repent
ourselves; carpets with crosses on them must go out of a church, since
the Lord was crucified on the cross and we walk on it; a Liturgy on juice
for want of wine is a sin; candles must burn on the altar and the table of
oblation, or the priest sins mortally.

In November 1973, reading the morning prayer “O God, cleanse me a sinner”,
his speech failed and his left side was paralysed; many thought he had
hours to live, but after twenty-five days his speech came back, though the
paralysis stayed, and unable to go to church he bore it without murmur,
praying at home and receiving communion daily. In the first week of the
Great Fast of 1974 he felt better and could even sing the irmos Helper and
protector — no one knew it was his last song; in the third week he stopped
eating; on Sunday 17 March he lay in a stupor from half past five in the
morning until ten at night, when he said clearly, God’s mercy will cover
everything, and began to bless someone, having been granted, it seems, to
see his departed brethren; then he came to himself, said quietly, I shall
die, closed his eyes and spoke to no one more, though he understood
everything; and on Thursday 21 March 1974, soon after five in the morning,
he gave his spirit to God in a peaceful and painless end. His body lay in
the Alexander Nevsky cathedral in Tbilisi until 26 March, with panikhidas
and the Gospel read by local and visiting priests; Archimandrite John
(Maslov) served the Presanctified Liturgy, Metropolitan Zenobius buried
him with the clergy in the presence of Bishop Gaioz of Tsilkani, and the
igumen Hilarion (Prikhodko), once of Glinsk, called him a man of wide soul
and deep faith and a great labourer whose heart was full of compassionate
love for every man, who lived not for himself but for the people — a
sorrower for men’s souls. He was laid in the Grmagele cemetery; on the
fortieth day Zenobius served the Liturgy and panikhida for him, and the
Catholicos-Patriarch David V came to the grave, laid a red egg on it and
said: Father Andronicus, Christ is risen; we know you are now in the
kingdom of heaven; what set you apart in this life? you had no enemies,
and so all loved you; your loving heart was open to all; pray for us, that
we too may share the joy of your blessedness. The Russian calendar keeps
him on 9 September of the old style, 22 September, with the synaxis of the
Glinsk fathers, and in the synaxis of the saints of Kursk.
