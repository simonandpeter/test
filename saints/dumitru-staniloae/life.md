# Dumitru Stăniloae

Dumitru Stăniloae was one of the marked figures of Orthodoxy, the Romanian calendar’s life says — professor of theology, dogmatic theologian, philosopher, historian, journalist and translator — whose name as the greatest theologian of the twentieth century was amply confirmed over his ninety years by his work and by the regard of the greatest academic institutions of the world.

He was born on 16 November 1903 at Vlădeni in Brașov county, the youngest of the five children of Irimie and Reveca. He went to the Andrei Șaguna school in Brașov, took a scholarship to the university of Cernăuți in 1922, left after a year for the Faculty of Letters at Bucharest, and in Lent 1924 met Metropolitan Nicolae Bălan, who gave him a scholarship from the metropolitan centre at Sibiu. In 1927 he took his degree in theology at Cernăuți with a thesis on the baptism of children and was sent to study at Athens, and in the autumn of 1928 he defended his doctorate at Cernăuți, on the life and work of Patriarch Dositheos of Jerusalem and his ties with the Romanian lands. Sibiu then sent him to Munich, to August Heisenberg’s seminar of Byzantine history and philology, and from January 1929 to Berlin, Paris and Belgrade, for Byzantine studies, dogmatics and the original works of Saint Gregory Palamas.

Back home he taught at the Andreian theological academy in Sibiu for seventeen years — dogmatics, apologetics, pastoral theology and Greek. He married Maria Mihu on 5 October 1930; their twins Dumitru and Maria were born on 10 May 1931, the boy dying that September and the girl in April 1945, and a third child, Lidia, was born on 8 October 1933. He was ordained deacon on 8 October 1931 and priest on 25 September 1932. From 1934 he edited the newspaper Telegraful Român for twelve years, and from June 1936 he was rector of the Sibiu academy for ten; in 1938 he published his fundamental work on the life and teaching of Saint Gregory Palamas. Under pressure from the communist authorities he had to give up the rectorship in 1946, the year the first volume of his translation of the Philokalia appeared at Sibiu, and he moved to the Faculty of Theology in Bucharest, succeeding Nichifor Crainic in the chair of ascetic and mystical theology.

From 1950 he lectured several times at the meetings of the Burning Bush without belonging to the group, and in the new wave of arrests in 1958 he was arrested on 4 September and taken to Aiud, and released only after five years, in January 1963. He worked as a clerk in the library of the Holy Synod and returned to his chair that October. From 1965 those who had censored him asked him for articles, wishing to show the West a picture of religious freedom; he lectured at Freiburg and Heidelberg in 1968 and at Oxford in 1970, and went to the Vatican in 1971 with the delegation of the Romanian Orthodox Church. He retired in 1973 but went on supervising doctoral students, while the Philokalia reached its twelfth volume in 1991. He became a corresponding member of the Romanian Academy in 1990 and a full member in 1991, received honorary titles from universities around the world, and died on 4 October 1993, aged ninety. The Romanian calendar keeps him on that day as a priest and confessor, and prints a troparion and a kontakion for him.
