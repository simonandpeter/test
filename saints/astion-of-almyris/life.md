# Astion, disciple of Epictetus

Astion was eighteen, the only son of the chief man of a city of the East, his mother a senator's daughter, and his parents, he said, looked at him every day as at a costly pearl. Walking with companions one day he came upon the cell of the priest [Epictetus](/saints/epictetus-of-almyris) and went in to see who lived there so far from men. The elder told him that his parents only looked at the pearl and did not possess it, for his soul was dearer than any pearl and Christ had chosen it for Himself, and spoke to him of the true gold tried in the fire and of the Church, the true mother who bears her children to immortality in baptism.

He went away without answering, for fear of his companions, and came back alone at dawn the next day; and when the elder asked whether the seed sown in him had sprouted, he said he feared his parents would throw themselves into the sea or lose their minds if he confessed openly. So he asked to be made a Christian in secret and then taken to a far country, that his parents' love should not stand between him and his salvation. Epictetus set him to fast and pray, taught him the faith and baptised him, and one night the two left together with a little silver for the road.

They sailed to the parts of the Scythians, came to the city of Almyris on the Danube and built a small cell nearby, and lived there as monks. Wonders were worked through Astion too: going down to the Danube for water he met a possessed man, and at the sign of the cross the devil fled crying that the purity of his heart had taken great power over them; and on another errand he found a man fallen from a high building and lying as dead, prayed, and raised him with the apostle's words, in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, rise and walk.

The page keeps one failure of his. Going out for water without asking the elder's blessing, because he was ashamed to interrupt him with guests, he was assailed on the road by unclean thoughts and struggled with them three days in shame, fasting and weeping and not confessing. Epictetus saw the grief on him and told him it was not the sorrow of the saints but the deadly sort, the sorrow of Ahithophel and of Judas; Astion then confessed, and the two lay out on the ground in the form of a cross and prayed, and he saw a black child with a lit candle fleeing from him and saying that his confession had broken their strength.

When the governor Latronian came to Almyris the idol-priests denounced them, and they were imprisoned. They would answer nothing at the tribunal but, We are Christians: that is our name, our family and our country. They were beaten, hung up, torn with iron claws and burnt with torches, boiled in a cauldron of pitch without harm, and kept thirty days without food or drink; the governor at last called them devils in the flesh, because they would give him no name, and sentenced them to the sword.

At the place of execution Epictetus asked that his disciple be killed first. Astion protested that the priest, his father and teacher, should be crowned before him; but the elder said he had kept him pure seventeen years and would not leave him alone at the end, and that Michael with the angels, Abel with the prophets, Peter with the apostles and Daniel with the confessors had come out to meet him. Then let the will of the Lord and yours be done, holy father, said Astion, and signing himself with the cross he said, You are my defender, Lord; into Your hands I give my spirit — and bowed his head and died. Epictetus fell across his body and was beheaded upon it.

His parents, Alexander and Marcellina, had set out by ship to find him when a traveller from the East told them he had seen their son suffering at Almyris. They came too late; Astion appeared to Vigilantius, a converted torturer, and told him to meet them at the shore and take them in. Vigilantius told them first that their son had gone away with an honoured elder to a farther country at the call of a glorious Emperor; then he brought them to the cell, showed them the cross and the gospel book left there, taught them, and led them to the graves. They were baptised by Evangelus, the bishop of that country, sold their goods for the poor and lived out their lives in God.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two of them on the Julian 7 July, where the Romanian calendar keeps them on the 8th — two calendars and two days, and both are recorded. Its first sentence is the pair’s own story as this corpus already had it: the presbyter Epictetus won Astion, his parents’ only son, to the faith of Christ, baptised him and made him a monk. Then, it says, they moved from somewhere in the eastern parts into the Scythian country, and settled in the Scythian town of Almyris — now Ramzina — at the outfall of the Danube into the Black Sea. They were tortured and beheaded for the faith of Christ about the year 290, a year inside the persecution the Romanian page names without dating their death in it, so the folder’s recorded interval is left standing rather than narrowed to it.

And both of them, this book ends, appeared after their death in a great light to his own parents — his mother Marcellina, and a father whose name this folder’s attestation carries and this paragraph does not, three folders of the corpus bearing it bare. They were converted to Christ and baptised by the bishop Evangelus; and he too was beheaded for Christ afterwards, and of him it was sung: Evangelus, a second angel. The bishop’s own folder stands on this same day in two other calendars, and this reading writes him no Serbian row, because the heading of the entry names the two monks and not him.
