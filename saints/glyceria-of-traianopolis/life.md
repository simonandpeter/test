# Glyceria of Traianopolis

In the first year of the reign of Antoninus, the pagan emperor of Rome, when Sabinus was governor in Greece in Europe, the Christians who lived at Traianopolis, a city of Thrace, gathered every day in a small house of prayer and asked God with much pain of heart for the peace and increase of the Church. Those who feared God were few then, and the few were in danger of banishment, for the emperor kept to the Greek error, sacrificed to the unclean idols, and sent his lawless order everywhere that whoever would not bow to them should be killed.

The order reached Thrace, and Sabinus brought it to Traianopolis and showed it to the people, and set a day, three days off, for the feast of their great god Zeus that was called the lampadophoria, the carrying of light. In that city was a maiden of good family, Glyceria, daughter of Macarius, who had once been proconsul at Rome. She had come away from Rome with her parents into those parts and had been left an orphan; believing in Christ she joined herself to the Christians, went with them daily to the house of prayer, and betrothed her virginity to her Lord. She used to say to them: brothers and sisters, fathers, and all of you who are a mother to me, take good heed whose emperor's image we bear, and with what sign we are marked upon the forehead.

When the day came and the citizens were running with lighted candles to the temple of Zeus, Glyceria kindled her heart for her Lord, signed her forehead with the sign of the Cross, and went in among them. She said to the governor that since she was of a greater family than anyone living in that city, born of a most illustrious father who had been proconsul in the great city of Rome, it fell to her to bring the sacrifice to God first. He asked her where her torch was. On my forehead, she said, is my torch, written there, and being unquenched it lights the true sacrifice we bring to God. Not understanding her, he told her to come near and sacrifice first; and she asked that all the torches be put out, since the eternal God has no need of smoking candles, so that they might see the spotless sacrifice she meant to bring.

When the torches were out she stood on a high place where the people could see her, uncovered her forehead, showed the sign of the Cross of Christ written there, and asked them whether they saw the bright torch upon it. Then she lifted her eyes and her hands and prayed to the God who had shown Himself to the holy youths in the furnace of Babylon and delivered them from the fire, who had stopped the mouths of the lions and broken the idol in the plain of Dura, that He would come and help His humble servant and break this idol made by a man's hand. At her prayer a fearful thunder sounded and the idol of Zeus, which was of stone, fell and broke into a thousand pieces.

The governor and the pagan priests, seeing it, burned with anger and told the people to stone her; and the stones did not touch her but fell about her in a great heap that ringed her round as though they had been laid there by hand. The pagans, not knowing the power of God, called her a sorceress. She was bound and taken to prison, and there the priest of God, Philocrates, came to visit her, and at her asking signed her with the Cross and said that the sign of Christ should help her to her purpose.

The next day Sabinus put her before the judgement seat, and she asked how she could bow to a god who had fallen and broken in pieces and could not help himself. He had her hung by the hair on a rack and her body torn with iron claws until she should die; she said she felt nothing and told him to find greater torments, for these were nothing, and the men who tortured her weakened. When they struck her on the face the angel of the Lord stood by her and terrified them, so that they fell down like dead men — though the governor did not see him. Then he sent her back to prison to be worn out with hunger and thirst, sealed the door with his own ring and set a strong guard that nobody should bring her food or drink; and the angels of God brought the martyr food that nobody knew of, and strengthened the bride of Christ.

Some days later, when he was to go to the city of Heraclea, he came to the prison expecting to find her dead of hunger, and found the seal whole, and her loosed from her bonds with clean bread before her in a dish and water in a cup; and the wretched man did not understand that God was feeding His servant. She was taken after him to Heraclea, where the Christians came three stadia out of the city with their bishop, the honourable Dometius, to meet her and to call her sufferings blessed; and the bishop prayed over her that Christ would be her guide in the confession of His most holy name.

The governor then ordered her thrown into a heated furnace. She fenced herself with the sign of the Cross and blessed God for giving His servant that day and that hour for an everlasting gladness; and when they threw her in, dew came down from heaven at once and put out the flame, and she stood in the furnace like a clean lamb, singing that all things are subject to God and that by His will the blaze of this fire loses its strength. She came out unharmed. The governor took that for sorcery and had the skin flayed from her head; and while they did it she thanked God that with the skin taken from her He had uncovered all that was within her.

He had her laid naked on a sharp stone in the prison, bound hand and foot. At midnight the angel of the Lord filled the prison with light, loosed her from her bonds, and healed the flayed skin back onto her body, so that there was no trace of a wound and she was whole and sound and bright of face. In the morning the keeper of the prison, whose name was Laodicius, opened the door and did not know her, and thinking she had escaped was about to kill himself for fear of the governor's torments; and she said, Laodicius, do yourself no harm, I am Glyceria. He asked her mercy, said that he believed in the God who helped her, and she told him to come after Christ and be saved. He brought her out, bound himself with her own chains, walked behind her to the governor and told him plainly what he had seen and that he wished to share this death; and the governor ordered him beheaded.

Asked at the last what it was that helped her, she answered: Christ, the Saviour of the world and the God of all comfort, who sent me food in the prison, loosed me from my bonds and made new the lost beauty of my face; He is my help. The governor gave her to the beasts. She went towards them gladly, as to a feast, and stood at the place where the beasts come out. A great lioness was let loose, roaring, and came and lay down at her feet and licked her ankles. Glyceria lifted her eyes and thanked God, who had gentled the anger of a beast to show His divine power and had turned everything hard into something light for her, and asked that she should not be left without the crown of His grace among His saints. A voice was heard from heaven saying: I have heard your prayer; come to Me in peace, for the doors of the kingdom of heaven are opened to you.

Then another lioness came and bit her, and made no wound and left no mark on her body; and so the martyr gave her holy soul into the hands of God, and the beasts went back to their den. In that same hour a terrible sickness came upon the governor: he swelled with dropsy and died in the street without reaching his own house. Bishop Dometius took up the much-suffering body of the martyr and buried it with honour in a fair place in Thrace near the city of Heraclea, and afterwards a healing myrrh flowed from her honoured relics and cured every illness.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same civil day and fills in the martyrdom act by act. saint.gr has her born at Traianopolis in the second century, when Antoninus Pius was emperor, the daughter of a man named Makarios who had been consul; she embraced Christianity young and took up a vigorous work of teaching. When the governor Sabinus heard of it he summoned her, and she came to him readily, with the Cross marked on her forehead, and confessed her faith in the Saviour boldly. He had her dragged to the idol temple to pray and sacrifice; she prayed there fervently and shattered the statue of Zeus.

The pagans dragged her out and stoned her in a fury, and not one stone touched her, so that many believed in Christ. After various tortures she was thrown into prison, where she catechised her jailer [Laodicius](/saints/laodicius-the-jailer) and brought him to the faith, and he confessed it with courage and was martyred. The tyrant, unpersuaded, ordered her tortured again and then thrown to the wild beasts; they too spared her, though one of them bit her, and of that bite she gave up her spirit to the Lord a few days later. Domitios, bishop of Heraclea, received her holy relic and laid it in a seemly place near the city, and many healings and deliverances of the possessed are attested at it. Her head line dates her death to 141, and she is named patron of Galatsi in Attica.

The Ohrid Prologue, read for the Julian 13 May, gives this folder its first year and the whole of its account. She was the daughter of some governor of Rome, it says, and when her father died and left her an orphan she settled at Traianopolis in Thrace. In the time of the impious emperor Antoninus she was brought out to offer sacrifice to the idol of Zeus. She drew a cross on her forehead; and when the emperor's deputy asked her where her lamp was — for all of them carried lamps in their hands — she showed him the cross on her forehead and said: this is my lamp. At her prayer a thunderbolt struck the idol and broke it in pieces.

The deputy was enraged, and ordered her beaten and thrown into prison; and he sealed the prison doors, resolved to kill the virgin by hunger. But an angel of God appeared to her and gave her heavenly food, so that when after some time the deputy opened the prison, thinking the martyr must have died of starvation, he was astonished to find her healthy and bright and cheerful. The jailer [Laodicius](/saints/laodicius-the-jailer), seeing that wonder, confessed Christ the Lord himself, and was beheaded at once.

After that she was cast into a fiery furnace and was left unharmed by the fire; standing in the midst of it she praised the Lord, calling to mind the wonder worked over the three youths in the furnace at Babylon. At the last she was thrown before lions, and having prayed to God this holy virgin gave up her soul to the Lord for whom she had bravely borne so many torments. She suffered, the book says, in the year 177; and from her relics there flowed a healing myrrh, by which the sick were cured of the gravest diseases.

The Russian church calendar keeps her on the same menologion day, 13 мая ст. ст., gives her two life pages and names her for Heraclea, where she died, rather than for the city she lived in. The fuller of the two tells it this way. Her father Макарий had been governor of Rome and had afterwards moved to the Thracian city of Троянополь; she lost both parents early, came to know Christians and went daily to the church. The governor Савин, ordered to make the Christians sacrifice, appointed a day for the whole city to worship the idol of Zeus; she told the Christians what she meant to do and asked their prayers, and on the day she went into the temple, stood up in the sunlight, took the covering from her head and showed them all the Cross she had traced on her forehead. She prayed, thunder was heard, and the statue fell and broke in pieces.

The stones they threw at her did not touch her. A priest named Филократ came to her in prison to strengthen her; an angel appeared among her torturers in the morning and they all fell down in terror; and the governor sealed her cell with his own ring so that nobody could reach her, and angels brought her food and drink all the while. Taken to Ираклия, where the bishop Дометий and the Christians came out to meet her with prayer, she was thrown into a heated furnace and the fire went out; the skin was flayed from her head and she was laid naked on sharp stones, and at midnight an angel healed her wounds. The first lioness loosed on her crept up and licked her feet; she asked the Lord to take her, heard a voice from heaven calling her, and a second lioness killed her without tearing her. Дометий and the Christians of Heraclea buried her, and her relics gave out a healing and sweet-smelling myrrh.

The two Russian pages do not agree with each other about when this was. The shorter opens «Пострадала за Христа около 177 г.», and the fuller opens with a persecution «во II веке во время гонения на христиан императора Антонина (138 - 161)» and then closes with the same «около 177 года» — an emperor sixteen years dead by the year his persecution is said to have ended in. The day line prints «(ок. 177)». Nothing here is chosen and the year this folder already carried is unchanged; the disagreement is the calendar's own, and the Serbian book the folder was dated from carries it too.
