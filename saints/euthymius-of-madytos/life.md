# Euthymius of Madytos

The Romanian calendar names him by his sister: «Sfântul Ierarh Eftimie, fratele Cuvioasei Parascheva de la Iași», the hierarch Euthymius, brother of Paraskeva, the patron of Iași. doxologia's page for him is a study of the sources rather than a synaxarion life, and what follows is only the part of it read here.

Its principal source is the Life and deeds of our venerable mother Petka, and how she was brought to the glorious city of Tărnovo, written by Kir Euthymius, Patriarch of Tărnovo, between 1375 and 1393, which survives in longer and shorter redactions. The shorter text, kept in a manuscript at Neamț, gives a few things about the childhood of Paraskeva and her brother in its second chapter. The two were born at Epivates, a village not far from Constantinople on the shore of the Sea of Marmara — today Selimpașa, some fifty kilometres east of Istanbul — of parents merciful, devout and steady in the commandments of God.

When their parents died, brother and sister agreed together to divide the whole inheritance among the poor. Paraskeva then began a life of harsh asceticism, and Euthymius became bishop of Madytos, an important port on the Dardanelles, on the European shore, where the strait joins the Sea of Marmara to the Aegean — today Eceabat in Turkey. He worked many miracles, and after his death his relics gave out so much myrrh that it seemed a river running down from there to the sea.

The longer redaction of the same chapter, in a manuscript from the Athonite monastery of Zographou, adds that he was sent while still young to study the Scriptures; that long afterwards, when their parents had died, he was tonsured with the name Euthymius; and that the virtue of his life made him known everywhere, so that he was ordained bishop of Madytos. The page's notes point beyond these to a Greek panegyric by George of Cyprus, an encomium on the great Euthymius bishop of Madytos, which its author had not been able to see, and to a passage from the life of Saint Euthymius the New of Madytos printed in the Evergetinos.

No year is set down here. The study's own notes raise the question — arguing from the reckoning that Paraskeva died at about twenty-seven, around the year 1050, that she and therefore her brother may belong almost a century earlier than is usually said — but that is a discussion on the page, not a date it states, and there is more of the study than has been read for this entry.

saint.gr keeps him on the same day and prints a synaxarion life where doxologia printed a study of the sources. He was born at Epibatai in Thrace at the beginning of the tenth century, it says, and his sister according to the flesh was [Paraskeva](/saints/parascheva-of-iasi) the Epibatene, whom the Greek calendar honours on 14 October. When his father Nicetas died his mother brought him to a monastery of Constantinople, where he remained thirty years and distinguished himself in the spiritual struggles; but he loved stillness, and so left the monastery and lived as a hermit in a deserted place.

He was ordained deacon by the bishop of Perinthos of that day and afterwards presbyter by another bishop, and at last was made bishop of Madytos on the Hellespont, marked out for the untiring practice of virtue and for an uncommon gift as a shepherd. God gave him the grace of wonderworking, and while he lived he worked a multitude of miracles and healed lepers and the deaf and dumb.

His fame reached the palace and the emperor Basil II, who reigned from 976 to 1025 and who came to see him at Madytos; and there the saint prophesied the emperor's victory over Bardas Phokas in 989. He fell asleep in peace between the years 989 and 996, and at his falling asleep his tomb gushed myrrh, so that many sick were healed and he was surnamed the Myrrh-flowing. The miracles God worked through him after his death are known, the page says, from the encomium written for him by George of Cyprus, later Patriarch of Constantinople, at the request of Meletios of Athens, who at that time presided over the church of Madytos and Koile — the Greek panegyric that doxologia's study named and had not been able to see.
