# Gerasimus of the Jordan

Gerasimus, the praise of hermits, was by birth from the parts of Lycia. From his youth his mind was set on God and he hedged himself about with the fear of God; and having received the monastic habit he went first into the innermost desert of the Thebaid in Egypt, where he lived for a time in spiritual labours, and then came back to Lycia, his own country.

He came into Palestine towards the end of the reign of Theodosius the Younger and settled in the desert of the Jordan, where he shone like a bright star; and there he made a monastery beside the river. In his days in Palestine, under Marcian and Pulcheria, the fourth Council of the whole world met at Chalcedon against Dioscorus, the ill-believing patriarch of Alexandria, and Eutyches the archimandrite, who said that there is but one nature in our Lord Jesus Christ.

After that council a monk named Theodosius, a sorcerer in his ways and a follower of Eutyches, came to Jerusalem and threw the whole of Palestine into confusion, deceiving not only simple people but many holy men, and the widowed empress Eudocia as well; with their help he drove the patriarch Juvenal from his throne and seized it himself, and those who stood firm in the right faith withdrew into the inner deserts.

Gerasimus too was deceived by the heresy for a time. Cyril, whom this page calls Cyril of Jerusalem, writes in the life of Euthymius the Great that the hermit lately come from Lycia had conquered the unseen demons but was tripped by the visible ones, that is, by the heretics; and that, hearing of Euthymius, he went to him in the desert called Rouva, stayed with him a long while, took in the teaching of the right faith, and cast off the heretical hurt, repenting bitterly of his former deception.

His monastery stood about thirty-five stadia from Jerusalem and one stadium from the Jordan. On Saturdays and Sundays the fathers came in to the church for the Liturgy and communed of the Holy Mysteries, and the people of Jericho, hearing how hard a life those hermits led, made it their custom to bring them what they needed on those days. Under him [Cyriacus](/saints/cyriacus-the-anchorite) lived in solitude, sent to him by Euthymius the Great; seeing him young, Gerasimus set him to obediences in the community. In the Great Fast the elder used to go into the deeper desert of Rouva.

The fathers had a mule for bringing water up from the Jordan, and the elder gave it into the charge of a lion. A soldier who had come to him for prayers took the mule away, and the lion was blamed for having eaten it; when the truth came out the elder smiled and told the brethren he had reproached the lion for nothing, and he named the lion Jordan. After the saint's death the lion would take no food but looked about him everywhere for the elder, until the father Sabbatius led him to the grave; and there the lion beat his head upon the ground, roaring aloud, and died upon it — not, the life says, because a beast has a reasoning soul, but because God willed to glorify the man who had glorified him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr adds the one turn of his life the Romanian page leaves out. It puts his birth in Lycia in the fifth century, of pious and humble parents, and says he was dedicated to God from infancy — and then that as a young man he was carried into the heresy of the Monophysites by the followers of the pseudo-patriarch Theodosius, a fanatical Egyptian monk who seized the patriarchal throne of Jerusalem while the patriarch Juvenal was away, with the help of the empress Eudocia before her own return to the orthodox doctrines, and held it for twenty months of unspeakable cruelties. Gerasimus saw his mistake quickly, the page says, because he was a man of good will and humble mind with the habit of visiting and consulting sanctified men; and from a learned ascetic named [Euthymius](/saints/euthymius-the-great) in the desert of Rouva he was taught the truth about the two natures of Christ and came back to the Church. He was tonsured in the desert of the Jordan in 451, and when many monks gathered about him for his guidance he founded a cenobitic monastery near the city of Baithagla.

saint.gr also dates what the Romanian life tells without years. It has him see, in a vision while he prayed in the Lavra, the end of [Euthymius the Great](/saints/euthymius-the-great) on 19 January 473 — reporting it from the narrative of [Cyriacus the Anchorite](/saints/cyriacus-the-anchorite), who went with him to the great saint's funeral — and puts his own repose two years later, in 475, under the patriarch Anastasius I of Jerusalem, leaving the succession of the Lavra to his fellow ascetics Stephen and Basil. A portion of his relic, it says, is at the monastery of Prousos in Eurytania. The page closes by reporting that the synaxarist of St Nicodemus puts him in the seventh century under Constantine Pogonatus, and by saying that this does not seem likely.
