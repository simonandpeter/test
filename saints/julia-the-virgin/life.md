# Julia the Virgin

When the great African city of Carthage was taken — the Romanian life says by the Persians — a crowd of captives was carried off, and among them a young girl called Julia, the daughter of a well-known man named Analson. She was taken into Syria of Palestine and sold there to a merchant who was a pagan. Although her master was an unbeliever she held firmly to the Christian faith she had been born into and to the Christian ways she had been taught in her swaddling clothes.

She prayed and fasted often, and when she had come to her full years she guarded her virginity carefully, living in chastity and much abstinence and serving her master faithfully; in all that was commanded her and was not against God she obeyed with a good will, as under God's eyes. What was against God nobody could force on her: her master urged her repeatedly to deny Christ and to live in the impurity of their pagan custom, and no threat would move her, for Julia was readier to die than to leave Christ.

He often meant to destroy her, but seeing her faithful and eager service he spared her, wondering at her good ways, her gentleness and patience, her humility and her great fasting — for she fasted every day but Saturday and Sunday, and the time her service left her she spent in fervent prayer or in the books she had learned as a child, bending only a little to sleep at night, always pale, her body thin and worn with labour and abstinence. Seeing her unchanged, he began to pity her and to love and honour her, for God turned the hard heart of the unbeliever.

When she was past twenty her master sailed for Gaul with a great cargo, and took Julia with him, since he saw his wealth multiplied through her hands as Potiphar's house in Egypt had been blessed for Joseph. Putting in at the island of Corsica he found a gathering of pagans of his own faith near the harbour, keeping a feast and sacrificing; he went to them with all his servants, bought a fat calf and offered it with them, and they fell to eating, drinking and dancing. Julia stayed in the ship, sighing from the bottom of her heart and weeping for the error and the ruin of those people.

One of the company came to the ship, saw her weeping and learned that she was a Christian, and told the chief of the gathering that a girl in the newly arrived ship blasphemed their gods. The chief asked the merchant why not all his people had come out, and offered him four of his own slave women for her, or her price in silver, saying he would force her to worship. The merchant answered that she could not be forced, that she would die sooner than leave her faith, and that he would not sell her for the man's whole estate, because his wealth multiplied in her hands and he had entrusted everything to her.

So the chief took counsel with his own and made a greater feast, and got the merchant thoroughly drunk. When he had fallen asleep, and all his people with him, they ran to the ship, brought Julia ashore and set her before their chief, who promised her freedom and her master his price if she would sacrifice. My freedom, she said, is to serve Christ, whom I serve with a clean conscience; your error sickens me. He had her struck on the face, and she answered that if her Lord had borne blows and spitting for her she would bear them for Him, and that tears should run down her face in place of the spittle.

He had her dragged by the hair, stripped and beaten bitterly over the whole body, and under the blows she cried that she confessed the One who was beaten for her, and that as her Master had borne the crown of thorns and the cross, so she, His servant, should share and follow His Passion. Then he had her virgin breasts cut off, and she bore it bravely; and wanting to destroy her before her master woke, he ordered a cross made at once and the martyr crucified on it, as the Jews had crucified Christ.

As she hung on the cross and came near her end, her master woke and saw her and was filled with grief, but could do nothing, for the bride of Christ was at her last breaths. When her soul was loosed from the body all present saw a dove whiter than snow fly from her mouth and rise toward heaven, and her torturers saw an angelic apparition beside her; great fear fell on them and they fled, leaving only her body hanging dead on the cross, which the Lord commanded His angels to keep.

Not far from Corsica is a smaller island, once called Margarit and now Gorgona, where there was a monastery of monks; an angel appeared to them and told them all about the martyr and bade them sail quickly and take her body down from the cross. They found it as the angel had said, wrapped it in clean linen, found her severed breasts cast aside by a stone a little way off and laid them back in their place, and carried her home and buried her with honour in their church, where healings of every infirmity were given from her tomb.

Wonders were done at the place of her passion as well. The Christians of Corsica built a small church there, on a cramped site on a sharp hill, in her name; and where her breasts had been thrown living and healing water ran out from under a stone, so that all the sick who drank of it or washed in it were made well. Every year on her day that stone sweated drops of milk and of blood, in witness of her virginity and her martyrdom — whitened as with milk by the purity of her virginity and reddened with the blood shed for Christ — and the drops ran from morning to evening, and those anointed with them were healed.

After many years the church grew old and began to fall, and because the place was very narrow the people wanted to build a new and larger one on a broader site not far off. When the stones, bricks and lime were ready for the foundation to be laid next morning, they found everything back at the old site, and the builders could not tell what the wonder meant. They moved the material a second time, and in the night it moved back again, and the watchmen saw a most shining maiden sitting in a cart drawn by two white oxen, carrying the material to the first place; so they knew that the saint wished her church renewed where it had stood, and did as she wished. Other wonders were done on both islands, on Corsica and on Gorgona, until her honoured relics were taken from there and moved to Brescia.

The Serbian church keeps her on the same 16 July, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović is the only one of her calendars that gives her a life at all: the Romanian line is a rank and a name, and the Greek calendar does not keep her. She was of a notable family of Carthage, it says, and when the Persians subdued Carthage — that is the book’s own sentence — many of the people were dragged off into slavery, and Julia was taken and fell into the hands of a pagan merchant in Syria. He advised her more than once to deny Christ and come over to his faith and never got her consent; and finding her faithful and trustworthy in service, he left her in peace and spoke to her of faith no more.

He loaded a ship with goods, took her with him and set out by sea for trade. When they reached Corsica there was a pagan feast, and the merchant joined in the sacrifice while Julia stayed in the ship, weeping that so many people live in stupid error and do not know the truth. The pagans learned of her, dragged her out of the ship against her own master’s protest, and tortured her: they cut off her breasts and threw them onto a stone, and then crucified her, and on the cross she gave up her spirit to God. An angel of God announced her death to monks on the nearby island Margarita, or Gorgona, and they crossed over and buried her honourably. The Prologue puts her passion in the sixth century, which is the first date of any kind this folder has carried.

The second half of the entry happens many years later, and it is about a building. The faithful wanted to raise a new church in her honour on another site, the old one being cramped and dilapidated, and they gathered the stone, the brick and the sand there. On the night before the day they meant to lay the foundation, all of it was carried by an invisible hand back to the old little church. They moved it to the new place again, and the same thing happened; and the night watchmen saw a most radiant maiden carting the material to the old church on white oxen. From this all understood that she did not wish her church built elsewhere, so they pulled the old church down and raised the new one on that same spot.
