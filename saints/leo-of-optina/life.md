# Leo of Optina

The first of the Optina elders worth remembering, the Russian life says, was Lev Nagolkin, born in the town of Karacev in the province of Oryol and named Lev at his baptism. In the world he was a hemp merchant who carried worked hemp to distant markets. Once a wolf attacked him and tore away much of one leg; being uncommonly strong and brave, he thrust his fist down the beast’s throat and crushed its jaws with his other hand, so that it fell helpless from the cart. He limped for the rest of his life.

His trading had brought him among men of every class, and he had learned their habits and their way of living. The life says that experience served him in his years as an elder, when people of every sort, known and unknown, came to him and opened their souls. He began his monastic life at the skete of Optina, moved to Beloberezhskaya, was tonsured with the name Leonid, and in 1804 succeeded its superior, Father Vasilie Kișkin.

At the monastery of Ciolnsk he met Father Theodore, a disciple of the elder Paisius Velichkovsky, and became his devoted pupil. Theodore taught him the high spiritual work, the craft of crafts, as the life calls unceasing prayer, by which the heart is cleansed of the passions. When Theodore fell ill a solitary cell was built for him two versts into the forest, and Leonid settled there with him and with Father Cleopa; there, in the silence of the wilderness, he secretly received the great schema and the name Lev.

The schema-monks went on to Valaam, where they lived six years, and drew many of the brethren to themselves by their wisdom and humility — enough that the abbot complained of them to Petersburg. A commission came, the elders were vindicated and the abbot sharply rebuked; but knowing human nature they were afraid to stay, and moved to Alexander-Svirsky. There in 1820 the emperor Alexander I, hearing from his coachman that the house had improved since Theodore and Lev came to it, turned aside to see them.

They had agreed beforehand to keep silence, so as not to stir envy among the brethren, and answered the emperor briefly and soberly until he stopped asking; he took his leave of them kindly. After Theodore died, Leonid stayed a while at Alexander-Svirsky and then at the skete of Ploshchansk, where Macarius was, and came to Optina at last in 1829. The life calls him the founder of the spiritual school out of which the whole succession of Optina elders came; the great elders [Macarius](/saints/macarius-of-optina) and Ambrose were his disciples.

Persecution followed him there. He was called a freemason, and the books of the Fathers he gave the monks — the writings of Abba Dorotheus among them — were called sorcery. Metropolitan Philaret of Kiev, who sat in the Synod, protected him and visited Optina to show him honour, and Philaret of Moscow wrote to the bishop of Kaluga: „Nu există pricină de ivire a ereziei în părintele Leonid” — there is no ground for heresy in Father Leonid.

Not long before his death the persecution fell again on the convents, upon the spiritual daughters of the Optina elders, and the nuns were driven out on untruth and ignorance; but they were vindicated before the end, so that the elder died in peace. In the first days of September 1841 he began to fail, and was ill five weeks. After grievous sufferings he departed to the Lord on 11 October 1841. The grief of the community, the life says, was beyond describing, and a great crowd carried him to the grave. The page prints no troparion for him.

The Russian calendar keeps him on its own 11 October, «Прп. Льва Оптинского (1841)», and marks the day as the Optina one; the line after his is the synaxis of all the saints who shone in that hermitage. The life it reaches calls him the first of the Optina elders and gives him a beginning doxologia.ro does not. He was Lev Danilovich Nagolkin in the world, born in 1768 in the town of Karachev in Orel province, and in his youth he was a clerk in the commercial line who travelled the whole of Russia and came to know people of every estate — the practical experience, the page says, that served him in the years of his eldership when people came to him for spiritual counsel.

In 1797 he left the world for the brotherhood of Optina under the abbot Abraham, and two years later moved to the Beloberezhsky monastery, whose superior was then the hieromonk Basil (Kishkin), an ascetic of high spiritual life. In 1801 he was tonsured into the mantle with the name Leonid, and ordained hierodeacon on 22 December of that year and hieromonk on the 24th. The page keeps one scene of those years: coming back from the haymaking, he was told by the superior to sing the all-night vigil, and went to the choir as he was, tired and hungry, and sang the whole service with one other brother.

In 1804 he became superior of the Beloberezhsky hermitage. Before that he had lived a short while in the Cholnsky monastery, where he met father Theodore, a disciple of the Moldavian elder [Paisius](/saints/paisius-of-neamt), and became his devoted pupil; Theodore taught him the higher monastic work, the prayer of the mind. Four years later he gave up the superiorship and withdrew with Theodore and father Cleopas to a quiet forest cell, and when their gifts began to draw crowds into that solitude the three went to a skete of Valaam, where they lived six years; when their life began to attract notice there too they went on to the Alexander-Svirsky monastery, where Theodore died in 1822.

In 1829 he came to Optina with six disciples, and the superior [Moses](/saints/moses-of-optina), knowing his experience, put the brotherhood and the pilgrims in his care. [Macarius](/saints/macarius-of-optina) arrived soon after — he had known Leo as a monk of the Ploshchansk hermitage and now came under his direction — and became his closest disciple, his confidant and his helper in the eldership.

He had the gift of healing among many others, and the possessed were brought to him. One of them, seeing the elder, fell down before him and cried out in a terrible voice that this grey-haired one would drive him out, that he had been in Kyiv, in Moscow and in Voronezh and nobody had driven him out, and that now he would go. The elder read a prayer over the woman and anointed her with oil from the lamp burning before the Vladimir icon of the Mother of God, and the demon went out.

That victory, the page says, was won only after the victory over his own passions: nobody saw him troubled by anger or irritation, and nobody heard a word of impatience or complaint from him. He said the Jesus prayer constantly, and outwardly among people was inwardly always with God. Asked by a disciple how he had come by such gifts, he answered: live more simply, and God will not leave you either, and will show you his mercy.

His eldership lasted twelve years. The wonders were countless, the page says, and crowds of the destitute came and surrounded him and he helped them all as he could; one hieromonk wrote that the simple folk told him of the elder that he was more to them, poor and unlearned as they were, than their own father, and that without him they would be orphans outright.

He came to the end of it with a foreboding of how near it was. In June 1841 he visited the Tikhonov hermitage, where a refectory was being built at his blessing, and said that he would not see their new refectory, that he would hardly live until the winter and would not be there again. In September he began visibly to weaken, stopped taking food and communicated every day. On the day of his death, 11 October old style, the all-night vigil was being served in honour of the memory of the fathers of the seven Ecumenical Councils.
