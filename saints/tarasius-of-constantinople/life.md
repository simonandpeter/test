# Tarasius of Constantinople

Tarasius was born at Constantinople. His father was called George and his mother Encratia, of good family and of patrician rank. When he had grown and had gone through the whole wisdom of letters he held various offices and honours in the imperial palaces, for his good understanding and his good ways made him loved and honoured by all, and he had become one of the emperor's counsellors. The emperor then was Constantine, the son of Leo and grandson of Copronymus, ten years old when he began to reign, with his mother Irene.

With that reign the heresy against the holy icons, begun under Leo the Isaurian, started up again. The patriarch was Paul the Cypriot, a good and right-believing man, who had hidden his faith and gone along with the heretics; he could find no one to help him set the icons back in their honour, and after four years on the throne he left the patriarchate in secret for the monastery of Saint Florus and took the great schema. Irene and her son came to ask him why. He answered that his sickness had brought him to the schema, but that it was the trouble of the Church that had driven him from the throne, and that he had put his own hand and his own ink to that heresy and repented of it bitterly.

Asked whom they should set in his place, Paul named Tarasius, first among the imperial counsellors, as the man able to drive off the lying words of the heretics with the staff of understanding. Before he died he told the senators plainly that unless a council of the whole world — the seventh — were gathered and the heresy against the icons wiped out, they could not be saved. Irene and her council found no one else fit, and gathered clergy, laity and people in the palace called Magnaura; and all cried out that there could be no one but Tarasius.

He refused outright. Standing in the middle of them he said that he saw the Church of God scattered and divided, and East and West cursing them daily; if a council of the whole world were gathered and all were united in one faith — as baptism is one, so the faith should be one — then, unworthy and unskilled as he was, he would take the governance of the Church; but if there were no such council and no union with the right-believing Churches of East and West, he would not be accursed and condemned, for no emperor could pull him out of the judgement of God. At that they all agreed that the seventh council should be held, and he accepted.

As patriarch he kept great abstinence and little sleep, passed the nights in prayer, never lay on a soft bed nor wore soft clothes, and would not let a servant take the shoes off his feet, remembering that Christ had not come to be served. He was past measure merciful to the poor, fed the hungry and clothed the naked, built them a hospital and promised them their food daily out of the revenue of the patriarchate, serving them at table with his own hands at Pascha; and out of his father's estate he built a hostel for strangers and a monastery on the Thracian Bosphorus, from which many were called to bishops' thrones.

He pressed the emperors continually until the council was summoned. Bishops gathered at Constantinople, but at the first attempt, in the great church of the Apostles, soldiers who held the heresy of Constantine Copronymus broke in armed and shouting, and the bishops were told to rise and scattered; Irene afterwards broke those soldiers and sent them to the villages to till the ground. The council then met at Nicaea in Bithynia, where the three hundred and eighteen fathers had met against Arius. Three hundred and sixty-seven fathers came, with the legates of Rome, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem, and Nicephorus of the imperial council, who was to hold the patriarchate after Tarasius.

The first session was on the twenty-fourth day of September. The bishops who had fallen into the heresy were heard and their repentance received, Basil of Ancyra reading out the anathemas against all who do not honour the holy icons; those who would not renounce it entirely had their judgement put off, for, Tarasius said, old sicknesses and long habits are hard to heal. The council sat twenty days, arguing from Scripture, from the older fathers and from the ancient traditions of the Church, and brought forward the story of the cloth Christ sent to Abgar of Edessa and the icon of Christ mocked by the Jews at Beirut; and the fathers wept at the reading.

He pastured the Church of the Lord twenty-two years and died in the reign of Nicephorus, who came after Irene, wept for by emperor, nobles and the whole city, and most of all by the poor, the orphans and the widows. He was buried in the monastery he had built on the Bosphorus, and healings were given at his tomb: a woman with an issue of blood many years, who put on man's clothes to get into the monastery, was healed by touching the tomb and taking oil from its lamp, and others after her; a man blind in one eye received his sight, and a man with a withered hand was made whole. Long afterwards, when Leo the Armenian renewed the war on the icons, Tarasius appeared to him in a dream in great anger and commanded a soldier named Michael to strike him — and it was a Michael of his own court who killed the emperor on the day of Christ's Nativity.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and adds the offices, the dates and the kinship. saint.gr names his parents George, a judge and patrician, and Eucratia, and says he was the uncle of [Photius](/saints/photius-of-constantinople), whom it keeps on 6 February; his learning raised him to the rank of protasekretis. Elected patriarch from the laity, against his own persistent refusals, he took a promise from the sovereigns first that an ecumenical council would be convoked; his consecration, the page says, was on 25 December 784. As patriarch he worked for the restoring of relations with the Western Church under pope Hadrian I, and for the calling of the Seventh Ecumenical Council at Nicaea in 787, which condemned the iconoclasts and annulled the iconoclast council of 754, taking the writings of [John of Damascus](/saints/john-of-damascus) for the ground of its dogmatic definition. Its eighth session sat at Constantinople, in the palace, where Irene and Constantine signed the council's terms.

To his piety, the page adds, the Church owes the care it took against simony, the buying of ecclesiastical office and of bishoprics above all; and beside that he gave himself to charitable and social work and was marked out for his almsgiving to the poor. His love of monasticism showed in the monastery he founded on the strait of the Bosporus, where he was buried after his holy repose, on the Wednesday of the first week of the Fast, in 806. In March 813 the emperor Michael I Rangabe clothed his tomb in silver, he and the empress Prokopia showing their reverence for his memory that way; and his synaxis, the page ends, used to be kept in the Great Church. Fragments of his body are at the monasteries of Panteleimon on the Holy Mountain and of Tatarna in Evrytania.
