# Cuthbert of Lindisfarne

Cuthbert was born about the year 634 into a rich and noble English family in the north of England. When he was eight and playing as usual on the hills about the house, a child of three among his companions began to rebuke him sharply for wasting his time and his strength in empty games. The words meant nothing to the others, but they went straight into Cuthbert's heart, for he felt that it was the Holy Spirit who had spoken through the child's mouth; and he gave up his games from that hour.

One night in 651, while he was still herding cattle, he saw a great light spread out of heaven and heard voices singing, and in the light a multitude of angels coming down to earth, who took up among them a human soul shining like the sun and carried it back into the heavenly glory. In the morning he learned that [Aidan](/saints/aidan-of-lindisfarne), bishop of Lindisfarne, had departed to the Lord at that moment, and he resolved then to become a monk.

After four years of military service he went to the monastery of Melrose on the Scottish border, where Saint Boisil shone, and was tonsured there; then he went for a while to Ripon, to help build it, and as guestmaster received a divine revelation that strengthened his resolve. Unwilling to follow the Roman monastic usages, he returned in 661 to Melrose, and after Boisil's death was made its abbot. He taught the brethren chiefly by the example of his own life, and went out through the villages to enlighten a people that was still half pagan.

It was his habit to go out at night, while the others slept, and pray standing in the sea up to his neck with his hands raised, keeping time with the waves; and one of the brethren bore witness that when he came out of the water two otters warmed him with their fur. In 664 the synod of Whitby decided for the Roman monastic tradition against the Celtic, and Cuthbert accepted it humbly and was made abbot of the great monastery at Lindisfarne: that he had been raised in the Celtic way and accepted the Roman made the brethren there accept the change.

He remained abbot until 676, when he withdrew to the nearby island of Inner Farne to live as a hermit with no companions but the seals, the otters and the sea-birds. Crowds came to him even there, to confess, to ask counsel or only for a blessing, and he went on healing. In 685 a synod under Saint Theodore of Tarsus, archbishop of Canterbury, elected him bishop of Lindisfarne in his absence, and after eight years of solitude he took up the burden. His closest friend was Saint Herbert, a hermit on an island in Derwentwater, who came to him every year for counsel.

After two years of hard work he laid the bishopric down again, feeling that his time had come, and returned to his cell on Farne; God let him enjoy that stillness for two months only. He fell ill and departed to the Lord on 20 March 687, and was buried at Lindisfarne as he had wished. Eleven years later, in 698, his grave was opened and his relics found whole, and were laid in an oak coffin and buried again. More than a hundred and fifty churches in England and Scotland were dedicated to him.

After his death Lindisfarne was called the Holy Island, and became a kind of Athos of England, until the Vikings sacked the monastery in 793 and killed the monks. Those who escaped carried away their father's relics with the Lindisfarne Gospels and the head of Saint Oswald, and after seven years of wandering laid them in a chapel at Dunholme — the Durham of today; the Norman conquerors built the present cathedral in 1093, and the relics were set in a shrine. In 1540 Henry VIII sent a party to break the shrine, and the monks were allowed to bury the relics; when the grave was opened in 1827 the bones were found finely vested, with the head of Saint Oswald beside them. The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and tells the story from the vision onward. saint.gr has him born at Lauderdale in 634 and orphaned young; one evening in 651, on the hills near the monastery of Lindisfarne, he saw a man's soul going up into heaven in a light not of this world, and a few days later heard of the repose of [Aidan](/saints/aidan-of-lindisfarne), the house's founder, kept on 31 Αυγούστου, and understood whom the vision had concerned. At seventeen he decided to become a monk and went to the monastery of Melrose, where he lived in obedience to St Eata, kept on 26 Οκτωβρίου. The missionary bent showed early, and he was drawn to the remotest and least accessible districts. In 661 king Alchfrith invited Eata to found a monastery, and Eata founded one at Ripon with some of his own disciples, Cuthbert among them; but the Church of Britain was being shaken by the quarrel over the keeping of Pascha, and the two went back to the house of their repentance at Melrose, where Cuthbert became abbot.

After the Synod of Whitby and the prevailing of the Roman tradition, St Colman of Lindisfarne, kept on 8 Αυγούστου, resigned his see in protest at the slighting of the Celtic tradition over Pascha and withdrew to Ireland; Eata became bishop of Lindisfarne and Cuthbert abbot of its monastery. His twelve years as abbot were not easy ones, the page says: tempers were sharp, and the new abbot had to reconcile two opposed parties inside the monastery and carry on his missionary work at once, which his proverbial gentleness, patience and discernment managed to do. In that hard time he took refuge on a rock islet near the monastery, where the traces of his cell survive under a wooden cross; and as the years passed he withdrew further, to Inner Farne, seven miles south of Lindisfarne, where Aidan had kept Lent before him.

There his only company was the sea-birds, and above all a breed of wild duck that lives there, which he tended with particular affection — for which, saint.gr says, he is reckoned the first man in Britain to have laid down rules of care for the natural world. His venerable presence, his abundant tears at prayer, his strict fasting, which recalled the Abbades of the Egyptian Thebaid, and his gift of prophecy made the far island a place of blessing for the faithful, who ran to him to be taught or to be healed in body and soul. In 684 the Synod of Twyford elected him bishop of Hexham, and a party of bishops, clergy and laymen headed by king Ecgfrith appeared at his hermitage to tell him the will of God and the synod's decision; the hermit of Farne refused to be parted from the stillness of his desert, and stillness and obedience fought it out in him until obedience won. On Pascha Sunday of 685 he was ordained bishop by [Theodore of Tarsus](/saints/theodore-of-tarsus-archbishop-of-canterbury), archbishop of Canterbury, kept on 19 Σεπτεμβρίου, and was shortly moved to the see of Lindisfarne while Eata took his.

He was bishop two years, and in them he supported, consoled, taught, prophesied and worked wonders, travelling to the furthest corners of his province to hold up a flock the plague was mowing down. Two months before his repose he foresaw his death and went back to his watery desert; a monk asked him when he would return, and he answered prophetically, When you bring my body back here. St Bede, kept on 27 Μαΐου, preserves his last words: Have peace among you, and divine mercy. He fell asleep in peace in 687 at the age of fifty-three, and his body was carried to Lindisfarne and buried in the sanctuary of the church of St Peter; eleven years later the relic was found incorrupt. After the Viking raid of 875 the monks of Lindisfarne took the relics of Aidan and of St Oswald, kept on 9 Αυγούστου, and Cuthbert's incorrupt body, and ended at Durham, where they laid them in the cathedral that had been raised there.
