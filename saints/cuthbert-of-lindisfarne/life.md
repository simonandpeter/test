# Cuthbert of Lindisfarne

Cuthbert was born about the year 634 into a rich and noble English family in the north of England. When he was eight and playing as usual on the hills about the house, a child of three among his companions began to rebuke him sharply for wasting his time and his strength in empty games. The words meant nothing to the others, but they went straight into Cuthbert's heart, for he felt that it was the Holy Spirit who had spoken through the child's mouth; and he gave up his games from that hour.

One night in 651, while he was still herding cattle, he saw a great light spread out of heaven and heard voices singing, and in the light a multitude of angels coming down to earth, who took up among them a human soul shining like the sun and carried it back into the heavenly glory. In the morning he learned that [Aidan](/saints/aidan-of-lindisfarne), bishop of Lindisfarne, had departed to the Lord at that moment, and he resolved then to become a monk.

After four years of military service he went to the monastery of Melrose on the Scottish border, where Saint Boisil shone, and was tonsured there; then he went for a while to Ripon, to help build it, and as guestmaster received a divine revelation that strengthened his resolve. Unwilling to follow the Roman monastic usages, he returned in 661 to Melrose, and after Boisil's death was made its abbot. He taught the brethren chiefly by the example of his own life, and went out through the villages to enlighten a people that was still half pagan.

It was his habit to go out at night, while the others slept, and pray standing in the sea up to his neck with his hands raised, keeping time with the waves; and one of the brethren bore witness that when he came out of the water two otters warmed him with their fur. In 664 the synod of Whitby decided for the Roman monastic tradition against the Celtic, and Cuthbert accepted it humbly and was made abbot of the great monastery at Lindisfarne: that he had been raised in the Celtic way and accepted the Roman made the brethren there accept the change.

He remained abbot until 676, when he withdrew to the nearby island of Inner Farne to live as a hermit with no companions but the seals, the otters and the sea-birds. Crowds came to him even there, to confess, to ask counsel or only for a blessing, and he went on healing. In 685 a synod under Saint Theodore of Tarsus, archbishop of Canterbury, elected him bishop of Lindisfarne in his absence, and after eight years of solitude he took up the burden. His closest friend was Saint Herbert, a hermit on an island in Derwentwater, who came to him every year for counsel.

After two years of hard work he laid the bishopric down again, feeling that his time had come, and returned to his cell on Farne; God let him enjoy that stillness for two months only. He fell ill and departed to the Lord on 20 March 687, and was buried at Lindisfarne as he had wished. Eleven years later, in 698, his grave was opened and his relics found whole, and were laid in an oak coffin and buried again. More than a hundred and fifty churches in England and Scotland were dedicated to him.

After his death Lindisfarne was called the Holy Island, and became a kind of Athos of England, until the Vikings sacked the monastery in 793 and killed the monks. Those who escaped carried away their father's relics with the Lindisfarne Gospels and the head of Saint Oswald, and after seven years of wandering laid them in a chapel at Dunholme — the Durham of today; the Norman conquerors built the present cathedral in 1093, and the relics were set in a shrine. In 1540 Henry VIII sent a party to break the shrine, and the monks were allowed to bury the relics; when the grave was opened in 1827 the bones were found finely vested, with the head of Saint Oswald beside them.
