# John Jacob of Neamț

John Jacob was born on 23 July 1913 in the village of Crăiniceni, commune of Horodiștea, in the former county of Dorohoi, to Maxim and Ecaterina, peasants of great faith, and was their only child. At his baptism he was given the name Ilie. Six months after his birth his mother, who had always been sickly, gave up her soul to the Lord, and the child was left in the care of his grandmother Maria; two years later his father died in the war, in the autumn of 1916, and he was brought up by close relatives. He went to school in his native village, then to the gymnasium at Lipcani-Hotin and the lyceum at Cozmeni-Cernăuți, where he was the best pupil in the school.

In the summer of 1932 his relatives wanted to send him to the Faculty of Theology at Cernăuți to make him a priest, but he told them he wanted to be a monk. A year later, working in the fields, he prayed God to show him his way, and heard a voice from above say, The monastery. From that hour he had no rest in his soul. With his confessor's blessing he took the holy books, the cross and the icon of the Mother of God from the house he was born in, on a Sunday, and entered the community of the monastery of Neamț. The abbot, Bishop Nicodim, received him with much love, sent him to venerate the wonderworking icon of the Mother of God in the princely church, and gave him his obedience in the infirmary and the library. He was very silent, obedient and humble.

In 1934 and 1935 he did his military service at Dorohoi as a medical orderly, with much pity for the sick and loved by everyone, and in the autumn of 1935 returned to Neamț to the same obediences. On 8 April 1936, the Wednesday of Holy Week, the rassophore Ilie Iacob was tonsured into the angelic monastic order by the hierarch Valerie Moglan, the new abbot of the great lavra, together with two other rassophore brothers, and was given the name Ioan. Longing for the desert life and burning in heart for Christ and for the Holy Places, he left for the Holy Land with two other monks of the lavra, Claudie and Damaschin.

Having venerated all the Holy Places and kissed the Cross of Golgotha and the Tomb of the Lord, the three withdrew to winter in the monastery of Saint Sabbas in the desert of the Jordan. His companions returned to Neamț, and he stayed at Saint Sabbas near Bethlehem for ten years, bearing hard temptations, illness and trials from men and from demons. His first obedience there was as sacristan: he made prosphora, kept the church clean and rang the bell for the services. He was also the monastery's infirmarian, and cared with love for the monks and for the many Arabs and Bedouin, sick or wounded in the war, who were brought to the infirmary, so that both sought him out and loved him. His confessor was the hieroschemamonk Sava, a Macedonian who knew Romanian and heard the confessions of all the Romanian monks struggling in the Holy Land.

By day he served the community and the sick; by night he struggled alone in his cell with secret prayers, prostrations, tears and readings from the Gospel and the Fathers. Knowing Greek well, he translated chosen patristic pages, which fed both himself and those who came to him, and he had the gift of writing spiritual instruction and verse, which he sent to his brethren in the Holy Land or gave to the Romanian pilgrims who came to the Tomb of the Lord. In 1939 and 1940 he struggled with a Romanian disciple in a cave in the desert of Qumran, near the Dead Sea; there he came to know the monk Ioanichie Pârâială, who remained his faithful disciple to the end. In 1940 and 1941, because of the war, he was held with many other monks of the Holy Land in a camp on the Mount of Olives.

Freed, he returned to Saint Sabbas and his old obediences. On 13 May 1947 he was ordained deacon in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, with the approval of the Patriarch of Romania and at the recommendation of the Archimandrite Victorin Ursache; in the same year he was ordained priest there by the hierarch Irinarh, and was named by the Romanian Patriarchate superior of the Romanian skete of Saint John the Baptist in the valley of the Jordan, near the place where the Lord was baptised.

For the five years he held that obedience he served all the services daily in Romanian, translated many pages of the Fathers with instruction for monks and pilgrims, composed a rich volume of spiritual verse, and renewed the cells and the church of the skete and, above all, its spiritual life, labouring much for the pilgrims from home, whom he confessed and communed and counselled. By night, unknown to anyone, he struggled alone, in his cell or out in the valley of the Jordan, trying to follow, so far as he could, [Mary of Egypt](/saints/mary-of-egypt). His one constant disciple was the monk Ioanichie, along with a few old Romanian nuns — Melania, Natalia, Galinia, Casiana and Magdalena — who were his spiritual daughters.

In November 1952 he withdrew from the office of superior and entered, with his disciple Ioanichie, the monastery of Saint George of Choziba in the desert of Choziba, in the valley of the brook Cherith. From the summer of 1953 he withdrew with his disciple to a cave nearby called the cell of Saint Anne, where, tradition says, she prayed God to give her a child; in another cave beside him a Cypriot monk named Pavel was struggling. There John struggled seven years in unceasing prayer, all-night vigils, long fasts and unknown tears, bearing every kind of temptation, want and total estrangement.

The cave was hard to reach, up a high ladder, and he received nobody, speaking with those who came chiefly by prayer, by holy writings and through his disciple. On great feasts and in the fasts he served the Divine Liturgy in the chapel of the cave of Saint Anne, and the two of them communed. In the daytime, at moments of rest, he would come out to the mouth of the cave, into the light, and write religious verse and translate patristic pages from the Greek. He ate once a day — rusks, olives, figs — and drank a little water, and slept a few hours at night on a board with a stone for a pillow.

In the summer of 1960 he was ill and bore everything with great patience. Feeling his end near, on Wednesday 4 August he received the Holy Mysteries, and on Thursday morning at five o'clock he gave up his soul into the hands of Christ, at the age of only forty-seven. Three days later he was buried in that same cave by the abbot of the monastery of Saint George, the archimandrite Amfilohie; and on 8 August 1980 his body was found whole, untouched by time and giving off a sweet fragrance.

On 15 August 1980 the same abbot had a coffin carved for him of cypress wood, laid him in it with great honour, and carried him in procession, with several hierarchs of the Orthodox Patriarchate of Jerusalem and thousands of pilgrims who had come for the feast of the Dormition, the monastery's patronal feast, and set the relics in the church of Saint Stephen within the walls, where the relics of Saint George of Choziba also lie. The translation was made with the blessing of Patriarch Benedict of Jerusalem. The Holy Synod of the Romanian Orthodox Church, considering the holiness of his life and seeing his relics, numbered him among the saints on 20 and 21 June 1992, under the name Saint John Jacob of Neamț, and set his feast on 5 august, the day of his passing to eternity.
