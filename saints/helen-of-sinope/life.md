# Helen of Sinope

Helen of Sinope was a virgin who was put to death as a martyr for her faith in Christ about the beginning of the eighteenth century, and the Eastern Church keeps her on 1 November. She lived about the year 1700 in Sinope, in the region of Paphlagonia, and was born into the Bekiary family in that town, the oldest city of Pontus. Her parents raised her in the Christian life, which filled her with a boundless love for Christ.

When she was only fifteen her mother sent her to a shop in Kryonas to buy embroidery materials. On the way she passed the house of Ukuzoglu Pasha, the Ottoman governor of Sinope, who was at his window at that moment; and seeing the girl and taking fire with a devilish lust, he ordered her brought to him. He tried several times to force her to bodily sin, and an unseen power stopped him every time. Not understanding what was happening, he shut her in the cellar of his house, and with God's help she managed to escape and get home.

Filled with anger, and suffering in his pride because she had escaped from his cellar, the governor gathered the elders of the Greek community of the place and told them that if they did not bring him the girl he would kill them all with heavy torments. At the elders' asking, and for the saving of the Christian people, her father broke his own heart, as the righteous Abraham once did, and sent the child to the pagan.

Again he tried many times to force her to his filthy sins, and the same divine power stopped him every time. Her life records that Helen would say the first six Psalms each time, which she had learned from her uncle, together with other prayers she had learned by heart on her own; the page prints the whole of the sixth psalm as she said it, "O Lord, rebuke me not in thine anger, neither chasten me in thy wrath."

Realising that he had no power over her, the pasha threw her into the common prison and gave orders that she be tortured to death. The executioners tortured her without measure, and at the last drove two great nails into her head; and then, seized with fury and swelling with pride, they cut off her head. After that they put her body in a sack and threw it into the waters of the Black Sea.

It is said that the body floated on the water as far as the place called Gai, where it sank. Some Greek sailors, seeing a heavenly light, went to the place where the sack with her body lay sunk, drew it out of the water and brought it to the priests. From the beginning her holy relics showed themselves to be healing and wonderworking. Her body was taken to Russia, only her head being placed in the Christian church at Sinope; and to this day she has worked countless wonders, especially for those who suffer from headaches and illnesses of the head.

When the Christian Greeks were driven out of Sinope in 1924 they took her head with them, and the man who cared for the holy relic was the president Christos Kapharopoulos. Today her head is placed for veneration in the church of Saint Marina at Ano Toumba, near Thessalonica. Between 10 and 12 April 2010 the wonderworking head of the new martyr Helen of Sinope was brought to Slovakia at the wish of Metropolitan George of Michalovce and Košice, taken from the Greek church with the blessing of Metropolitan Anthimos of Thessalonica; and she is honoured as the protectress of the metropolis of Michalovce and Košice, beside three other patron saints, and as the protectress of the young people of all Slovakia. doxologia.ro's page for her carries no Tropar tab, so the calendar prints her no hymn.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 1 November, and tells the whole story. Helen suffered in the eighteenth century, saint.gr says, and came from the fair city of Pontos, Sinope, of the devout family Bekiaris; she was fifteen, most beautiful in body, and her purity gave her face a particular grace. Her uncle, her father's brother, who then taught in a secret Greek school of Sinope, had the most to do with her upbringing. One day her mother sent her to buy thread for embroidery, and on the way stood the house of Oukouzoglou pasha, the governor of Sinope, who saw her from the window and had her brought before him; and he tried many times to force her, and could not, because an invisible wall protected her while she prayed without stopping. Instead of seeing the wonder his soul hardened, and because he could not have his way he tortured her cruelly and at last beheaded her.
