# Maura of Ceahlău

Hermit life on the mountain of Ceahlău, which older writers also call the Mountain of Pion, is as old as Christianity at the foot of the Carpathians and grew after Romanian monasticism was reorganised in the fourteenth century. Among the many who laboured there was Maura, who lived towards the end of the seventeenth century. She came from a village in the valley of the Bistrița, not far from Ceahlău, and was baptised Maria. Her parents were of good family and devout, and brought her up to prayer, fasting and good works.

She was a gentle child who loved the creatures around her, feeding the birds of the air and the animals of the wood above all in hard winters of deep frost and snow. She learned the Gospel at home and deepened it at the village church and at the monasteries and sketes nearby, where she went on pilgrimage with her family; old notes record that close to her village stood the skete of Silvestru, founded by a hermit of that name, its church built by the prince Bogdan IV, son of Alexandru Lăpușneanul. Bearing the name of the Mother of God, she promised the Virgin in her youth to keep her body pure all her life.

At twenty she took her parents' leave and went into the forest to find a place among the hermitages of Ceahlău, a doe keeping her company on the paths. A nun living alone deep in the wood, to whom she went for counsel, told her to begin in a community: «Mergi la Schitul lui Silvestru și acolo vei găsi odihnă sufletului». She went to the skete of Silvestru, where the nuns received her; she was given charge of the church, which let her sit long before the icon of the Mother of God, and in her free hours she went into the forest for medicinal herbs, the deer coming back with her as in a procession.

After some years the sisters clothed her in the angelic habit with the name Mavra. She built herself a small hut of earth and wood near the other cells, and afterwards asked the abbess's blessing to withdraw to a hidden place on the mountain: the clearing at Ponoară, directly below the slope of the Panaghia. There she kept long fasts and unbroken vigils, slept a few hours in a chair, made hundreds of prostrations and ate once a day in the evening, a few rusks soaked in water and some fruit she had dried herself.

Nuns from the houses of Moldavia began to seek her out for a word, and several asked to stay; with the help of the villagers at the foot of the mountain they built a small church of the Transfiguration with cells beside it, and the place is called the Nuns' Clearing, «Poiana maicilor», to this day. She knew the whole Psalter by heart, and God gave her foresight, healing and the gift of guidance, so that crowds climbed the mountain to her; the miracles her sisters saw they kept hidden out of reverence.

Feeling her end near she thanked God before her disciples that He had kept her to old age in the purity she had promised in her youth, saying she had lived there many years and no longer knew their number, and that she had never ceased praying for her parents and benefactors, for the peace of the world and for her people. She was confessed and communed, and asked to be left alone to give thanks; the next morning the sisters found her laid out on her poor bed, face to the east and hands crossed on her breast as though someone had placed them, dressed as a nun is dressed for burial. On the third day she was given to the earth in the Nuns' Clearing, as she had herself commanded.

Her memory was carried by word of mouth for more than two centuries before it was written down in the older Romanian synaxaria and paterika, and her face is painted in a number of churches — the old metropolitan cathedral of Iași, the church and bell tower of Durău, two churches at Sihăstria, Văratec, the skete of Vovidenia at Neamț. Her troparion was sung by the Tronos choir on 6 February 2026, at the general proclamation of the canonisation of the sixteen Romanian women saints; doxologia's page for it is a recording, and prints no text.
