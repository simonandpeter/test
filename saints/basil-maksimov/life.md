# Basil (Maksimov)

Basil was born on 28 January 1887 in the village of Babka in the Pavlovsk
district of Voronezh province, the son of the peasant Nikita Maksimov, who
fell gravely ill and went blind, so that the family lived poorly. A devout
and obedient boy, he was blessed by the village priest to serve in the
sanctuary; a visiting bishop who served at the patronal feast took a liking
to the pious, musically gifted boy of fourteen, took him to Shadrinsk where
he served, and put him in the church school. There he met his future wife
Julia, then at the music school, the daughter of the priest Alexander Konev
of Mysovaya station in Irkutsk province. In 1914 he was ordained priest for
a church at Shadrinsk, then moved to Petropavlovsk, and in 1923 to the
Moscow region, to the church of the great martyr Nicetas at Kabanovo in the
Orekhovo-Zuyevo district, where his blameless and zealous service made him
archpriest, mitred and dean. Of the church’s houses only the priest’s and
the watchman’s lodge were left by then, and in the lodge lived the devout
maiden Euphemia Vishnyakova with her father the watchman, a rope from the
belfry run into their room against thieves. He served often and preached at
every service, preparing carefully the day before from his large library,
knowing that his sermons reached his parishioners’ hearts and therefore
displeased the authorities, and counting the enlightening of his flock a
duty he could not lay down; the people knew he stood in danger and valued
it, trusted him, and knew that in that hard time they were not alone; and he
went at every call to anoint and communicate those who could not come to
church. On 10 June 1927 his wife died at thirty-two — she picked a pimple on
her chin, blood poisoning set in, and she was gone within the week — leaving
him with Maria and Nina, ten and four, and Nicholas not yet two; they had
lived soul to soul and she had been his first helper, and at first he could
not sleep, and when the children were asleep went to her grave and prayed
there for hours, so that they sometimes woke to find him gone. When a fire
in the village burned several houses he gave the big church house to the
burned-out families and moved to a little house on the edge of the village,
and later to the lodge, Euphemia and her father on one side and he and the
children on the other, and Euphemia took the children on herself and tried
to be a mother to them. In 1934 he wrote to a niece in Alma-Ata that the tax
had come, 360 roubles and 55 more by 15 March, of which he had paid half
with the people’s help — it is the fast now, the people come, and they must
be satisfied; today the singers sang, and there were sixty preparing for
communion; it is very hard and I am tired, but I feel well, because the
people are content; they love me and help me, and I give them all my
strength. In 1935, in Moscow on church business with two of his sanctuary
servers, he began to spit blood at his daughter Maria’s lodging near the
Danilov cemetery and was told at the hospital that he had tuberculosis of
the lungs, with little hope that the cavities would heal; his parishioners
saved him — as soon as they knew, they buried him in food, a rota being kept
in the village for who would bring what — and whether by the food or by the
love it showed, he was healed completely.

In 1937, when the news of arrests came from everywhere, the authorities more
than once offered him, since he had a fine strong voice, a place as a singer
in the theatre if he would leave the church, which he rejected as absurd,
and he prepared for arrest by burning what he held dearest, his dead wife’s
diary. In the night of 22 to 23 August 1937 the NKVD knocked at the lodge;
there was no search; one of them wedged the door shut with a stick so that
the children could not follow, and before walking to the car he asked leave
to go to his wife’s grave and pray, and was allowed. Euphemia, watching from
her half, went at once to the children, who were too small to understand
that their father had gone for ever. In the Orekhovo-Zuyevo prison the
interrogator put to him the false witnesses’ stories — that he had agitated
against the Stakhanov movement, said the old factory owners cared more for
the workers than the Soviet power did, preached against the collective
farms, taken part in political gatherings at a parishioner’s house, let
unregistered visiting priests serve, sheltered and paid one of them, and
demanded that the parish registers be kept by the old rules against the day
the regime fell — and he answered that talk of the Stakhanov movement might
have happened but he had given it no bad colouring, that he never touched
politics in his sermons, that he knew the women named as worshippers at his
church and had been in that house only for a wake, that the visiting priests
had served before the Synod’s circular forbade it and not after, that he had
sheltered and paid no one, and that he wanted the registers exact for the
church’s own order and nothing else. In early September Euphemia and the
children sent him a parcel — coat, scarf, cap, towels, toothbrush, plain and
scented soap, underwear, a knitted shirt, galoshes — with a note from Nina:
Papa, we are well; we are studying well, do not worry about us; we all kiss
you, Nina, Kolya, Marusya; and on its back he wrote: my dear and beloved, do
not worry about me; I am well; everything is in order; the case is not yet
finished; study well; has anyone been appointed in my place? live there, do
not go anywhere; thank Euphemia; send the black outer shirt and the white
one; I kiss you all; I bless you; God keep you — 12 September 1937. Moved to
Moscow, he told the interrogator on 20 September that he did not admit
guilt, was sentenced to death by the troika on 22 September, and was shot on
23 September 1937 and buried in an unknown common grave at Butovo. His elder
daughter sold the twenty-four-place dinner service the Dulevo works had once
made to his order, bought a room in Veshnyaki with the money, and took her
brother and sister to her; his secular books went to a devoted parishioner
whose daughter became a teacher, and his church books to a singer, who
passed them to a young man who read them, was enlightened, and was
afterwards ordained priest. The life is by the igumen Damascene (Orlovsky);
the Church keeps him on 10 September of the old style, 23 September, in the
synaxes of the new martyrs of Butovo and of Moscow.
