# Chionia of Aquileia

Chionia was the sister of [Agape](/saints/agape-of-aquileia) and of [Irene](/saints/irene-of-aquileia), sisters in the body and in the spirit, whose house stood near that of the priest Zoilus outside Aquileia in Italy. Diocletian was staying in that city when he had Chrysogonus brought from Rome and beheaded on a deserted stretch of shore nearby, and it was Zoilus who found the martyr's body by a revelation from God and hid it in a coffin in his room.

Thirty days later Chrysogonus appeared to Zoilus in a dream and told him that within nine days the three virgins of Christ would be put to torture; he was to tell Anastasia, the servant of God, to care for them and to rouse them to the struggle until they were crowned. The same was revealed to Anastasia herself. Not long afterwards the emperor went into Macedonia, and the Christians held in the prisons of Aquileia were taken after him, the three sisters among them, with Anastasia following at a distance; and there Dulcius the governor was given the task of forcing them to sacrifice.

Sisinius the count passed the sentence on the two elder sisters: Agape and Chionia, who had not submitted to the threat of judgement and carried out the imperial order, were to be burned. At that they were filled with joy and cried aloud: «Mulțumesc Ție, Doamne Iisuse Hristoase, că ne-ai învrednicit a fi mărturisitoare numelui Tău celui Preasfânt, în mâinile Tale, Stăpâne, primește sufletele noastre» — we thank Thee, Lord Jesus Christ, that Thou hast counted us worthy to confess Thy most holy name; into Thy hands, Master, receive our souls.

They were thrown into the fire and gave up their souls to their Lord in prayer, and the great fire, though it burned, touched neither their bodies nor their clothes nor left a mark on them. Their younger sister Irene was sentenced the next day and killed with arrows, and her body was afterwards laid beside theirs.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and differs from the Romanian notice in its details. saint.gr says the three were sisters and spiritual shoots of the Church of Thessalonica, and that the souls of all three virgins were adorned with many Christian graces. When the persecution of the Christians under Maximian came, the sisters took refuge on some high mountain; but their hiding-place was discovered, and in the year 304 they were arrested and brought before the ruler Dulcitius. He tried every way to make them deny Christ, and they, with the virtues they had for weapons, went on confessing Christ crucified. Then Agape and Chionia died, having been thrown into the fire, while Irene, shut up in a brothel where nobody dared to touch her, met her martyr's end from an arrow shot at her by a soldier. The relics that were left from the pyre, it adds, were gathered up by reverent Christians and buried west of the city a little way from the walls, and a small church was raised there which was afterwards made larger. Its couplets are two, one for Agape and Chionia — «Χιὼν τὸ πῦρ ἦν τῇ Χιονίᾳ τάχα, Οὗ συμμετασχεῖν ἠγάπησεν Ἀγάπη», the fire was perhaps snow to Chionia, and Agape loved to share in it — and one for Irene, «Βέλος σε πέμπει πρὸς τὸν εἰρήνης τόπον, Ἀφ' αἱμάτων σῶν ἐκμεθυσθὲν Εἰρήνη», an arrow drunk with your own blood sends you to the place of peace, Irene. A third line names the day, «Χιονίην τ' Ἀγάπην ἐκκαιδεκάτῃ κατέκαυσαν».

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the three sisters on the same day as the other two calendars and tells the story with several things neither of them has. They were sisters by birth, from the neighbourhood of Aquileia. When the emperor Diocletian was staying in that city he gave orders for the death of the noted spiritual father Chrysogonus; and an old presbyter named Zoilus was shown in a vision where the martyr's body was lying unburied, hurried to it, laid it in a chest and kept it in his own house. On the thirtieth day after that Chrysogonus appeared to him and told him that within nine days these three maidens would suffer, and that he himself would depart in the same time; the same was revealed to Anastasia, who had gone after her teacher. And indeed after nine days the old man departed, and the sisters were brought to trial before the emperor.

The emperor urged them to bow to the idols and all three refused and confessed their firm faith in Christ. It was Irene, the entry says, who told him how foolish it is to bow to things of stone and wood, ordered for an agreed price and made by the hands of some mortal man. In his rage he threw them into prison; and when he set out for Macedonia the slaves and prisoners were taken along after him, these three among them, and he handed them to a commander named Dulcitius for examination. Inflamed with a dark passion, Dulcitius meant to defile the virgins; but as he went to enter their prison, at the hour when they were praying to God, his mind was taken from him, and he fell upon the black cauldrons and pots standing before the door and embraced and kissed them, and came away sooty and black all over. When the emperor heard of it he set another commander, Sisinius, over their trial.

After heavy torture the judge sentenced the two elder sisters to be burned. The Prologue says nothing of the flames leaving their bodies and their clothes untouched, which is the Romanian notice's wonder, and nothing of the relics gathered from the pyre, which is the Greek page's; what it adds instead is that Saint Anastasia gathered all three bodies into one place and buried them honourably. They all suffered honourably for Christ the King and Lord, it ends, about the year 304 — the year this folder now holds, where it held none, and the same year the Greek page gives for the arrest.
