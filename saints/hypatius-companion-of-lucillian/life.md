# Hypatius, companion of Lucillian

Hypatius is the first of the four young men named in doxologia's line for the day, and one of the four its life names together — Claudius, Hypatius, Paul and Dionysius — thrown into the prison at Nicomedia for their faith in Christ, in the days of Aurelian, emperor of Rome. There the old man [Lucillian](/saints/lucillian) found them: a former servant of the idols, baptised in his old age, who had brought many pagans to Christ and had been denounced and brought before Silvanus, then count in Nicomedia, who broke his jaws, had him beaten with rods and hung head downwards, and then cast him into that same prison.

Lucillian rejoiced over the four, and spoke with them of Christ and strengthened them for the martyr's contest: not to fear passing torments, for the everlasting reward in heaven; not to be afraid of death, for the life that is to come; and not to spare the flower of their youth for Christ, who prepares for them in His kingdom a happiness that does not grow old. So they all prayed together day and night and comforted one another in the hope of Christ.

After some days they were tormented again and thrown together into a heated furnace, and God poured out His mercy on them as He did of old on the Hebrew children in the furnace of Babylon: the fire turned to coolness and the flame to dew, and a great rain came down and quenched the furnace, and they came out unhurt. The pagans set the miracle down to Christian sorcery, and the unjust judge condemned them to death and sent them to Byzantium for it. There the four young men were cut down with the sword, and Lucillian was hung upon a cross and pierced with nails through his whole body.

The calendar's line for the day names each of the four, so each has a folder here; but the life gives them no separate words, and what it says of Hypatius it says of all of them together. It names Aurelian and Silvanus and prints no year, so none is recorded here. The virgin Paula, who served them in prison and followed them to their death, gathered the bodies of the four young men after they were beheaded and buried them reverently.

The Greek calendar keeps the company on the same day, 3 June, and saint.gr dates them: «(; - 270)», in the years of the emperor Aurelian. Its account opens on the Lord's word that what is impossible for men is possible for God — for who, it asks, would have expected a man who spent almost his whole life inside idolatry, and was even a priest of it, to become a Christian? When Lucillian first heard Christian preaching in his native Nicomedia, divine grace made a real earthquake inside him: his idolatrous convictions, so deeply rooted in his soul, came down like towers of paper, his old eyes were opened, and with a young man's liveliness he proclaimed his faith in Christ and tried to bring other souls to him by preaching.

He was denounced for it to the count Livanios; he confessed Christ before him with courage; and the count, pressed by the pagan priests, who reckoned Lucillian a deserter from their religion, ordered him tortured. Then they threw him into a fire to be burned, and a heavy rain put the fire out; so they sent him to Byzantium, where he was counted worthy to bear witness by death on a cross. In the prison Lucillian had found four children, Claudius, Hypatius, Paul and Dionysius, imprisoned for the same cause, who were afterwards beheaded; and after the saint's death the virgin Paula took his holy relics and buried them, for which she too was seized, was cruelly tortured and at the last beheaded.

He is one of the four whom the page calls νήπια, infants, and whom Lucillian found already in prison for the same cause; its verse on them says that the sword, of children whose number is twice two, makes sons of God the Father.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the company on the Julian 3 June, the menologion day both the other calendars keep, and its heading enumerates all six: [Lucillian](/saints/lucillian), [Claudius](/saints/claudius-companion-of-lucillian), Hypatius, [Paul](/saints/paul-companion-of-lucillian), [Dionysius](/saints/dionysius-companion-of-lucillian) and the virgin [Paula](/saints/paula-companion-of-lucillian). Its account is the shortest of the three, and it is the only one that brackets the suffering in years: it happened, the entry closes, in the time of the emperor Aurelian, between 270 and 275. The Greek page gave the reign and one year inside it, and the Romanian gave none at all, so the interval this folder records has been widened to the bracket this book prints rather than narrowed to the year the other did.

He is the second of the four youths the heading names, and this book keeps no separate word for him, as neither of the other two does either: the four are named in one breath and suffer in one sentence. What is new is the company’s, not his — the pious talk, the prayers and the psalms in the prison, and the soldiers who did the beheading at Byzantium. The bracket of years is the only hard number the entry gives.
