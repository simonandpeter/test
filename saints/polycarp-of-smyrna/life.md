# Polycarp of Smyrna

Polycarp was by birth and upbringing a son of the city of Ephesus. His parents, Pancratius and Theodora, were believers, very rich and merciful, and when they were denounced to Marcion, the governor of Ephesus, he sent soldiers and had them brought to him. Theodora was carrying Polycarp at the time. They answered without any fear that they had learned from the holy apostles to worship the true God who made heaven and earth, and that they turned away from the lifeless idols he kept in place of gods.

The governor had them thrown down and beaten hard, then shut in prison, where they were left long without human help and worn down with hunger and thirst; and there Theodora gave birth. Seeing that Marcion meant to take the child and raise him in his own error, God sent an angel, who healed the parents of their wounds, told them not to fear a death suffered for Christ, and carried the infant to a rich old Christian woman named Callista, charging her to baptise him, bring him up and tell no one. Marcion searched many days, found nothing, and at last had the parents beheaded on a hill outside the city.

Callista named the boy Pancratius, after his father, and raised him as her own. He kept old men's thoughts from the first and took no part in children's games, and of all the virtues he loved almsgiving most: he emptied his mother's granaries for the poor in secret, and when she found them bare he prayed, and they filled again with every kind of produce, and the jars with oil and wine besides. She kissed him and said that from that day she would not call him Pancratius but Polycarp; and that, the life says, is where his name came from.

The account of his end is the letter the church of Smyrna sent out. The fire did not burn him but stood round him, cleansing him as gold in a crucible, and a fragrance came from it beyond all spices, so that the executioner was called to reach him through the flame and pierced him with a long spear; blood ran from the wound like a river from its spring and put the fire out altogether. The Jews asked the judge not to give the body to the Christians, and the centurion burned it after the Greek custom; his bones, purer than gold and more precious than costly stones, the Smyrnaeans gathered out of the ashes and kept in an honoured place, to keep the day of his passion there with gladness.

The letter puts that commemoration on the seventh calend of May — that is, the twenty-fifth day of April — on Great Saturday, at the eighth hour of the day, in the time of the high priest Philip and under the governor Trallianus. It was written down, the page says, by Caius out of the parchments of [Irenaeus of Lyon](/saints/irenaeus-of-lyon), Polycarp's disciple; Socrates of Corinth copied it from him, and Pionius the presbyter took it from them both and renewed what age had rubbed away. doxologia gives no year of his birth or his death, and its troparion tab for him carries a recording and no text.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and tells the martyrdom at length. saint.gr has him born about the year 80 to devout parents, Pancratius and Theodora, who had been shut up in prison for the faith of Christ, and baptised young; with [Ignatius of Antioch](/saints/ignatius-of-antioch), whose memory the page puts on 20 December, he was a disciple of [John the Theologian](/saints/john-the-theologian), and [Bucolus](/saints/bucolus-of-smyrna), bishop of Smyrna, ordained him his successor shortly before his own repose. [Irenaeus](/saints/irenaeus-of-lyon) supplies the page with two incidents: that Polycarp turned many back from the heresies of Valentinus and Marcion, and that when Marcion once came up to him and asked «ἐπεγίνωσκε ἠμᾶς», acknowledge us, he answered «ἐπιγινώσκω, ἐπιγινώσκω σὲ τὸν πρωτότοκον τοῦ Σατανᾶ» — I acknowledge you, I acknowledge you as the firstborn of Satan. In his old age he had to go to Rome over the Asian keeping of Pascha on the fourteenth of Nisan, to settle the matter with Anicetus, bishop of Rome.

After his return, already very old, his apostolic work so angered the idolaters that his martyrdom followed. Quintus, a zealot Christian who had come to Smyrna from Phrygia, urged a group of Christians of Philadelphia to present themselves to the proconsul Statius Quadratus and declare their faith; all of them were martyred except Quintus, who lost his nerve at the last moment and sacrificed to the idols. The crowd, though it admired the martyrs' courage, demanded that the «atheists» be executed and that Polycarp be hunted out — he had withdrawn to a farm under pressure from the Christians. He was taken in the year 167 and brought before the proconsul, and the old bishop was not shaken: his face was calm and bright. The police officer Herod and his father Nicetas tried to make him deny Christ, and he answered that he had served Christ for eighty-six years without ever leaving him, and asked how he could now blaspheme him and deny him. The proconsul ordered him thrown into the fire; the old man undressed himself and waited, praying the long prayer the page prints. The fire made an arch round his body without touching him, and an executioner finished him with the sword, after which the holy relic was thrown into the fire and the faithful gathered up what remained.
