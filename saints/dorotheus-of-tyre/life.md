# Dorotheus of Tyre

Dorotheus was bishop of the city of Tyre in the reign of Diocletian, the pagan emperor of Rome; and because of the dreadful persecution then raised against the Christians he left his see and hid himself in places unknown. Afterwards, when the great Constantine reigned and the Church of Christ had relief, Dorotheus returned again to Tyre and to his throne, and shepherded the flock of rational sheep well, bringing many from the error of idol-worship to God.

He lived on until the days of Julian the Apostate, who, on taking the empire, at first did not fight the Church of Christ openly but in secret, ordering the nobles of his own mind who ruled the provinces to torment and to kill the Christians. Seeing the great torment done to the faithful, Dorotheus again left the throne of the Church of Tyre and fled the hands of the tormentors; for the Lord commanded that we should not give ourselves to dangers that come openly from persecutors, but flee them, saying: when they persecute you in this city, flee into another.

So he went out from Tyre into Thrace; but there too he did not escape the servants of the idols, God calling him to the martyr's crown. He was seized by Julian's nobles in the city of Diopolis, where, suffering many beatings and enduring torments of many kinds, he gave up his blessed soul into the hands of Christ God, being very old: he had a hundred and seven years from his birth. The page gives no year.

He left behind him writings of many kinds useful to the Church, in Greek and in Latin, for he was well skilled in both these tongues, a man perfect in spiritual and in outward wisdom. He wrote the lives of the prophets and of the apostles and other useful discourses and commentaries; and now, himself written in the book of life, he dwells with those saints whose lives he wrote.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and puts both ends of a long life to it. saint.gr says he was a shepherd who had the form of knowledge and of truth in the law, the exact knowledge and truth that lie in the Old and the New Testament. When the murderous persecution against the Church broke out in the years of Diocletian his flock, rather than lose its precious shepherd, prevailed on him with warm entreaties to withdraw from the place of the persecutions; he went off to Dysso, a city of Thrace, and lived the ascetic life there until the savage persecution had passed. When the storm was over he came back to his flock and with all the fatherly affection that marked him he strengthened the weak in the faith and took the lead in helping widows, orphans, the sick and the afflicted generally. He lived many years, and lasted into the reign of Julian the Apostate; the idolaters, under that emperor's protection, seized Dorotheus, tortured him without mercy and killed him in 362, and so he won the crown of martyrdom, being then a hundred and seven years old. A church writing of his survives, the page adds, on the seventy disciples of Jesus Christ.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 5 June, the menologion day both the other calendars keep, and sets the whole life in five lines: bishop of Tyre from the days of Diocletian until the days of Julian the Apostate, under whom he was tormented and suffered for the Orthodox faith; a hundred and seven years on the earth; and the end in 361, where the Greek calendar says 362 and the Romanian gives no year at all. The folder now holds 361 or 362 and chooses neither. It does not hold 254 for the birth, although a hundred and seven years before 361 would give it: the age is a length of life and no source for a year, and the 255 recorded here is a figure the Greek page prints for itself.

The one thing in it that is new is a title. Both earlier pages say he was learned and wrote much in Greek and in Latin; the Romanian names the lives of the prophets and of the apostles, and the Greek a writing on the seventy disciples. This book says he was a very learned man who wrote many instructive works in both tongues, and that the famous one among them is his Greek-Latin Syntagma — which neither of the others names.
