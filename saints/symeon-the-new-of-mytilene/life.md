# Symeon the New of Mytilene

Symeon was the youngest of the seven children of Adrian and Konstanto of Mytilene, born in 765 or 766; five of the seven became monks, and three of those five were [David](/saints/david-of-mytilene), Symeon himself and their brother George. At eight years old his mother took him with her to Mount Ida in Asia Minor to see David, who had been a monk there thirty years; Symeon stayed with his brother, and his mother went back to Mytilene and died soon after.

He learned his letters in his brother's monastery, became a monk at twenty-two and was ordained priest at twenty-eight by the bishop of Gargara. Two years later David died, having foreseen his own death and told Symeon to go back to Mytilene. Symeon obeyed, and at the Panagia in the southern harbour of the town, at the Molos, he climbed a pillar in order to imitate the contest of the old Symeon the Stylite, and lived there in fearful asceticism, fasting, hardship and prayer. He took his brother George to live beside him, and with him and their sister Hilaria, a nun, and other monks they built a monastery to which crowds came to hear the word of God.

Then the rage of the iconoclasts troubled the monastery and the Church at large. The emperor Leo V the Armenian, whose reign saint.gr dates 813 to 820, proclaimed persecutions again; the bishop of Mytilene was exiled and an iconoclast named Leo put in his place, who turned at once on Symeon and his monks. By that bishop's doing Symeon was condemned to death by fire, but was saved by a wonder and stayed a while unmolested on his pillar, until the same bishop forced him to leave Mytilene and withdraw with his monks to the little island called Saint Isidore, in the gulf of Gera.

Later the iconoclast bishop got from the emperor Michael II the Stammerer, 820 to 829, an order exiling Symeon to Lagousa, an uninhabited island opposite the country of Troy; there he went with seven disciples and stood on a pillar ten metres high. Afterwards he left for Constantinople, where he judged he could give the Church services it needed, and settled in the monastery of Niketas of Medikion; from there he travelled from the Hellespont to the islands of the Aegean and as far as the Black Sea, holding up the Christians by his word and consoling the persecuted fathers in their places of exile. On those journeys he worked as a fisherman wherever he stopped, to provide not so much for himself as for those who needed help; and he healed the sick and founded a women's monastery as well.

After the death of Michael the Stammerer his iconoclast successor Theophilus proclaimed a savage persecution again and seized Symeon and his company, meaning to shut them in prison and make an end of them. He was saved from that danger too, by the intervention of the empress Theodora, but did not escape the hundred and fifty strokes of the rod the emperor ordered, nor exile to Aphousia in the Propontis, where he went along with other distinguished preachers of Orthodoxy such as Theophanes and Theodore, the ones called the Written-on. The page's account of him runs on past that point, and this folder records only what was read of it.
