# Alexander, companion of Antonina

Alexander was a soldier of twenty-three years, and doxologia.ro prints his life and that of [Antonina](/saints/antonina-of-crodamna) as one story. In a persecution against the Christians, Antonina, a Christian virgin of the city called Crodamna, was brought before Festus the merciless governor, refused to be made priestess of Artemis, confessed Christ, and was beaten and imprisoned; and after three days in which a voice from heaven strengthened her, and after she had laughed in the governor's face and told him his seat would soon be overturned, he ordered her taken to a house of harlotry.

While the holy virgin sat there praying earnestly, the angel of the Lord appeared to Alexander and said: "Go to the governor and speak with him to let thee go in to Antonina; and when thou hast clothed her in thy garment let her go out from there unknown to that wicked governor." The good soldier of Christ went to the governor and said: "Governor, command me to go in to Antonina; and if I persuade her it will be well, and if not, then do with her as thou wilt." The governor gave him leave, saying: "If it please thee, do with her what thou wilt."

Alexander went in and fell at her feet: "Servant of God, virgin Antonina, the Lord has sent me to thee to tell thee what he has said to me, how thy virginity is to be kept." She was afraid; but a great light shone in the house and a voice was heard: "Fear not, Antonina, for he who called thee to this struggle, being compassionate and of great mercy, calls this Alexander also to the same martyr's crown. Put on his garment and, keeping thy head down, go out from here." So she put on his garment, covered her head, went out past the governor and escaped like a deer out of the snare.

The governor, thinking it was Alexander who had left, sent four soldiers in with orders to abuse her; and what they found was Alexander in her clothes. The two were brought together before Festus, and answered him with one voice that no such torments could force them to worship his unclean gods, which have neither understanding nor power. He ordered their hands cut off, and they stretched out their hands to the sword, saying that by no torments would he bend their godly mind; and when the swords had done it they glorified God as though they felt no pain.

Then he had a deep pit dug, hung them up and had them beaten until they should feel their wounds, and had the pit filled with wood. Feeling nothing, they gave glory to the Lord; so he added torments to torments and had their sides burned with lighted torches, and they cried out that they suffered for the love of Christ and looked for an everlasting reward, and that the Lord would soon destroy him. He had the wood lit, the martyrs smeared with pitch and thrown into the pit of fire, dry pitch laid on so that their bones should burn utterly to ashes, and the pit stopped with earth so that the women should not gather their bones as the Christians were used to do.

Festus went home and tasted neither food nor drink but was struck dumb, for an unclean spirit sent into him by the holy angels tormented him until the seventh day, when he gave up his unclean soul in bitterness; and when he had perished the persecution ceased there. The two martyrs finished their suffering on a Saturday, at the ninth hour of the day. The page gives no year.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 10 June, and saint.gr puts a year to the martyrdom, «(; - 313)». Its account names the eparch Phystus, and it makes Alexander's part in the story plain: Antonina, a Christian virgin of the small town of Kardamos — or Krodamos — had been denounced to Phystus for her Christian way of life, had answered him by calling on him to repent and be baptised, and had been thrown into a brothel, where her prayer brought so violent an earthquake on the house that the women there drove her out of it themselves. Phystus seized her again and shut her in another place; and there a certain Christian named Alexander came, and got her away by lending her his own clothes. The eparch, beside himself when he learnt of it, ordered them both taken at once, heard their defence, and gave them to a hard torture: they were mutilated, smeared with pitch, and their bodies thrown into the fire, which gained them the crowns of martyrdom, in the year 313. The page ends by saying that their relics were afterwards carried to Constantinople and laid in the monastery of Maximinus, where a synaxis of them used to be celebrated.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two of them together on the Julian 10 June, the menologion day both the other calendars keep, and its heading names them in one line. Both were from Alexandria, it says — where the Romanian life calls her a Christian virgin of the city called Crodamna, which is the name this corpus keeps her under; the two readings are recorded and neither is preferred. It tells the escape as the earlier books do: at the command of an angel of God the soldier went into the prison, though until then unknown to her, wrapped her in his own military cloak, told her to bow her head and walk out past the guards at the door; so the virgin got away and the soldier stayed behind.

Then he was brought before the judge and questioned for the name of Christ, and she, hearing of it, gave herself up to the judge; and he put them both to torments of many kinds, cut off their hands, beat them on the bare body, burnt their wounds with candles, and at the last threw them into a fire laid in a pit and heaped earth over them. **And this book dates it**: they suffered for the love of Christ and passed into the courts of the King of heaven on the third of May, 313 — the year the Greek calendar had bracketed and this folder had never recorded, and a day neither of the other books gives. The day is the date of the passing as this book states it, not a day it keeps them on.

The malicious judge Festus, at the hour of the martyrs’ death, was struck dumb, and an evil spirit seized him and tormented him seven days and at the last killed him. That is on these pages already in more words, down to the seventh day, and a third book agreeing to it is worth recording and changes nothing else.
