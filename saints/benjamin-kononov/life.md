# Benjamin (Kononov)

The Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery keeps him on 4 апреля ст. ст. with one companion in a line of four words, «Прмчч. Вениамина и Никифора (1928).», and the name link behind it, page 9166, gives what the line leaves out: «Вениамин (Кононов), архимандрит настоятель Соловецкого м-ря, сщмч.» — the archimandrite and abbot of the Solovki monastery. The calendar has no life for him; azbyka.ru does, and it is long.

He was born on 4 January 1868 in the village of Евсевиевская in the Шенкурский uyezd of Arkhangelsk province, to the peasants Василий Егорович and Пелагия Ильинична Кононовы, and baptised Vasily. He was taught at home, as the time and the circumstances went. His parents had been to the Solovki monastery more than once and took their son with them, and after each visit the resolve to give his life to the Lord grew firmer in him.

In 1893, at twenty-five, he entered the monastery as a labourer, and in 1897 was received a novice. He ran the bakery and kept the monastery’s supply shop, and finished six classes of the Solovki brothers’ school; he was found well-behaved and obedient, with a strong turn for the inward life. On 12 June 1903 the abbot tonsured him and gave him the name Veniamin, after a hieromartyr of Sinai. He was ordained hierodeacon on 28 August 1905 and hieromonk on 15 July 1908, and his obediences were at the relics of the monastery’s two founders and as teacher of the law in the brothers’ school.

He was made confessor of the monastery on 22 February 1910, abbot of the Антониев-Сийский monastery on 30 October 1912, and archimandrite that December. Solovki meanwhile fell into four years of strife against its own abbot, which ended with the Synod retiring that man in August 1917; and on 4 October 1917, in a secret ballot of a hundred and seventy monks and seventy-seven novices, two hundred and thirty-eight of the two hundred and forty-seven papers were for the former confessor of the house. He took the monastery over, and a fire broke out in it on the night of 7 December.

What he said to the brethren then azbyka prints: that God had allowed the fire for their grievous sins and their strife against the abbot before him, that they had made that man first and then spat on him, and that he feared the time was coming, and soon, when he too would seem bad and unwanted and plots would be laid against him. He would hear no more accusations against his predecessor. The quiet lasted a little while.

Then the decree on the separation of Church and state took the Church’s legal standing and its property, and the brethren hid the most honoured holy things and the vessels still in liturgical use — by his direction, in the wall and under the porch of the Transfiguration cathedral and over the altar of the Nicholas church — with his closest helper in the work the hieromonk whose folder stands beside this one. The Red Army entered Arkhangelsk in February 1920, a commission reached the islands in April, and the stores began to leave. On 20 June 1920 he wrote to the Kem food committee asking it not to let the brotherhood starve through the winter on a wild and harsh sea island, and to leave the yearly ration for four hundred and thirty men. The request was ignored.

In August 1920 a telegram reported that the walled-up church silver had been found on the information of monks who had turned against the house, and the order came back to arrest the silver and find who had hidden it. Late in 1920 the two of them were arrested for concealing the monastery’s valuables and keeping arms, and sent to forced labour at the timber workings of Kholmogory. Released in 1922, they lived at the Solovki metochion in Arkhangelsk, and when that closed, with an Orthodox pharmacist who took the two homeless monks in.

The noise of the city was not what they wanted. In the summer of 1926, on the advice of a former Solovki novice, they moved to the village Часовенское and that same summer built themselves a cell in deep forest, forty versts from the nearest settlement. The hut was about twenty square metres and held what was needed: two beds, some icons, and from the monastery a nickel-plated samovar, a coffee-pot and a striking clock. They made a kitchen garden and an ice-house, lived on the vegetables and on fish from the lake on whose high bank the cell stood, and twice a year their friend brought what else they needed. The place was so remote that only a stray hunter came by, and they lived there in prayer and work for almost two years.

A young man who had been twice convicted of theft was living in the nearest village, and in 1927 a komsomolets of about twenty settled in Часовенское, took work as a forest ranger and learnt the way to the cell from local hunters. In the winter of 1927 he found it and asked a night’s lodging on the strength of some common acquaintance. The monks felt the wrong in him, azbyka says, and by the duty of Christian hospitality could not refuse, and let a murderer in to sleep; he looked the room over and kept his purpose.

In the spring of 1928 he talked the thief into it. They came to the lake towards evening on 17 April, the Tuesday of Bright Week, and the whole cell was lit from within, the monks keeping the paschal service. When the prayers were done and the light was out, the murderers came close to the windows and fired at where the beds stood. There were groans and then silence; and the two were afraid to go in, so from the entry they climbed into the loft, took some boots, two raincoats, underclothes, socks, trousers, a piece of black tarpaulin, a blue enamelled kettle, a set of fish-hooks and a carpenter’s tools, found a vessel of kerosene there, poured it over the walls and set them alight — and heard cries from inside the cell, the two priest-monks being still alive.

On 9 June 1928 their friend came to visit as he had promised and found a burnt cell and two charred skeletons. The murderers were arrested soon after; the one tried to justify what he had done by the monks’ being counter-revolutionary, and the court gave the two of them ten years and eight. The calendar’s line closes «(1928)», and that is the year. azbyka lists two further days beside this one, the moveable synaxis of the New Martyrs and Confessors of the Russian Church on 7 February and the synaxis of the saints of the Arkhangelsk metropolia on 1 November; both are synaxes and neither is written into the feast.
