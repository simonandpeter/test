# Stelian the Paphlagonian

Stelian was born of a family of good Christians at a time when the Orthodox East was shaken by the persecutions of emperors of Byzantium let loose against the holy icons, when many hierarchs, theologians, priests, monks and faithful fell victim to the iconoclasts. His parents were people of means, but they were passing through another grief: their children died shortly after birth. Stelian alone was left alive, and the love of his parents was turned on him, woven through with the fear of losing him too.

So he grew up with an unquenched love for children and with pity for those in suffering, for the absence of brothers and sisters in his parents' house marked him for the rest of his life. Inclined to spiritual things and kept always by his parents on the way of the church, in thanks for the gift of the life he had been given, he found in the church of his town a second home, and in the air of the services the true breath of his life.

When both his parents died and he was left an orphan, he sold everything they had gathered, gave all he had to God's poor, and turned to one of the many monasteries which in those days adorned the Christian ground of Asia Minor. He settled in such a hearth of spiritual life in the province of Paphlagonia, not far from the ancient Christian settlement of Caesarea in Cappadocia, the homeland of Saint Basil the Great: a mountainous province where monastic and hermit life flourished on the heights and in the many caves, some of which, left empty after the Turkish invasion, keep the beauty of their Christian paintings to this day.

In such a monastery the young Stelian sheltered, in obedience and following closely the counsels of the old monks. Knowing his zeal for the monastic life and his love for Christ, the abbot set about preparing him for the priesthood; but he, reckoning himself unworthy to take a rank above that of the angels — as Saint Gregory the Theologian and Saint John Chrysostom had once reckoned — asked his confessor's leave to live as a hermit in one of the caves near the monastery. There he lived many years in fasting and unbroken prayer.

His life among those rocks, with his mind lifted to the Lord alone, did not stop him pouring out his love and pity on Christians in suffering. When he learned from the faithful who came to him for counsel, or to have him pray for them, that a cruel plague was abroad in those parts and was cutting down the children first, Stelian remembered his own parents' weeping and was kindled with pity; and he opened his heart to God and sent up humble prayers that the plague might be taken away.

And as another Elijah, who had raised the son of the widow of Sarepta in Sidon, his fervent prayers were accepted: the plague ceased and many children were saved from death. Knowing the warmth of his heart towards children, his pity for all who suffered, and the power of the prayers he offered to God, mothers with sick infants began to come to Stelian's cave to win by his prayers the health and the life of their children, and his prayers had such strength that most often they went home happy.

His fame grew and spread through the neighbouring lands, but his humility went far beyond the honour people tried to bring him. In that state — of burning love towards God and of sacrificial pity in prayer for all who suffer — he passed to eternal things; but the miracles at the cave where he had lived did not cease. His incorrupt body was brought to the church of the monastery of his tonsure and had the same strength in healings as Stelian had while he was alive.

Knowing the devotion of the crowds, the love with which they surrounded his memory, and the miracles done before his relics, the Church decided on his canonisation — his numbering among the venerable wonderworkers — and appointed as the day of his commemoration 26 November, the day of his passing to eternal things.
