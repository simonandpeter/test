# Nilus the Younger of Calabria

Nilus the Younger was born at Rossano in lower Italy in 910, the Greek synaxarion says, to parents who thought him a gift of God and dedicated him to the Theotokos. Orphaned young and brought up by a devout sister, he grew into a handsome, clever and eloquent young man, took up with a girl of the town and had a child by her; but in a high fever he saw a vision of death and damnation so vivid that he shook all over, and one day, telling no one, he left for the monasteries of the Mercurion. The governor had written threatening any monastery that took him in, so he was sent on to the monastery of Saint Nazarius in Lombard country, where he took the habit on condition that he might go back after forty days, and swore never to accept honours or high office.

At the Mercurion he grew very close to [Phantinus](/saints/phantinus-the-wonderworker), and lived in a cave near the monastery, fighting his temptations with tears, prostrations and thorns. The entry tells of the devil coming to him in the likeness of the apostles Peter and Paul, of a tumour in his throat that dissolved after he refused the fish he thought might cure it, and of a year’s injury from a demon that healed as he read aloud an encomium of the two apostles. When Phantinus foresaw the Saracens destroying the Mercurion and left, its monks asked Nilus to choose their abbot, and he made Phantinus’s brother Luke take the office.

Disciples gathered round him: Stephen, a poor young farm labourer whom he trained with great severity and cared for, sending his mother and sister to a convent; George, one of the leading men of Rossano; and Proclus, the first to be called abbot, for as the community grew Nilus always gave that title to someone else. He built a convent for nuns at the chapel of Saint Anastasia near Rossano. The imperial judge Eupraxius, who had meant to punish him for staying away from his welcome, fell ill with gangrene for three years, was made a monk by Nilus and died; and when the strategos Basil offered him gold from the taking of Crete, Nilus refused it and told him Calabria would soon fall to the Saracens.

So he left Calabria for Capua and then Rome, and came at last to Tusculum, about twelve miles from Rome, and the monastery of Saint Agatha, where there were some Greek monks. Gregory, the prince of Tusculum, a man known for tyranny and injustice, fell at his feet and offered him his house, his lands and his fortress; Nilus asked only for a small piece of land where he could live in quiet and his monks could pray. There, with a few monks round him, he gave his rags to the monk Paul, received communion, blessed them, and was carried into the church, where he fell asleep in peace, in 1003, the entry says, though its heading prints 1005; one of the monks heard him say that he would not be put to shame, for he had kept all the Lord’s commandments. He also wrote hymns to Saint Benedict, sung at a vigil by a choir of sixty. The Greek calendar prints no hymn for him.

*After the Ορθόδοξος Συναξαριστής (saint.gr), 26 Σεπτεμβρίου — [the entry](https://www.saint.gr/2505/saint.aspx); read 17 September 2026.*
