# Romylos of Ravanica

No synaxarion has a notice of Romylos; his life is in manuscript 154 of the
skete of Kafsokalyvia, written by his disciple Gregory. He was born about
1300 in the Danubian city of Vidin to devout parents, a Greek father and a
Bulgarian mother, and named Raiko at baptism; from childhood he longed for
learning, and his teachers, admiring his wisdom and good sense, called him
the child-elder. Grown up, to escape the marriage his parents were planning
he fled in secret to a monastery in the province of Tarnovo, where after the
usual probation he was tonsured in the small schema as Romanus and served
with great reverence as sacristan. In those years Gregory of Sinai, kept on
6 April, left the Holy Mountain with his disciples and settled at Paroria,
today’s Strandzha, on the Byzantine-Bulgarian border; when Romanus heard of
this teacher of noetic prayer and the hesychast life he asked his abbot’s
blessing to put himself under him, and took another brother, Hilarion, along.
Gregory received them with joy and, Romanus being strong and of powerful
build, gave him the heaviest work, which he did in absolute obedience —
carrying wood and stone down from the mountain and water up from the river
at its foot, mixing clay for the buildings, serving in the kitchen and the
bakehouse, nursing the sick brethren. He was given the care of an old monk,
ill and bad-tempered, who because of his illnesses could eat only fresh
fish, and served that hard old man with wonderful meekness, fishing the
river for him and in winter breaking the ice and standing barefoot in the
freezing water with the net — a martyr by choice, the life says, giving his
life every moment for love of his neighbour.

When the sick elder and Gregory had both died, Romanus — whom everyone
called “good Romanus” — put himself with Hilarion under another elder, until
robbers who plundered those parts and tyrannised the monks drove them to
Stara Zagora, where that elder soon died too; then, Hilarion being the
older, Romanus obeyed him, and when the tsar John Alexander (1331–1371) had
cleared the robbers out they went back to the desert quiet of Paroria to
converse with God in the prayer of the heart. With the virtues that had
become his second nature and unceasing prayer he received many gifts of
God, above all the gift of ever-flowing tears. Later, with Hilarion’s
blessing, he withdrew into complete solitude to feed undistracted on divine
contemplation, and after many years so was tonsured in the great schema as
Romylos. Turkish raids destroyed the monastery, and he fled with his
disciple Gregory to the Holy Mountain, to Melana near the Great Lavra,
where the Athonite monks soon recognised his virtue and came for their
souls’ good — and broke his beloved quiet, so that he moved to a lonely cell
at the foot of Athos. After the defeat of John Uglješa’s Serbian and
Bulgarian army by the Ottomans at the Maritsa in 1371 and the irregulars’
invasion of Thrace and Macedonia, many monks left the Mountain in the
general fear, and Romylos among them went to Avlona in Albania, where he
corrected the corrupted ways of the people and taught them the right faith,
from which they had drifted far. Longing for stillness, he went on to the
monastery of the Mother of God at Ravanica in Serbia, his last earthly
home; he died there soon after, in 1375. The grave in which his disciples
laid his much-tried body gives out an unspeakable fragrance, and miracles
and healings continue for those who come to it in faith. The Romanian
Lives, by Father Makarios of Simonopetra, tell the same; the Greek and
Romanian calendars keep him on 18 September.
