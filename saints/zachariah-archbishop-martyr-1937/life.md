# Zachariah (Lobov)

Zachariah — Zakhar Petrovich Lobov — was born on 23 March 1865 in the
village of Petrovka in the Pavlovsk district of Voronezh province, the son
of a minor official of no noble rank. Choosing the clerical road, he
finished the church school at Pavlovsk and the seminary at Novocherkassk
in 1888, and from that September served at the Novocherkassk cathedral,
where he became archpriest and sacristan. He had four children — Olga,
Vera, Gabriel and Alexis, the last of whom became the monk Anthony and, an
archimandrite, was in exile near Arkhangelsk in the thirties; a
great-grandson, Oleg Lobov, was living at Novocherkassk in the early
nineties. Widowed, he took the monastic tonsure keeping his own name, and
on 5 October 1923 Bishops Mitrophan (Grinev) of Aksai and Innocent
consecrated him bishop of Nizhne-Chirskaya, vicar of the Don diocese,
whose last bishop, Nicholas (Orlov), had died in the Tsaritsyn prison in
1922. In 1924 he was arrested and sentenced by the Rostov Chekists to two
years’ exile at Solovki, and no one held his see after him. There were
more bishops at Solovki then than at liberty: the camp photographs show
beside him Metropolitan Eugene (Zernov), later of Gorky, Archbishops
Mitrophan (Grinev), Hilarion (Troitsky) of Vereya and Seraphim
(Meshcheryakov), and Bishops Paul (Vvedensky) of Morshansk, Platon
(Rudnev) of Bogorodsk and Ambrose (Polyansky) of Vinnitsa. On 7 June 1926
he was one of the twenty-four hierarchs who signed the Memorandum of the
Solovki Bishops, submitted for the government’s consideration, which
proposed that Church and state should each keep strictly out of the
other’s affairs — the bishops renouncing any part in political life, but
asking to hold a Local Council, elect a Patriarch and worship freely; the
government never answered it. In 1926–1929 he was a vicar of the Tver
diocese, living at Torzhok and Bezhetsk.

On 24 April 1929 he was raised to archbishop and given the Voronezh
diocese, as archbishop of Voronezh and Zadonsk, and came to Voronezh in
the days of Pascha, asking in his first word to his flock to be received
as archpastor and father and turned to with the whole heart and soul — let
us be one, for in unity is our salvation, heavenly and earthly. For the
first year and a half he lived in the watchman’s lodge of the Dormition
church, then in rented rooms on Shchemilovskaya street and Komarov lane;
until March 1932 he served in the Trinity Smolensk cathedral, and when it
was closed moved his see to the Dormition church, where as before the
people of the town and the villages round, deprived of their churches and
pastors, came to him for a word, counsel and teaching. Metropolitan Manuel
(Lemeshevsky) described him: he kept Wednesday and Friday strictly, ate
once a day, a lenten millet soup, and that only after the all-night vigil,
and in the first, fourth and seventh weeks of the Great Fast likewise once;
in private life he was simple and now and then liked a joke. In the hard
years of militant atheism he kept and strengthened the Orthodox faith of
his flock with God’s help; in 1931 he sat in the Provisional Patriarchal
Synod, on 19 April 1932 was given the right to wear the cross on his
klobuk, and from the first stood with Metropolitan Sergius
(Stragorodsky), the deputy locum tenens, and supported him in all he did.
From Solovki and from his service he knew many hierarchs: in 1933 Bishop
Vassian (Pyatnitsky) of Tambov, freed from exile, stayed a week with him;
in 1934 Archbishop Onuphrius (Gagalyuk) of Kursk two months; in 1935
Bishops Paul (Vvedensky) of Morshansk and Seraphim (Protopopov) of Yelets,
passing through Voronezh on business.

On 23 May 1935 he was arrested and his rooms searched, his vestments and
the church money taken; on 19 July he was charged with counter-revolutionary
agitation “exploiting the religious prejudices of the masses” and with
spreading provocative rumours, on the testimony of the clergy of his own
Dormition church — the priest I. S. Kotov, the psalmists P. I. Dolgopolov
and I. L. Nazarchenko and the warden I. N. Skripitsyn, who had turned
against their bishop and spoke of his hostility to the Soviet power, of
things said, or supposedly said, about the peasants’ misery under the
collective farms, the workers’ beggarly wages and the state’s policy of
rooting out religion, closing churches and repressing priests; the one
concrete instance they gave was his sermon on the eve of Pascha 1935, in
which he was said to have compared the first Christian centuries with
present-day Russia as to the persecution of believers and their pastors.
The other witnesses — F. Anipkin, warden of the church of Manino in the
Kalach district, the former psalmist M. Merkulov, and M. Gurov, whose
house he lodged in — denied any counter-revolutionary propaganda and were
not heeded. In August 1935 the indictment was confirmed and the case sent
to the special collegium of the Voronezh regional court, which on 10
September 1935 gave him five years in the camps; on 12 November he was
sent off. In his last years he went blind and dropsy set in, and of it he
died in the camp in 1937 — the Russian calendar keeps him on 9 September of
the old style, 22 September, and in the synaxes of the new martyrs and
confessors of Solovki and the saints of Voronezh and Lipetsk. He was
rehabilitated on 30 June 1992, and the Council of Bishops of 13–16 August
2000 numbered him among the new martyrs and confessors of Russia; the
life is from the Voronezh diocese’s own site.
