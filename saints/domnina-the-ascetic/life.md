# Domnina the Ascetic

Domnina was born of well-believing and wealthy parents, and gave herself to God from infancy, taking up the labours of the desert and the restraint of every bodily impulse. She made herself a hut beside her mother's garden and passed day and night in it, weeping without ceasing, so that her tears wet not only her cheeks but her clothing as well, for the clothing she used was made of hair.

At cockcrow she would go into the church and offer God a hymn of praise together with the whole crowd of the faithful. Her food was lentils softened in water, and she bore the whole labour of that fasting although her body was frail and she was, the life says, all but dead while yet alive.

She covered her face carefully with a cloth, so that neither could she see anyone's face nor anyone hers. Her hair garment covered her to the knees, and she spoke easily and gently with all who came to her. Living so, day and night, she was raised up at the last, with the works that had pleased God, to Christ her beloved Bridegroom. doxologia gives her no country, no year and no hymn.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, under the epithet «η Νέα», the New, and it is saint.gr that gives her a country and a century: she came of the city of Cyrrhus in Syria and lived in the first half of the fifth century, of parents pious and rich who had brought her up as Christianly as could be and gave her the means for almsgiving and other good works. The bishop Theodoret, seeing how living her piety was, thought much of her and used her as an example to the rich young women who supposed they could reconcile their Christianity with worldly show and vanity; and whenever he saw a girl who longed to take the road of the Gospel, he told her to keep Domnina's company. In the late afternoons, the page says, she was in the habit of going to the church next door to her and making her petitions there in the silence of it, and with such holy occupations she ended her life and went to the everlasting dwellings that had been her constant and burning desire the whole time she was on earth.

The Сретенский monastery’s calendar keeps her on the Julian 1 March as «Прп. Домнины Сирийской (ок. 450-460).», and its life page gives first a summary of its own and then the whole of the chapter the blessed Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, wrote about her in his history of the lovers of God. The summary makes her a disciple of the holy [Maron](/saints/maron-the-hermit), whose memory it puts on 14 February; says that she built herself a hut thatched with straw in her mother’s garden and struggled in it, eating nothing but lentils soaked in water; and that she went to the church every morning and every evening so veiled that nobody ever saw her face.

Theodoret, who knew her, says it at more length and in the present tense. The booth was made of millet stalks; she wetted not only her face but the hair shirt she wore with ceaseless tears; she went to the church close by after the cocks had crowed, and again at the end of the day, to give praise there with the other men and women, holding and teaching that a place set apart for God is more to be honoured than any other, and she persuaded her mother and her brothers to spend their wealth on the house of God. Her body was worn out with fasting and all but half dead, the skin like a membrane over thin bones.

She lived open to anyone who wished to see her, men or women, but took no one’s face in and showed her own to nobody, being covered by a veil that reached her knees; her voice was weak and indistinct and her words came with unceasing tears. Often, Theodoret writes, she took his right hand and put it to her eyes and gave it back so wet that it was as though the hand itself were pouring out streams. She served the great ascetics he had already written of, and looked after those who came to her, lodging them with the shepherd of her village and finding them everything they needed out of her mother’s and her brothers’ estate; and she sent him bread and vegetables and lentils soaked in water when he came into that northern country.

The one thing the two hosts do not agree on is when she died, and the folder now carries both readings. saint.gr put her in the first half of the fifth century, which is what the floruit here records; the Sretensky summary closes «Святая подвижница мирно отошла ко Господу около 450–460 годов», about 450 to 460, which is also what the day’s own line prints, and that is entered as a death and marked as approximate. The day prints no hymn under her name.
