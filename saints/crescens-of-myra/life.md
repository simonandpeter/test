# Crescens of Myra

Crescens was of Myra in Lycia, of a distinguished family and advanced in years. Seeing that ungodliness was growing proud and the law of the idols being exalted, and that many were held down in the slavery of error and worshipped things without souls, he went in among them and urged them to leave their vanity and turn to the God whom the Christians honour, as the maker of all things and the giver of life to every breathing thing.

The governor called him a poor wretched creature for choosing torture of his own free will; the saint answered that it is a greater happiness and honour to suffer for Christ. Asked about his station, his country and his name, he said to all of it only that he was a Christian. The governor pressed him to do the idols honour at least in form, and he refused, saying that the body cannot do anything other than what the soul, which governs it, wills.

So he was given over to torture, and in the midst of it he gave up his soul into the hand of God. The life gives no emperor, no year and no manner of death beyond that.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and adds one thing the Romanian life has not got: it makes him a disciple of the Apostle Paul. saint.gr says he was of Myra in Lycia and lived in the time when idolatry was all-powerful, and that as Paul's disciple he showed an admirable piety through the whole of his life; even at an advanced age he did not stop labouring for the return of the idolaters to the Christian faith. When the prefect of the city learnt of what he was doing, the page says, he called the saint in and advised him to stop preaching, because he was putting his life in danger and it would be unjust for him to suffer a martyr's death in his old age. Crescens answered with modesty, and with a bravery unusual for his years, that the tortures would be a benefaction to him and the death he was threatened with a gain.

Its couplet is an image of the fire: «Θάμβος βλέπειν Κρήσκεντα τοῦ πυρὸς μέσον, Ἡγούμενον λειμῶνα τερπνὸν τὴν φλόγα» — it is a wonder to see Crescens in the midst of the fire, counting the flame a pleasant meadow; and its third line names the day, «Κάτθανε καὶ Κρήσκης πέμπτῃ δεκάτῃ πυρὶ λαύρῳ». The Greek hymns take up what the two calendars agree on least: the kontakion says that though he received the contest in old age he had a young man's mind.
