# Blaise of Sebaste

Blaise spent all the years of his life meekly and uprightly, in a manner pleasing to God and without reproach, keeping away from every evil work, in the times when pagan idol-service was still honoured and many men worshipped the creation and the work of their own hands. The citizens of Sebaste in Cappadocia saw how he lived, and some of the right-believing among them took pains to make him their bishop; and he shepherded the flock of Christ well, in years when persecutions came often upon the Church and many entered the struggle of suffering and were crowned as martyrs.

In those days the great martyr Eustratius, first of his five companions, contended unto blood under Diocletian. While Eustratius sat in prison Blaise visited him by night, giving much gold to the guards, and called him blessed for the manliness of his suffering; and he took from him the testament entrusted to him, which Eustratius had written before his end, ordering what should be done for his body and for his goods, as it is written in the account of his passion.

Blaise hid himself from the anger of the tyrants, as many saints then did, not giving himself up to torture without God's will; in the city the Christians were few and most fled into the desert, the mountains and the caves for fear of the torments. In the reigns of Diocletian and of Licinius he hid on a desert mountain called Argeos, where he had a cave for his ascetic labour. There he kept a still and hermit's life and prayed without ceasing; and the wild beasts came and were blessed by him. If they came at the hour when he was at prayer they stood before the cave and waited, not cutting his thought off from God, and would not go until he had laid his hands on them; and any of them that fell sick was healed by the laying on of his hands.

At that time the governor Agricola ordered his hunters to take a multitude of man-eating beasts of every kind, meaning to make the death of Christ's disciples the more cruel; some he killed with the sword, some he burned in fire, some he drowned in water, and some he gave to the beasts. The hunters, ranging the mountains and the deserts, came to Argeos, saw a crowd of beasts playing before the cave, went in and found Blaise praying in it as in a chamber. They said nothing to him but went back and told Agricola, and he sent soldiers to bring in whatever Christians were hiding there.

On the road the pagans turned to God for the saint's gentleness and teaching and for his wonders, and healings were given through his prayers not to men only but to beasts. A woman brought him her only son, who had a fish-bone stuck in his throat so that he could not speak and was near death; Blaise put his hand on the child's throat and prayed that the Saviour take out the bone by his unseen power, and also that whenever anything of the kind should happen among men or cattle and someone should name him, saying «O God, for the prayers of thy servant Blaise, help me», the Lord would hasten to help. The child was made whole at once.

Nearer to Sebaste a poor widow who had nothing but one piglet came weeping to him, for a wolf had run off with it. He smiled and told her not to grieve, for her piglet would be given back to her alive and unhurt; and as he went on his way the wolf ran up to the widow, carrying the piglet in its mouth, and let it go whole before her, and fled into the desert.

In Sebaste Agricola threw him into prison, and at the judgement greeted him as a friend of the gods; Blaise returned the greeting but told him not to call gods the demons who would be given to the eternal fire with those who honour them. He was beaten long with thick staves, and hung on a tree and torn with iron tools, and both times sent back to prison. Then the poor widow killed the piglet the wolf had given back, boiled its head and feet, put them in a dish, gathered what seeds and fruits her poverty allowed into a basket, lit a candle and carried it all to him in the prison. He tasted the food, blessed it, and told her to keep his memory in that manner every year, for then nothing needful would be lacking in her house, and whoever else should do the like would be filled with God's gifts.

Seven God-fearing women followed him as he was taken back to prison, gathering from the ground the blood that dripped from his body and anointing themselves with it, and were seized. Told to sacrifice, they asked first to be allowed to wash in the nearby lake and to have the gods brought there; the governor, delighted, had his idols sealed into sacks and given them to carry. At the deep place on the shore they sent the soldiers away for modesty's sake and threw every idol into the water, where the unclean gods sank like the lead that sealed them. Brought back, they told the governor that gods who could not save themselves from women's hands could deliver nobody from danger.

A furnace was heated, lead was melted, iron combs and seven plates of brass shaped like garments were made red-hot and set on one side; on the other were laid new bright clothes and women's ornaments, and they were told to choose. One of them, who had two children, took up a bright garment and threw it into the furnace, and her children said to her: «Mother, do not leave us on this earth to perish; as thou hast fed us with thy sweet milk, so feed us with the heavenly kingdom.» They were hung up and torn with the combs, and the soldiers saw milk run from their wounds instead of blood. Thrown into the furnace, they came out unhurt, for the fire went out.

The governor had their heads cut off. At the place of their end they knelt and prayed to be numbered with Thecla, the first woman martyr, through the prayers of their father and shepherd Blaise, who had led them into the true way; and the two children came to their mother and said that her crown was ready in heaven, and asked to be left to the brave and much-enduring bishop. So the seven women were beheaded.

Then Blaise was brought out again and asked whether he would sacrifice. He answered that he did not fear the governor's threats, and that he gave his body for Christ, over which God alone had power. Told he would be thrown into the lake, he ran ahead, signed the water with the Cross and walked on it as on dry land, and sat down in the middle of it as on the ground, calling to the tyrant's men on the bank: if they had gods, let them show their power and walk on the water too. Sixty-eight men called their gods by name, threw themselves in and perished. An angel came down and told him to receive the crown made ready for him by God.

He came back to the bank with his face like light, went to the governor and rebuked him, and sentence of death was passed: Blaise, who had not obeyed, had slandered the emperor, reviled the gods and destroyed sixty-eight men in the water by his spells, was to be beheaded together with the two children. At the place of execution he prayed for himself and for the whole world, and above all for those who should come after him and keep his memory, that no sickness come near them, that their houses be filled with every good thing and their prayers be heard in every need; a bright cloud came down and overshadowed him, and the voice of Christ was heard from heaven: «All thy askings I will fulfil, my beloved struggler.»

The executioner cut off his honourable head, and after him the two children, in the same place outside the walls of Sebaste, on the eleventh day of February. A blessed woman named Elisa took the martyrs' bodies and buried them there with honour, and healings were given from the grave to the sick. When the widow heard of his end she honoured his memory with her piglet, gathering her acquaintance to her, for God had blessed her house with plenty; other right-believing people followed her, and from then began the custom, kept by some to this day, of keeping the memory of the hieromartyr Blaise after the manner of that widow — bringing candles and incense to church, feasting those of one faith, and giving alms to the poor and destitute. The page gives no year.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and gives the year and the trade the Romanian page did not: saint.gr heads him «(; - 316)», says he studied medicine and gave his services free as an act of charity to the suffering and the sick, providing them their medicines and the cost of their nursing out of his own pocket, and that the Church then received him into the sacred clergy and elected him bishop of Sebaste. Under Licinius the prefect Agricola arrested him; the soldiers scourged him with rods, hung him from a piece of wood, took him bound to prison and then threw him into the depths of a lake, out of which he was saved by God's wonderworking intervention, and only then was he beheaded. Two of the day's other entries hang on his martyrdom: [two youths](/saints/two-youths-of-sebaste) he catechised and baptised in prison, and [seven women](/saints/seven-women-of-sebaste) who rebuked Agricola for it. The page adds that his synaxis used to be held at his martyrion, which stood near the martyrion of the apostle Philip.

The Serbian church keeps him on the same day. The Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović makes him a Cappadocian by birth, God-fearing and gentle from early childhood and chosen bishop of Sebaste for his virtues, a great spiritual and moral light in a pagan city; through the hard persecution he strengthened his flock and visited Christ's martyrs in the prisons. When Sebaste was left with no Christians at all — some killed, the rest fled — the old man withdrew to Mount Argaeus and settled in a cave, where the wild beasts recognised a holy man, pressed close about him and were gently stroked.

The pursuers found him even there and took him off to judgment. On the road, the Prologue says, he healed a boy with a bone caught in his throat, and at a poor widow's complaint that a wolf had snatched her piglet he made the wolf bring it back by the force of his prayer. The judges beat him and raked him, and his steadiness turned many pagans to the faith. Seven women and two children were imprisoned with him; the women were beheaded first, then he and the two children. He suffered in 316. The people pray to him, it adds, for the thriving of their livestock and for protection from wild animals, and in the West against sore throats as well.

The Ohrid Prologue's entry for the Julian 13 December names him in another man's story, and in the last scene of it: before the death of [Eustratius of Arabraca](/saints/eustratius-of-arabraca), the Roman commander of Satalion who gave himself up to the governor Lysias and died in a fiery furnace, it was Blaise who communed him in the prison — and the book sends its reader on to its own 11 February, which is the day this corpus keeps him on. That last communion is why this folder now carries Eustratius as a relation in both directions; nothing else on it was touched, and it takes no Serbian attestation row from a day that is not its own.
