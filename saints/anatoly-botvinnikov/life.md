# Anatoly (Botvinnikov)

He is one of two of the ten people on this line for whom the Russian calendar prints a life, and his begins in a peasant family. He was born on 15 October 1881 in the village of Kopani in the Bykhov district of the province of Mogilev. When the boy was fifteen his father died, and his mother and he moved to the province of Tobolsk, where they were given land and set up a holding, and he farmed as his father had.

Then the Russo-Japanese war began and he was called up as a private. In the fighting he found himself at Port Arthur, where he was wounded; he was taken prisoner and spent about a year in captivity, and the consequences of the wound stayed with him for life, so that his arm and his back would not move freely. After the peace with Japan he was released and came home. He had never thought of serving the Church before, but the war, the nearness of death, the wound and the life among pagan captors turned his soul over and set one thing in front of the rest, the salvation of his soul; and he decided that if he lived he would become a monk.

In April 1906 he entered one of the Siberian monasteries, where for several years he did obediences of every kind. In 1912 he was sent to China as an Orthodox missionary. A year later he came back to Russia and entered the Nikolo-Terebensky monastery in the province of Tver, and there he was tonsured, keeping the name he already had. In 1920 he was ordained hierodeacon; a year later the Soviet authorities abolished the monastery but left the church, where he went on serving; some years after that he was ordained hieromonk, and in 1928 he was sent to the church of the village of Sorogozhye in the Tver region, where the persecution of the early thirties found him.

On 27 October 1930 an assistant OGPU officer questioned two members of the Komsomol from the Sorogozhye village soviet, a young man of twenty-four and a girl of twenty, and both gave evidence against their parish priest. What they reported was mostly what their own mothers had said at home: that after the service on a Sunday in October the priest had preached a sermon complaining that life had become hard for him, that the Soviet power was wronging the servants of the church with taxes, and that he had had to pay a tax he could not bear. One of them added that his mother had come home from church and turned on him for being a Komsomol member who did not believe in God, had cursed all the communists for driving the priests and religion out of the world, and had then asked him for money to help the priest, because the faithful had started a subscription list for the suffering father.

The officer thought that evidence enough, and on 5 November the hieromonk was arrested and shut in the prison at Bezhetsk. Having nothing else on him, the officer did not say what he had been arrested for but asked him to tell about himself; he said he came of peasants, had served as a soldier in the Russo-Japanese war, and had spent all the rest of his time in a monastery and as a priest in a parish.

He was already in prison while the investigator spent three more days gathering proof of his anti-state activity from the villagers. What was collected was this: that at Pascha that year, in the village of Khalkovo, he had told the peasants that the Sorogozhye church was old and would soon fall down and could not be served in, and that they should petition for the opening of the Alexis church; and that he had turned to them and said, believers, do not abandon the church, do not believe the fables that there is no God — God is, remember that there will be a judgement, the living and the dead will rise, and all sinners will get their deserts and will fall into the fire of Gehenna. One witness repeated the sermon about the taxes and said that the priest had wept as he preached it; her mother, called in to be questioned, answered in one sentence that she had been in church about October and had heard the priest weep as he said that life under the Soviet power had become hard. Others refused to testify at all, saying they knew of the sermon only by hearsay.

On 18 November the investigator questioned him. He answered: I do not admit myself guilty of the charge brought against me, and I have nothing to add. On 1 December the indictment was drawn up, charging him with spreading false rumours among the peasantry about a supposed persecution of the church and its servants by the Soviet power, and on 10 December 1930 an OGPU troika sentenced him to three years in a corrective labour camp. Men like him, the page says, were never released before the day named in the sentence, in the hope that hard labour would kill them in the camp; and in spite of the illness his old wound had left him, he lived, and in 1934 he was given a parish in the village of Dubrovskoye in the Tver region. Nothing changed in him there: he served as zealously, preached as he had, and tended his parishioners tirelessly.

He served that parish three and a half years. On 15 October 1937 he was arrested, and the next day the investigator told him that the investigation knew exactly of his counter-revolutionary activity and that he should tell of it himself; he answered that he had engaged in no counter-revolutionary activity, had carried on no anti-Soviet agitation, and did not admit himself guilty. The investigator could collect almost nothing from the witnesses this time — only that he had compared life before the revolution with life after it, and that he was not in favour of the state loans. He was held in the prison at Bezhetsk, and on 11 November an NKVD troika ruled that he should be shot. He was shot on 13 November 1937, the civil date of this menologion day. He was numbered among the saints at the Jubilee Council of Bishops in August 2000, for veneration by the whole church.
