# Gregory (Raevsky)

Gregory Raevsky was born on 28 September 1888 in the village of Zavidovo in Tver province, a deacon’s son. After the Moscow seminary he married Lydia Belyaeva, a priest’s daughter of the same village, and in 1912 was ordained priest to its Dormition church. The couple had no children, and when a husband and wife of the village died almost at the same time in 1913, leaving three orphans, they took in one of them, eight-year-old Anna, and brought her up as their own beside their daughter Nina, born in 1922. He was first arrested in 1927 and held in the Butyrka on a charge of spreading false rumours, and released after two months when his innocence came out.

On 9 February 1930, in the first winter of collectivization, a village demonstration for the collective farm failed because most of the people were in church, where some six hundred had come to the service and more than half took communion. The OGPU opened an inquiry the next day, took statements from Komsomol members, the village soviet’s chairman and the reading-room keeper about his sermons, and arrested him on 14 August 1930. On 5 January 1931 the OGPU troika gave him five years of labour camp, and he was sent to the works of the White Sea–Baltic canal. Freed early in 1934, he came back to Zavidovo and served again; his wife died on 12 May 1935. In the summer of 1936 he was one of only two priests who came when the district’s clergy were summoned to discuss the draft Stalin constitution, and he declined to discuss it, saying that priests had been deprived of civil rights.

He was arrested again on 30 July 1937. Questioned, he denied any agitation, and when the investigators called in his parishioners, the neighbours they questioned said they knew him well as a man loyal to the authorities. From the prison he sent short notes to his family and to the priest Nicholas Dmitrov, and before he was taken to the prison at Tver he wrote to Nina to study hard and heed those he had asked to take the place of father and mother. On 29 September 1937, the day after his forty-ninth birthday, the NKVD troika sentenced him to death, and he was shot as soon as the sentence was read. He lies in a common grave in one of the cemeteries of Tver, the exact place unknown. He was glorified among the new martyrs and confessors of Russia by the jubilee council of bishops in August 2000.
