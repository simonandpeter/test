# Michael (Yeregodsky)

Michael Petrovich Yeregodsky was born on 17 September 1878 to a hereditary
church servant, a psalmist, which no doubt shaped his wish to give
himself to the service of God after his father, grandfather and
great-grandfather; Gabriel, bishop of Veliky Ustyug, allowed him to act as
psalmist in place of his father, then ill. People who give themselves to
God and try to bring their life into line with the Lord’s commandments
shine from within, as if the eyes reflected a soul that has touched
eternity, and so Michael remained in people’s memory — handsome, radiant
— and his wife Anna Artemyevna matched him, admired for her beauty. The
wages received in church were not enough to feed a large family, so the
parents and their six children worked much on their own field, one of
his daughters, Elizabeth, recalled: to make a garden he and the elder
sons had to grub out forest, and on the ground won they planted potatoes
and rye; the farm was strong, with a horse and a cow, and they lived in a
big two-storey house whose chief room was the father’s library. For all
the hardness of life he could set the bustle of the day aside and be for a
moment simply a romantic — for his beloved Elizabeth he always found
daisies, the forest’s gift; after his arrest she would search for them,
and with his arrest the daisies vanished from the forest. He loved the
village children and taught them church singing, and the village
respected him greatly and came to him with every request, to write a
petition or an application, and he refused no one. 1937 drew near, and the
family’s life was measured in hours, each day bringing new misfortune: the
large family was laid under impossible taxes, and horse and cow were taken
one after the other; he was twice sent to Koslan for a year’s forced
labour; the family’s house was taken, leaving them two small rooms, and
their ploughland was taken to build a hospital, a scrap of barren ground
given instead. His young children were persecuted too — not moved up to
the third class, sent to the timber-cutting; the eldest, Vyacheslav, was
taken into an office on condition that he renounce his father, and left
home; Vladimir and Peter went on working in the forest; and soon the elder
children and the relatives had to leave to escape persecution — Vyacheslav
to Leningrad, where he worked in a factory and died in the siege, Peter to
Sevastopol; Vera broke her leg at the timber-cutting, her father got her to
Archangel in time for treatment, and she did not come home but stayed
among strangers. His brother George begged him to leave with him, and he
refused: he would not leave his native place, where his parents were
buried. Only the daughters Liza and Zhenya stayed with him and Anna. In
those inhuman conditions he still found strength to help others: the
villagers had been forbidden to go to church and did not know what to do,
and he began to gather the parishioners at his house — they were tracked,
and the day after one gathering the headmaster called the two girls in to
ask who had come and why; we know nothing, they said, we slept on the
stove and saw nothing. Then the church was destroyed before the people’s
eyes while he was haymaking in the fields: the villagers ran to tell him,
the church was beheaded, the earth shook with pain as the huge heavy bell
fell and seemed to open and take it in, the houses trembled and glass
shattered — so they killed the church. When he reached the village they
were already tearing the boards off; he begged and pleaded that the
building be left, that it was guilty of nothing and could still serve
people, and when the young satraps would not listen he turned to his
fellow villagers for help — and, wonderfully, the building at least was
saved. Beside it a great bonfire blazed, books carried out by the armful,
icons flung through the windows into the fire, and the bolder girls
snatched out icons not yet alight and carried them home. Nothing was holy
to the killers: from the church they went to the old cemetery, did not
scruple to drag the cast-iron slab from the grave of his father Peter
Alexandrovich and send it with the bell to be melted down — he managed to
hide the cross from the grave, slab and cross sent by the Vologda diocese
in honour of its servant; and even that memory was outraged: when Anna
died the children set the rescued cross on her grave, and the next day it
was gone, and Liza found only half of it — the other half stood on someone
else’s grave. Having wrecked the old world they pressed another life and
other ideas on the people, and Michael was in the way and was to be
removed. On 10 August 1937 he and his wife were at work in the fields
seventeen versts from the village when a messenger ordered him to the
village soviet; he went back alone, and that night the searchers came to
his young children — Zhenya slept through it, Liza watched the thieving.
Father did not come back from the soviet; in the morning their mother
came, could not believe it, and wept long, saying he had come through
three wars alive and now they had taken him for ever and we shall never
see him again. He was not allowed to take leave of his family; he and
several others were put on a cart and driven to Koslan, and the villagers,
learning of it, came out on the road to say goodbye, many weeping, some
trying to slip bread or money into his hand; the road along which the
innocent were driven has not grown over to this day, though everything
round it is long overgrown with willow, and the children ran to it
waiting for the cart to reappear. Soon the family had to fight for its
existence: everything was taken, the house confiscated, and the
kind-hearted villagers were not allowed to help the family of an enemy of
the people — whatever they brought was taken away next day — while the
sick woman and her children were made to pay tax; the relatives who had
got away helped as they could, Vyacheslav sending money from Leningrad.
The youngest son Vladimir came back from the war wanting to live in his
village and could get no work anywhere as the son of an enemy of the
people, and left, taking the youngest sister Evgenia; Liza would not leave
her parents’ house, waiting for her father or some word of him. Thirty
years later a stranger came into her house and told her that her father
had been shot as soon as he reached Syktyvkar; later it became known that
he was arrested on 10 August 1937, sentenced on 29 August by the NKVD
troika to the highest penalty, and shot on 31 August 1937 — 18 August of
the old style, the day the Russian calendar keeps him. He was
rehabilitated under the law of the Russian republic of 18 October 1991,
and only then did Elizabeth begin to speak of her father to his
grandchildren, having kept everything in her heart till then; her
daughter Gemma remembered coming to visit and finding her mother
overjoyed — she had found in the forest the daisies her father had given
her sixty years before, gone with his arrest and back after his
rehabilitation, and had moved the wonder-flowers from the forest and
planted them by the house. The building of the church he once saved stands
to this day. The life follows Natalia Prokofieva’s lives of the new
martyrs of the Komi land; the Russian calendar keeps him also in the
synaxis of the saints of Komi.
