# Agapitus of Synnada

Doxologia's calendar names Agapitus, bishop of Synnada, for 18 February, and gives no life. His page carries the name, the date, an icon and a link to a second page of icons, and nothing else: no life tab, no troparion tab, not even the short notice some of its pages print. The only other thing kept under his name is a twentieth-century Greek icon from the collection called the Synaxarion of the saints of the day, on which he is painted together with Leo, bishop of Rome, and the martyr Parigorius, who are kept on the same day.

So what this entry can say from this source is the name, the see and the day. Where he came from, when he lived, and what he did or suffered, doxologia does not print, and nothing here supplies it.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and gives the life the Romanian calendar withheld altogether. He came, saint.gr says, from Cappadocia, and was born of devout and God-loving parents; he lived in the time of the emperors Diocletian, whose reign it gives as 284 to 305, and Maximian, 285 to 305. At a young age he went off to a monastery near Synaos and became a monk, was loved by the abbot for the virtue of his life and was taught the sacred letters, and received from God the gift of wonders too: by his prayer he killed a great serpent that had appeared near the monastery and was destroying men and beasts, and he did good to those who ran to him.

Later, under the emperor Licinius, whose reign the page gives as 308 to 323, Agapitus was taken into the army. There he saw tormented for their faith in Christ the gloriously victorious martyrs [Victorinus](/saints/victorinus-companion-of-agapitus), [Dorotheus](/saints/dorotheus-companion-of-agapitus), [Theodulus](/saints/theodulus-companion-of-agapitus) and [Agrippas](/saints/agrippas-companion-of-agapitus) — whom the same Greek day keeps, and whose only account anywhere on the site is this mention — and many others besides; and he wished at once to become a partaker of their martyrdom. But while they were made perfect in Christ by the sword, he was kept whole and unhurt, though they struck him with a javelin, by God's dispensation, so that he might lead many to salvation.

After his military service, when the emperor was now Constantine the Great, 324 to 337, he gave himself to the study of the holy Gospel, and the bishop of the city of Synaos ordained him presbyter; and after his bishop's falling asleep, by the common mind of clergy and people, he was elected bishop himself. Having exercised the high priesthood in a way pleasing to God, the page says, he fell asleep in peace. Its couplet plays on his name: the Master whom you loved, Agapitus, is he who calls you to the places you loved.
