# David of Thessalonica

David was born and grew up in the great and shining city of Thessalonica. From childhood he despised every bodily ease, and renouncing the world and what is in it he left friends and kinsmen, honour, passing glory, money and possessions, and his own soul besides, as the Gospel commands, and followed the Master, taking the cross in his youth. He was tonsured a monk and stayed in the monastery of the holy martyrs Theodore and Mercurius, which was called Koutouliaton.

There he lived in stillness, labouring beyond what is human and keeping every virtue with diligence, and giving the first place to abstinence and to humility, knowing that a full stomach drives off vigilance and chastity and that vainglory destroys all the virtues utterly. Reading the Scriptures day and night, he wondered at the saints before the Law and under it — Abel for his sacrifice, Abraham for his faith, Joseph for his wisdom, Job for his patience, Moses the lawgiver, Daniel and the three children kept unharmed in the fire and among the lions.

Reading also the lives of the ascetics after the Lord's incarnation, he was astonished most of all at Symeon of the Wonderful Mountain and at his namesake, and at Daniel and Patapius the stylites, who lived under no roof at all, tormented by wind and rain and snow. He wept over those lives, and resolved to live in the same strait way as long as he could, that he might find room after death. So one day, his heart burning, he climbed an almond tree on the right-hand side of the church, made himself what little bed he could on a branch, and lived there.

The stylites at least had a firm standing, the life says: their pillars were built and stood, and when they slept or had other need of rest they were still. This one moved always on the branch of a tree and never had rest, tormented by rains and winds and cruelly afflicted by the snow, burnt by the summer sun. Yet his soul did not grow slack or small or idle, and his face, that had the look of an angel, did not change but was beautiful as a rose.

At last an angel told him to come down, and he called his disciples, told them the vision and had them build him a cell. The archbishop Dorotheus came up with his most reverent clergy, kissed him and brought him down from the tree with great reverence and led him into his cell, and they kept a great feast. In the cell God gave him such grace that he drove out devils, gave light to the blind and healed every incurable sickness in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ — a young man with a demon, who cried at the door that fire came out of the cell and burned him and was freed at a word and the sign of the cross; and a woman altogether blind, who was led to him and whose eyes he sealed with the cross, and who saw clearly and went away giving thanks.

After some years Dorotheus died and Aristides was chosen in his place. In that time the barbarians were doing great damage in Thessaly, and the prefect of Illyricum wrote to the metropolitan to go himself to the emperor, or send some worthy man, and ask that a prefect be appointed and fixed at Thessalonica, which had only a steward. The citizens, gathered in the church, cried out with one voice that David should be sent, so that the emperor might be moved by the sight of a holy man.

David first pleaded his age, then remembered the angel's word and consented, and told the archbishop plainly that the emperor would grant by God's help whatever he asked, but that they would not see David alive again: coming back from the imperial city, a hundred and twenty-six stadia short of his poor cell, he would go to his Master. Taking two of his disciples, Theodore and Demetrius, he went down to the harbour and sailed.

At Byzantium the emperor Justinian was away, and the empress Theodora received him with much honour and reverence, wondering at his face and its grey hairs, asking his prayer and his blessing; and she told the emperor on his return that God had sent them His angel, and that she had thought she saw Abraham himself. The next day, before the whole council, David took live coals and incense in his bare palms and censed the emperor and all of them for more than an hour without his hand being hurt at all. The emperor rose from his throne and met him with reverence, granted everything the metropolitan's letters asked and everything else he asked by word of mouth, signed them in red letters and put them into his hand, saying: Honourable father, pray for me.

He sailed for Thessalonica, and as he had foretold he did not reach the city. Off the lighthouse he told his disciples that the hour of his end had come, and bade them bury his relics in the monastery where he had lived; and at the place called Emboli, from which his monastery could be seen, he looked towards it, crossed himself, kissed his disciples and gave up his soul into the hands of God.

There was a fearful wind at that moment, and the ship, which had been running full, suddenly stood still for a long time and would not move at all; a sweetness of incense came that cannot be told, and voices were heard in the air praising the Lord. Then the voices ceased and the ship moved, but not to the usual harbour: it went to the western side of the city, to the place where the pagans had once thrown the relics of the holy martyrs Theodulus and Agathopodes. The whole city came out with the archbishop, carried his relics into the monastery, made a four-cornered wooden coffin for them and buried them with honour; the prefecture was moved to Thessalonica by imperial command, and the saint was kept every year in that monastery.

A hundred and fifty years later an abbot there named Demetrius, wanting a small portion of the relics, set men to dig the grave; the slab above it split into four at once, and seeing that the saint did not wish it he left off. His disciple Sergius, who became abbot after him and at last archbishop of Thessalonica for his virtues, prayed often to be allowed a portion; and being assured from God that the saint consented, he opened the grave, and a wonderful fragrance came out, and seeing the body still whole he did not dare take any part of it but only a few hairs of the head and the beard. From that time those who love Christ kept those hairs carefully and kissed them on his feast, which is kept on the twenty-sixth day of June. The life names Justinian and Theodora and prints no year, so none is recorded here.
