# Panaretos of Paphos

Panaretos was born at the beginning of the eighteenth century, most probably at Peristeronopigi of Famagusta, saint.gr says, and he became a monk there in the house of Saint Anastasius. By 1764 he was abbot of the monastery of the Panagia Palouriotissa, and in 1767 he came to the throne of Paphos and shepherded it until he fell asleep in the Lord. He lived in the hard years of the Turkish rule, without schools or free learning, and made himself broadly educated by his own effort and by studying in what schools the monasteries kept.

He had wide horizons and combined them with a severe ascetic life which he hid carefully. He ate sparely and without luxury, like the poorest Cypriot of his time; he kept long vigils at prayer, confessed with humility and served with reverence. Knowing that the greatest victory is over oneself, and to keep in mind that the people God had entrusted to him were heavily chained in slavery, he wore a brass chain and an iron chain on his body. He took up everyone's troubles as his own, and cared as much for the learning of his enslaved people, and for letters and the arts, as for theology and church affairs.

He paid the whole cost of printing Theophilos Korydaleus's «Περί γενέσεως και φθοράς κατ' Αριστοτέλην» in 1780 and helped with the archimandrite Kyprianos's Chronological History of Cyprus in 1788; he had a silver case made for the skull of the Apostle Philip, now at Omodos, and an icon painted showing himself offering it; he rebuilt or renewed the church of his monastery at Peristeronopigi and the churches of Nikokleia, Drousia, Drymou, Arodes, Theletra and Philousa, and the monasteries of Chrysorroyiatissa in 1770, of the Cross at Omodos and of the Cross at Minthi. In 1774 local magnates were denounced to the Sultan for misrule through his agency, and in 1783–84 he went with the other bishops to Constantinople, where they got the tyrant Hadjibakkis removed.

He worked wonders while still alive: his synaxarion tells how he rebuked a priest who lied and swore he had not embezzled money, bidding him be silent, and the man was struck dumb, and when he was dying he sent a letter, and his bishop came, and at his repentance his tongue was loosed. He foresaw his own death and dug his grave himself, on the spot where the sanctuary of the cathedral church of Saint Theodore in Paphos now stands; he foresaw too that the former bishop Parthenios of Karpathos had put in at the harbour of Paphos, and sent for him and lodged him, and Parthenios buried him the next day. He had given orders to be buried in the clothes he wore, but his protosyngellos disobeyed him for the first time out of love, and so the chains were found, grown in places into his flesh.

The people of God acknowledged his holiness at once, and his formal enrolment among the saints of the Church of Cyprus was made in the days of the Cypriot patriarch of Constantinople Gerasimos III. His chains are kept today at the monastery of Stavrovouni; an old wall-painting of him is on the island of Salamina in Greece, and icons of him were made later all over Cyprus. Paphos honours him particularly, and Metropolitan Chrysostomos II of Paphos raised a church to him at the village of Koloni in 1989.
