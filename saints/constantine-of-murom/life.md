# Constantine of Murom

The princes Constantine of Murom and his sons Michael and Theodore lived, saint.gr says, in the eleventh and twelfth centuries in Russia. The blessed Constantine, a descendant of the prince [Vladimir](/saints/vladimir-the-great) who was equal to the apostles, asked his own father, the prince Sviatoslav of Chernigov, to make him ruler of the city of Murom, which was inhabited by idolaters, so that he might enlighten them and spread the Christian faith among them.

He sent his son Michael to Murom as his envoy, and the idolaters killed him. When the prince came up to the walls of the city with his army the inhabitants submitted and received him, but for a long while they resisted the truth and stayed in the darkness and error of idolatry. At one point they came up to the prince's house meaning to murder him; but he came out before them unafraid, holding in his hands the icon of the Mother of God of Murom, and suddenly the idolaters submitted and were baptised in the waters of the river Oka.

On the spot where his son Michael had been killed the faithful prince built a church in honour of the Annunciation of the Mother of God, and later raised another dedicated to the holy Boris and Gleb. He laboured much for the spreading of the Christian faith among the people of Murom, and in that apostolic work his son the prince Theodore helped him with a warm zeal. In 1129 the prince Constantine fell asleep in peace and was buried in the church of the Annunciation, beside his sons Michael and Theodore.

The Russian church calendar keeps him on 21 мая ст. ст. with his two sons and gives the three of them two readings. Prince Константин — Ярослав Святославич in the world — was a descendant of Vladimir, equal to the apostles, and asked his father, prince Святослав of Chernigov, for the city of Murom as his portion, because it was settled by pagans and he wished to enlighten that country with the light of the Christian faith. He sent his son Михаил ahead to the men of Murom as his envoy, and the pagans killed him. When the prince came up to the city with his retinue the inhabitants submitted and received him, though, the second reading says, they would not agree for a long time to leave their paganism.

He did not force them to the faith of Christ. He built first a church of the Annunciation on the place where his son had been killed, and soon after another in honour of «свв. Бориса и Глеба», the two passion-bearers; he called the elders of the city to him more than once and urged them to change their faith, and the clergy who had come with him preached Christ to the people. Then a crowd of fierce pagans, discontented with the prince, came up to his house threatening him with death. He prayed, and went out boldly to the crowd of rioters with an icon of the Mother of God — the second reading calls it the Murom icon — and this so astonished them that they themselves asked to be baptised. The one reading says the baptism of the men of Murom was done solemnly, with the same rites as under Vladimir at Kiev; the other says it was done in the river Oka.

His son Феодор helped him zealously in spreading the faith of Christ among the people of Murom. In 1129 prince Константин died and was buried in the church of the Annunciation beside his sons, Михаил and Феодор. The second of the calendar’s readings sets the three of them in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, which is wider than the line’s own «(XII)» and than the year it prints for him, and no interval has been widened here on that account.
