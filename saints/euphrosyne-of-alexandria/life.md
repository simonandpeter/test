# Euphrosyne of Alexandria

There was in Alexandria a man named [Paphnutius](/saints/paphnutius-father-of-euphrosyne), rich, honoured and God-fearing, whose wife was barren; they grieved that they had no one to leave their goods to, gave alms, fasted and prayed, and at the prayer of a holy abbot God gave them a daughter, Euphrosyne. Her mother died when the girl was twelve, her father raised her on the Scriptures, and when he betrothed her to a rich young man she - already resolved to keep her virginity for Christ - fled her father's house.

She did it in the one way that could not be undone by searching: she put on a man's dress and came to a monastery of men, calling herself the eunuch Smaragdus, and the abbot, warning that so beautiful a face would trouble the brethren, gave "him" a cell apart under the elder Agapetus - who marvelled at how the young monk laboured in prayer, fasting and vigil day and night, serving God in purity of heart. Her father, meanwhile, mourned his vanished daughter and came often to that same monastery for consolation, and was comforted - by Smaragdus, whom the abbot sent to him, and whom he did not recognise through thirty-eight years.

Only on her deathbed did she tell him: I am your daughter Euphrosyne. Paphnutius fell upon her, wept over the two losses that were one, and when she had reposed took her cell himself and finished his life in it. The Romanian calendar keeps her on this day.

The Greek synaxarion keeps her on 25 September as the daughter of Paphnutius, and sets her in the years of Theodosius the Younger, around 410. She was an only daughter and very rich, and her father [Paphnutius](/saints/paphnutius-father-of-euphrosyne) the richest man in Alexandria. She lost her mother at twelve, and at eighteen her father meant to marry her to a young man of high rank; but she gave what she had to the poor, left the house in secret and, after many adventures, came disguised as a man to a men’s cenobitic monastery, where she took the name Smaragdus and lived thirty-eight years, the monks admiring her struggle and her ready service to all. At the end of her life she met her father again, when he too became a monk in the same monastery. The Greek calendar keeps her with three apolytikia, the first of them the common troparion of a nun, and a kontakion.
