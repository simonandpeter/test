# Nicholas the Studite

Nicholas was born at Kydonia in Crete in 792 and was taught his first letters there. While he was still young his parents sent him to Constantinople to his uncle Theophanes, a monk of the famous monastery of Studion, where he became a monk himself. In the quiet of the house he had the chance to take a great theological and literary education, and he came to be reckoned one of the greatest shorthand writers of his time.

In the years of the iconoclasm the monastery of Studion and its monks suffered heavy persecutions for their attachment to the struggle for the holy icons, and Nicholas had the same lot as the rest; which is why he was surnamed the Confessor. When the iconoclasm ended in 847 he was elected abbot of the monastery; a little later, in 850, he resigned, and in 859 he founded the monastery of Konoroby, helped by a rich and pious man called Samuel.

Even from there the ecclesiastical quarrels between the parties of the patriarchs Ignatius and Photius drew him away. He went to Prokonnesos, then to Mytilene, then to Hexamili on the Thracian peninsula, from which in 866 he was brought back under guard, as a prisoner of a sort, to Studion. The next year the emperor Basil I and the patriarch Ignatius, in his second patriarchate, offered the much-tried man the abbacy of Studion, and he, grieving at the disorder of the times, refused.

He fell asleep in peace on 4 February 868, and his honoured body was laid near the holy remains of the glorious Studites Naukratios and Theodore. The page prints his apolytikion, his kontakion and a megalynarion; the corpus holds only the first two, because it stores troparia and kontakia and nothing else.

The Serbian church keeps him on the Julian 4 February, and Bishop Nikolai's Ohrid Prologue tells a different course of the same life. The kinsman who drew him from Crete to Constantinople is [Theodore the Studite](/saints/theodore-the-studite) himself, abbot of the house, where saint.gr has an uncle Theophanes who was a monk of it; he came to visit, stayed, and was made a monk there. In the persecution of the Church by Leo the Armenian — the only source here to name him — Theodore and Nicholas were cruelly tormented, humiliated, beaten with ox sinews and at last thrown into prison, where they lay three years. On Theodore's death Nicholas became abbot of Studion, which in the Greek reading is instead an election in 847, a resignation three years after it, and a refusal of the office in 867.

Four miracles stand only in this source. He healed Eudokia, wife of the emperor Basil, and Helena, wife of the patrician Manuel; to Theophilos Melissenos, a nobleman whose children did not live, he gave his blessing over a newborn daughter and foretold that she would live and bear much fruit, which came to pass to her parents' joy. And on the day of his death he called the monks and asked what they lacked. Grain, they said. He who fed Israel in the wilderness, said the dying man, will send you wheat in plenty within three days — and on the third day a ship full of grain, sent by the emperor Basil, came in below the monastery. The Prologue puts his repose on 4 February 868, in the seventy-fifth year of his life, which is a year short of the span between the two dates saint.gr gives.
