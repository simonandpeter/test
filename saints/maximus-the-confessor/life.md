# Maximus the Confessor

The Russian calendar keeps 13 August (Julian) for his death and the translation of his relics; his chief memory is 21 January.

Maximus was born in Constantinople around 580 and grew up in a devout Christian family; he had a broad education — philosophy, grammar, rhetoric, the ancient authors, and a mastery of theological dialectic — and entering the imperial service became first secretary to the emperor Heraclius. Court life weighed on him, and he withdrew to the monastery at Chrysopolis across the Bosphorus, where he was tonsured and, for his humility, soon chosen igumen, though he said he remained a simple monk. In 633, at the request of the future patriarch Sophronius of Jerusalem, he left for Alexandria and began there the struggle of his life against the Monothelite heresy, which taught a single divine will and activity in Christ and so brought back the condemned Monophysitism by another door, and which by 630 held three patriarchal thrones of the East. He spent some six years in and around Alexandria; after Heraclius’s Ekthesis of 638 had imposed the one will, he spoke to people of every rank and station and they felt, his life says, an irresistible pull towards him. Under the brutal Constans II he went to Carthage, where in June 645 he disputed publicly with the Monothelite ex-patriarch Pyrrhus, who confessed his error and went with him to Rome to recant before Pope Theodore; African councils condemned the heresy; and when the Typos of 648 forbade all talk of one will or two, Maximus asked Pope Martin to bring the question before the whole Church. The Lateran council of October 649 — a hundred and fifty Western bishops and thirty-seven of the East, Maximus among them — condemned Monothelitism and anathematised the patriarchs Sergius, Paul and Pyrrhus.

Constans had the pope and Maximus seized, in 654; Maximus was charged with treason, imprisoned, exiled to Thrace in 656 and brought back to a Constantinople prison, and with two of his disciples was tortured: their tongues cut out and right hands cut off. He died in exile in 662. His writings on Scripture, dogma and the ascetic life are a rich inheritance.
