# Blandina of Iași

Blondina Gobjilă, known to everyone as mother Blandina, was born on 24 February 1906 in the village of Grușenți-Chelmești in Bessarabia, into a priest's family; her father was Zaharia Popovici and her mother Serafima, and she was the youngest of their four children. Her name came from the martyr Blandina who lived in the second century near what is now Lyon and was killed in 177; and the meaning of it, from the Latin blandus — gentle, comforting, pleasing — described, doxologia says, both her character and the way she dealt with people through everything that came.

She was well brought up in both senses, and the priest's household marked her deeply. From the age of six she went daily to the services her father served, read at the chanter's stand, knew the order of the services and could take the chanter's place when needed, and knew the Liturgy by heart. She went through school and became a teacher of the first seven grades. On 27 August 1926 she married Gheorghe Gobjilă, an agricultural engineer and himself a priest's son, and they had one son, Vladislav.

Her life, the page says, falls into three stretches of fifteen years. The first, from 1926, was quiet and happy. It ended at the beginning of 1941, when, Bessarabia having been annexed by the USSR, the regime turned on the intelligentsia and the propertied families: her husband was arrested on 5 May 1941 and she on 21 June, the chief charge being that she had meant to flee to Romania.

In the prison at Kazan she dreamed one night that the Saviour appeared at the little window of her cell, crowned with thorns and crucified, the blood running down from His head; she wanted to go and wipe the wounds and stop the blood, and He said to her, See how much I too suffer unjustly. Her husband was in the same prison, and they managed to speak through a break in the wall; he was being interrogated with brutal violence. She went before her own interrogators with the Gospel words that those brought before rulers should not be anxious what to say, crossed herself, and said within herself: Lord, into Thy hands I give my life.

From Kazan she was moved to Sviyazhsk, where a monastery building had been made into a prison; the conditions were of the worst and she fell ill, her whole body covered with sores, and she lost a great deal of weight, holding meanwhile that everything is in God's hand, who makes life out of death and death out of life. Because she proved conscientious and honest she was put in charge of the kitchen, and after two years she was moved to the camp at Orlovo Rozovo, where she was set to field work and then, when she could not keep the others' pace, made night cook by a camp chief she remembered as a man with a gentle face and a good heart.

Two hundred nuns from the monastery of Saint John of Kronstadt were brought to that camp, condemned for religious propaganda, and she sought them out at meals, because they spent the rest after eating in prayer and spiritual singing; with them she said the akathists. Word came in time that her husband had died of hunger on 27 May 1943, and she called him blessed for having escaped the torments and finished as a martyr, and resolved never to marry again, and did not. In the yard of a storehouse where she later worked there was a nursery, and with one of the nuns, when the supervisor was away, she baptised about thirty-five children under a year old, saying the Creed and the words of baptism and dipping each child three times.

She was released on 21 June 1949, after eight years, but was not allowed to go where she wished: released prisoners remained enemies of the people, and she was sent with others to work on a collective farm in Siberia. Only in 1956 did the second stretch of her life end. After the return she lived in extreme modesty at Iași, giving herself to the Church, at the services every day, cleaning the church, spending her life in prayer, and forgiving everyone, including those who had tormented her; she died in 1971, and the crowd at her burial showed how much she was loved. The canonisation of the sixteen Romanian women saints, hers among them, was proclaimed on 6 February 2026.
