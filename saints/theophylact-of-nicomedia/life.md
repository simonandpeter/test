# Theophylact of Nicomedia

In the days when the Church was troubled by the heresy of the icon-fighters, Theophylact came from the parts of the East to Constantinople and made a friend of the great luminary of the Church, Saint Tarasius, while he was still a layman and a senator in the imperial palaces, holding the office of secretary of affairs. After the death of the emperor Leo the icon-fighter (717–740), and while his son Constantine (741–775) reigned with his mother Irene, the patriarch Paul called the merciful left his throne of his own will, and Tarasius was raised to the patriarchate in his place.

Tarasius gathered the seventh ecumenical council (787) and anathematised the iconoclast heresy; and then Theophylact, together with Saint Michael of Synnada, left the world, took up the monastic life, and was sent by Tarasius to a monastery built at the mouth of the Euxine sea. There he laboured much in the monastic toils, grew in good works and won boldness towards God. Once, at harvest time, when there was great heat and men were failing of thirst for want of water, he prayed, and a brazen vessel that stood there poured out water enough for their need.

The Romanian account sets that wonder beside the water God brought out of the rock long ago for thirsting Israel in the desert, and beside the living water that sprang from a dry jawbone when Samson was near dying of thirst. Seeing a life so shining, the patriarch Tarasius judged these venerable fathers worthy of the high hierarchical order: Michael he sent to Synnada, and Theophylact he chose bishop in Nicomedia.

What diligence he showed there for the rational flock of Christ, and what care he took for the poor, the destitute and the widows, is told by the houses of God he built, by the hospitals and the guest-houses, and by the alms beyond counting that went out from him every day. He served the sick himself, and the blind, the lame and the helpless. Going about the dwellings of the poor and the streets of the city he carried a vessel of warm water, and wherever he found the wounded he washed and cleansed them with his own hand, never shrinking from it.

After Tarasius departed this life and the most wise Nicephorus received the throne of the Church of Constantinople, the storm of the war against the holy icons rose again, stirred up by the emperor Leo the Armenian. Nicephorus gathered chosen hierarchs — Emilian of Cyzicus, Euthymius of Sardis, Joseph of Thessalonica, Eudoxius of the Amorians, Michael of Synnada and this same Theophylact of Nicomedia — and went with them to the emperor. They taught much out of the divine Scriptures and counselled him not to trouble the Church of Christ with a heresy the seventh council had anathematised, but they could not persuade him.

Then, when the other fathers had fallen silent, Theophylact said to him that, making nothing of God's long-suffering and taking no care for his own salvation, he set himself against the old teachings of the holy fathers and troubled the Church; and that a bitter and unlooked-for ruin would come upon him, a peril like a whirlwind, out of which he would find nobody to deliver him. The emperor drove them all from him with dishonour and condemned them to banishment in separate places: Nicephorus to the island of Proconnesus, Michael of Synnada to Eudocias, and Theophylact to the city of Strobilos, which is by the sea in Kibyrrhaiotis.

There the confessor of Christ and great helper of the right faith spent the rest of his life in hardship and great affliction, and departed to the Lord; doxologia gives no year for it. Leo the Armenian perished too, by an evil and unlooked-for death, as the saint had foretold: on the day of Christ's Nativity he was cut down with swords by his own soldiers in the church, at the hour of Matins. When the iconoclast emperors were at an end and the empress Theodora with her son Michael took the sceptre, and Saint Methodius was raised to the patriarchate, the honoured body of Theophylact was brought to Nicomedia and laid in the church he had built.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and fills the life out considerably. saint.gr says he came from the East and lived in the years of Leo IV, came to Constantinople to go on with his studies, and quickly got a name for wisdom and the friendship of high officers of state and of the future patriarch Tarasius, at that time protoskrites. When Tarasius was made patriarch in 784, he sent Theophylact, together with the Michael who later became bishop of Synada, to a monastery on the Black Sea; and about the year 800 — the page's own «probably» — Theophylact was elected bishop of Nicomedia. There he was known for works of mercy: churches, the great hospital of the Unmercenaries Cosmas and Damian, homes for the old and for the poor, collections for destitute widows and orphans, and the sick nursed by his own hands. When Leo V the Armenian moved against the holy icons, the patriarch Nicephorus took Theophylact and five other hierarchs up to the palace to reprove him out of Scripture, and it was Theophylact who spoke last and plainest: I know that you despise the guilt and the long-suffering of God, but destruction will come upon you suddenly, and the ruin will be like a storm. The emperor condemned them all to exile, and Theophylact to Strobilos, where he stayed thirty years with patience and reposed in peace in 840; under the empress Theodora and the patriarch Methodius his body was brought back to Nicomedia in 846 and laid in the church he had built. Theodore the Studite, the page adds, calls him a pillar of truth, the foundation of Orthodoxy, the guardian of piety and the support of the Church.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović tells how he came to the habit and what he said to the emperor. When [Tarasius](/saints/tarasius-of-constantinople), the first of the imperial counsellors, was chosen patriarch of Constantinople while still a layman, several of his friends and admirers out of the lay estate took the monastic habit with him and from him, and Theophylact was one of them; and Tarasius sent him as bishop to Nicomedia. As bishop he was a good shepherd to a reasoning flock, and showed himself uncommonly full of mercy towards the wretched and the orphaned.

After Tarasius's death the patriarchal throne was taken by another, and a little after that the imperial throne by Leo the Armenian, who was an iconoclast and as such raised a whole storm in the Church of Christ. Although the iconoclast heresy had been anathematised at the Seventh Ecumenical Council, this emperor set it up again and meant to put it in the place of Orthodoxy. Theophylact opposed him to his face; and when the emperor would not give way, he told him that a bitter destruction would come upon him suddenly, and that he would find nobody to deliver him from it.

For those words he was removed from his see by the emperor's order and sent into banishment, where he passed thirty years bearing many straits and insults, and where at the last he gave up his soul to the Lord. The Prologue puts that at about the year 845, where the Greek calendar this folder was also written from puts it at 840; the thirty years are in both, the disagreement is in the year alone, and nothing here was changed on it.

The Russian calendar tells his life as a chain of patriarchs and gives him his companion. He lived at Constantinople in the eighth century, in the time of the iconoclast heresy. After the death of the iconoclast emperor Leo IV the Khazar his son Constantine VI came to the throne, and the patriarchs changed with him: the patriarch Paul, unable to govern his flock while iconoclasm was so widely spread, left the see of his own will in 784, and in his place was chosen [Tarasius of Constantinople](/saints/tarasius-of-constantinople), until then the first of the imperial counsellors. At the new patriarch's insistence the Seventh Ecumenical Council was called in 787 and condemned the heresy, and relatively peaceful times came for Orthodoxy, so that the monasteries began to fill with monks again.

Theophylact was Tarasius's devoted disciple, and with the patriarch's blessing he withdrew to a monastery on the shore of the Black Sea together with [Michael of Synada](/saints/michael-of-synada). The two zealous ascetics received from God the gift of wonderworking, and the Russian account gives one instance of it: in a great drought, when the reapers in the field were failing of thirst, an empty vessel began at their prayer to pour out so much water that it was enough for the whole day. After some years in the monastery both were consecrated bishops by Tarasius, Michael to Synada and Theophylact to Nicomedia.

At the head of the church of Nicomedia he built churches, hospitals and houses for strangers, gave alms without sparing, took charge of orphans, widows and the sick, and nursed the lepers himself, not shrinking from washing their sores. When the iconoclast Leo V the Armenian took the throne in 813 the heresy broke out again with new force. Tarasius's own successor in the patriarchate could not move him, and pleaded with the emperor along with his bishops, in vain, not to break the peace of the Church. Theophylact, who was present at that meeting between emperor and patriarch, rebuked the heretics and foretold Leo the Armenian a swift destruction; for the bold prophecy he was sent into exile to the fortress of Strobilos in Asia Minor. There he languished thirty years, to his death about the year 845 — five years later than the year the Greek calendar gives. After the restoration of the veneration of icons in 847, under the empress Theodora and her son Michael, his relics were carried back to Nicomedia.
