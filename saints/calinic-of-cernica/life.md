# Calinic of Cernica

Calinic was born on 7 October 1787 in Bucharest, near the church of Saint Bessarion, and was given the name Constantine at his baptism. His parents, Antonie and [Floarea](/saints/filofteia-of-pasarea), were very devout: the eldest of their children was a married priest in his youth and afterwards became a monk under the name Acachie, and their mother, when her children were grown, withdrew to the monastery of Pasărea and took the great and angelic habit as the schemanun Filoteia. Constantine, the youngest, had a careful religious upbringing and learned his letters at the schools that then went with the churches of Bucharest.

In 1807 he entered on the monastic struggle at the monastery of Cernica, under the obedience of the venerable abbot, the archimandrite Timotei. He was tonsured into the monastic habit on 12 November 1808 under the name Calinic, and ordained hierodeacon on 3 December of the same year. A chosen vessel of the Holy Spirit, the life says, he loved prayer, silence, honesty and almsgiving from his childhood; as a young monk at Cernica he fasted a great deal, kept his rule and his prayers faithfully, fought against sleep, and was never missing from the services.

On 14 September 1850, by common counsel, he was chosen bishop at Râmnicu-Vâlcea, and parted from his spiritual children at Cernica with much grief and many tears, because — as the life puts it — he could not refuse the wish of his beloved spiritual son Barbu Dimitrie Știrbei, prince of Wallachia, and submitted to the will of the general Assembly.

Such was the struggle and the life full of holiness, and such the wonderful end, of Calinic of Cernica the wonderworker, who passed to the heavenly dwellings, in the company of the God-bearing Fathers, on 11 April 1868. His canonisation was made on 20 October 1955 under Justinian, Patriarch of Romania, and his commemoration, the page says, is kept on 11 April.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same 11 April and tells the same life with the dates filled in. saint.gr calls him one of the greatest spiritual figures of the nineteenth century, born at Bucharest on 7 October 1787 of Antonios and Floaera — his mother became a nun in her old age and took the name [Philothea](/saints/filofteia-of-pasarea) — and Constantine in the world. He was still a schoolboy in Bucharest when his longing for God drew his steps to the monastery of Cernica; in March 1807 he decided to leave the world for good, on 12 November 1808 he was tonsured and took the name Calinic, and the month after that the Bulgarian bishop of Vratsa, a refugee at Bucharest from the Turks, ordained him deacon in the church of Saint Nicholas at Cernica.

In 1812 he was sent with his spiritual father to the monastery of Neamț to ask help for the repair of that church, which an earthquake had ruined, and took the chance to visit the other monasteries of Moldavia; in 1813 he was ordained presbyter in the church of Batiște by bishop Dionysius Lupu, the future metropolitan of the country, and in 1815 was made steward of the monastery; in 1817 he left for the Holy Mountain to be taught the monastic life of the Athonite fathers. When the abbot Dorotheos fell asleep on 14 December 1818 the community of Cernica elected the hieromonk Calinic abbot, and two years later he was given the office of archimandrite. His thirty-two years as abbot were a time of flowering: shrines, cells and workshops were built, those who had letters copied precious manuscripts and the works of the fathers, and the monks grew from three hundred in 1838 to three hundred and fifty in 1850.

saint.gr dwells on his almsgiving. He was so merciful, it says, that when he had nothing left to give he gave his own clothes, and wept as he begged his helpers to gather money so that he might have something to share among the poor and the suffering; he founded a school for the children of the district and took on the building and renewal of many churches and shrines. In 1850, after forty-three years in the monastery, he was called to the episcopate, refused, and at last gave way to the entreaties of the voivode Barbu Știrbei. He found his diocese in a bad state — ten years under locum tenentes, the see and the cathedral destroyed, the priests few and unlettered, the church school closed since the revolution of 1848 — and set to it at once, ordaining good and reverent clergy, refounding the church school of Craiova in 1851 and moving it to Râmnic in 1854, and founding schools for the training of chanters.

In 1854 he began building a new cathedral; between 1859 and 1864 he built at his own expense a new church at the skete of Frăsinei, where he introduced the rules of the monastic life of the Holy Mountain; and in 1860 he founded a printing house for church and school books, which he gave to the city of Râmnic on the condition that half its income go to the upkeep of the schools, of the poor pupils, and of Frăsinei. He was a true patriot, the page adds, taking part in the deliberations of the country's public assembly and playing a considerable part in the union of Moldavia and Wallachia; in the spring of 1857 he sent a circular to all the abbots and priests asking that services and prayers for the union of the Romanian people be held in every church. God blessed him with the gift of wonderworking, and many sick who called on his prayers were healed. Old and ill, he withdrew to Cernica in May 1867, leaving the diocese to the archimandrite Gregory; the government of the day refused his resignation from the throne out of respect for him, and so he remained to the end the father and spiritual guide of his flock. He fell asleep in peace in 1868 and was buried in the church of the Great-martyr George which he had himself built.
