# Isidore of Chios

In the first year of the reign of Decius, an imperial order went through every part of the Roman dominion that the soldiers should be enrolled, numbered and set in companies, and the ships of a commander named Numerian came to the island of Chios, where the enrolled men were gathered. Isidore was on the island then, a man of Alexandria by birth, strong of body and brave, and a Christian in faith. He spent his days in fasting and self-restraint, walked soberly in good works, and kept clear of the pleasures of the world and of the pagan uncleanness; and for his bravery he was enrolled and put into Numerian's company.

A second order followed, that all who honoured Christ should bow to the pagan gods, and that any found among the soldiers believing in Christ should be forced by every means to the idols and to the sacrifices Rome's old custom appointed. A soldier named Julius went to Numerian and told him that Isidore was a Christian. Brought before the judgement seat and asked his name, he gave it; asked whether he would not obey the emperor Decius and sacrifice, he asked in return who these gods were, that a Christian should sacrifice to them — deaf and blind idols, he said, with no feeling in them at all.

The commander called that a foul blasphemy and said the man deserved great torments. Isidore answered that he was worthy to suffer every kind of torment for his God and to die, so that his Master might count him with the martyrs who had died before him for His holy name; and that those who believe and hope in Christ never die, for the Lord had said that he who believes in Him shall live though he die. Kill my body, he said, and over my soul you have no power; torment me as you please, for I have the true and living God, Jesus Christ, who abides in me now and will be with me after my death.

Numerian tried first to bargain — sacrifice once, and then do as you like — and Isidore told him that a man who has once denied his Lord cannot be called faithful, and that whoever is unfaithful in little is unfaithful in much. Four soldiers were ordered to strip him and beat him without mercy with ox sinews. Those standing round told him to submit before he died under the blows, and he said that he submitted to the will and the commandment of his Lord, the heavenly and everlasting Emperor, whom he confessed and honoured and would not deny, lest he himself be denied on the day of judgement.

After the beating the commander went back to coaxing and to threats and got nothing but reproach for himself and for his god, and in his anger he said he would have the unclean tongue cut out. Cut out my tongue, Isidore answered, and you will still not take the confession of the name of Jesus Christ out of my mouth. They cut out the martyr's tongue, and he went on speaking clearly, glorifying Christ the true God. Terror took the commander; he fell to the ground and lost his own tongue and was left dumb, and when they lifted him up he could say nothing and made signs, and asked for paper, and wrote the sentence of beheading.

When it was read out the martyr cried with joy, thanking the Lord for not putting him far from His grace and mercy, and praising Him who had given him a tongue that would not stop, to glorify His greatness. He went to the place of execution rejoicing, like a spotless lamb, looking up with a bright face and glad eyes and thanking his Master for receiving him into His dwellings and His places of rest; and there he asked for time to pray, and bowed his head, and was beheaded for Christ, who had bowed His own head on the Cross for us.

His body was thrown out for the dogs and the beasts, and a friend of his named Ammonius, a Christian in secret, took it up and with other believing brethren dug a grave for it in secret and laid it in the earth like a treasure of great price; and Ammonius himself afterwards followed the same road, for sailing towards the Hellespont to Cyzicus he was crowned with martyrdom. A God-fearing woman named Myronia came from Ephesus, raised the martyr's honoured relics out of the ground, anointed them with costly spices, wrapped them in clean linen and laid them in a place of honour, and when the persecution was over she built a church over them in his name; and the sick took healing from his relics.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same civil day and adds his city and the two men who killed him. saint.gr has him a sailor of the imperial fleet in the years of the emperor Decius, out of Alexandria. One day, with the squadron anchored at Chios, the centurion Julius denounced him to the admiral Noumerios as a Christian, and Noumerios soon heard the same from Isidore himself when he called on him to confess; he had him beaten hard and thrown into prison. His father, hearing of it, set off for Chios in great distress that his son had abandoned the ancestral religion, and had no difficulty in seeing him. Isidore kissed him with reverence and affection, and told him he ought rather to rejoice, since his son had seen the light Jesus Christ gives; the father begged him warmly to return to idolatry, and then in anger cursed him and urged Noumerios to put him to death quickly. After various tortures he was beheaded, and the page reads the Lord's word in it — «a father shall deliver up his child to death».

His body was thrown into a ravine for the birds to eat, with a few soldiers watching lest Christians take it. A Christian woman named [Myrope](/saints/myrope) — whom the page sends to 2 December — came by night with two servants while the guards slept, took up the relic and buried it. When Noumerios learnt next day that the body had been carried off he supposed the soldiers had been bribed and imprisoned them, letting it be known that he would kill them if they did not say to whom they had given it; Myrope judged it unjust that they should die, presented herself and told him the truth, and was imprisoned in her turn. After her own martyrdom the Christians buried her reverently beside the grave where she had laid Isidore.
