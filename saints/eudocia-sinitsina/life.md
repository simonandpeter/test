# Eudocia (Sinitsina)

She was born in 1879 at Troparevo in the Mozhaysk district of the Moscow province, the daughter of the peasant Ephrem Sinitsyn, and in her youth entered as a novice the Prince Vladimir monastery near the village of Filimonki in the Podolsk district. That house had been founded in memory of the preservation of the emperor Alexander III and his family in the railway accident of 17 October 1888. A lady-in-waiting, the princess Vera Borisovna Svyatopolk-Chetvertinskaya, petitioned the governor-general of Moscow about it in January 1889, and to find out whether the thing could be done at all she invited twenty-five sisters to live monastically on her estate beside Filimonki; when it was clear that the estate's income would keep them she petitioned the Moscow consistory as well.

Her second petition is quoted in the life. On the estate she was setting in order for the community, she wrote, there was a well-appointed church and ground well suited to monastic life; the village with the church and the cells stood in an ancient park with a river along one side, and there was a good piece of woodland besides. Her sincere wish, she closed, was to make the memory of the Sovereign's miraculous deliverance lasting by setting up a women's monastic community where poor homeless women might settle, earn their bread by their own labour and offer up prayers to God; and she asked the consistory to consider her request as soon as it could. Leave was given on 3 October 1890, and under an abbess three nuns and a hundred novices came to live there. Eudocia stayed until the house was closed in 1929, and then settled in Filimonki itself with a sick distant kinswoman.

At the beginning of 1938 the duty witnesses gave the NKVD the evidence it needed: that she distributed church literature, instilled religious convictions and drew people into circles of churchmen, that her most active work was among the young and among children whom she drew in with various presents, so that many left the Komsomol, and that priests came to her from Moscow whose surnames were unknown and held secret conversations of an anti-Soviet character. The chairman of the village soviet added that she carried on anti-Soviet work neatly and underground, that the monastery's former priest came often to these nuns, and that what they talked about was clearly not to the advantage of the Soviet power; there were others there too, he wrote, who went often to Moscow as agents of fascism.

She was arrested on 25 February 1938, shut in the prison at Serpukhov and questioned. Told that the investigation knew she had said after the closing of the monastery that the Soviet power would not last and all the churches and monasteries would work again, she answered that she had often had to speak about the monastery with various people, and had said only that some of the nuns had lived well there before and were badly off now, and that she had said nothing against the power. Told that she carried on anti-Soviet agitation among adolescents and urged them to join religious circles, she answered that she met the children of workers and farmers every summer, and only because flowers grew beside her house, which she often shared out among them, but that she had not instilled religion into them. Told of a conversation of October 1937, she said that a priest from Peredeltsy had indeed been in her house that month to communicate a sick woman who lived there, and that they had talked about the Church and about religion but not against the Soviet power. On 8 March 1938 an NKVD troika sentenced her to be shot; shortly before the execution she was taken to the Taganka prison in Moscow. She was shot on 20 March 1938 and buried in an unmarked common grave at the Butovo range outside Moscow, which civil day is the Julian 7 March the calendar keeps her on.
