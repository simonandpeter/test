# Patrick of Ireland

Patrick is the best-loved and the best-known saint of Ireland. Doxologia's life for the day says he was born about the year 381 in a village called Bannaven Taberniae, into a family of servants of the altar: his grandfather Potitus was a priest and his father Calpurnius a deacon. The name Patrick, or Patrichie, meant in Latin a man of high birth; and by his own account he had forgotten God by the age of sixteen.

In that year pirates plundered the village where he was living and he was sold as a slave in Ireland, where he kept sheep on Mount Slemish in County Antrim. Going into captivity changed his life altogether: from a rich and carefree young man he became one who said at least a hundred prayers by night and as many by day. He had two visions, the first showing him that he would return home and the second telling him that his ship was ready; and after six years of slavery he came back to his parents.

Later he met Germanus, bishop of Auxerre, and had another dream in which he was asked to go to Ireland. He asked the bishop's counsel about it; and Germanus, who had been praying God to give him someone to take the place of Palladius — Palladius having left Ireland less than a year after his ordination, because of the cruelty of the Irish — saw in that dream God's answer. Patrick was ordained bishop, and in the year 432 he set out from the monastery at Auxerre for Ireland with a few companions.

Surrounded by pagans, he met with many trials. The druids were displeased at his presence and sent Dichu to kill him, and Dichu was held fast at the moment he raised his sword; Patrick loosed him from the unseen power that held him, and in return Dichu gave him a shelter in which to celebrate the Liturgy. After that wonder many of the Irish received the faith in Christ, and later, with his disciples, he baptised the whole country.

He departed this life on 17 March, about the year 480. Iconography shows him holding a shamrock in his hand, the sign of the Holy Trinity, one God in essence and three in persons; and his day, the page adds, is kept not only in Ireland but in many countries of the world.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and names his household. saint.gr has him born about the year 380 at Glevum, near the mouths of the Severn and the Avon, of a Brittano-Roman family: his father Calpurnius was a deacon and a decurion, his grandfather Potitus a priest, his mother Concessa. At sixteen he was taken captive to Ireland and stayed six years, and the hours of solitude he spent as a slave herding the flock went by in prayer to the God he had discovered in his trial. The Lord himself spoke to him in his sleep and told him to go home by a ship that was ready for him — the first of a series of appearances which, the page says, his own writings dwell on.

He escaped in 402, but a storm drove the ship to the north-western coast of Gaul, to Armorica, where the crew could find no food; at the captain's asking he prayed, preached to them of God's almightiness and love for man, called them to turn and repent, and promised food that same day, and so it fell out. After wanderings in the islands of the Tyrrhenian sea he came back to Britain, and a vision called him again to Ireland. He went first to Auxerre in Gaul and stayed years preparing for the priesthood; he is said to have known St Martin and St Germanus, and it was Germanus who sent him to Ireland as bishop in 432.

saint.gr credits his preaching with great effect: thousands baptised, many priests ordained, churches and a bishopric raised, and the monastic life encouraged where until then it had been Celtic in character. On his missionary journeys he would not travel from Saturday evening to Monday morning, keeping the time for the Lord and for the Sunday Liturgy. The page names the writings that survive — his Confession, which is his autobiography, a letter condemning slavery, and the breastplate hymn — records the tradition that he drove all the demons and all the snakes out of Ireland, and says he taught the mystery of the one consubstantial Trinity by the shamrock, a local plant whose leaf is of three parts and yet one.
