# Justin the Philosopher

Justin was born in the parts of Syria Palestina, on the borders of Samaria, in the city first called Shechem and afterwards Flavia Neapolis. His father was of good and honoured stock but Greek in religion, and Justin himself was in the same error of idol-worship before he was enlightened. From his youth he kept at his books and advanced in Greek learning, having a quick mind, and when he had learned rhetoric well he wished also to learn philosophy, wanting above all to know what God is.

He went first to a philosopher of the Stoics and found nothing about God there, for that man neither knew God nor thought the question worth teaching. He left him for a Peripatetic who seemed wise, and who soon began to bargain over his fee; seeing him a lover of silver, Justin cast him off as greedy and judged him unworthy of the name of philosophy. Then he came to a teacher of the school of Pythagoras, who required him first to learn astronomy, geography, arithmetic and music; and Justin, seeing that these would take many years and feed nothing in his soul, left him too.

He attached himself last to one of the school of Plato, whose teaching then had great fame and honour, and who promised to lead him from the likeness of bodily things to the bodiless, and from the images of things below to the things above, and so to the knowledge of God. Justin agreed, hoping to reach the divine wisdom he longed for, stayed a long while with that teacher, quickly learned Plato's doctrines and became a finished and famous philosopher among the Greeks. Even so he could not yet come to the faith of the Christians or to the true knowledge of God.

Once, walking alone outside the city in a place apart near the sea, turning over his philosophy, he saw an unknown and honourable old man, adorned with grey hairs, who asked him why he looked so closely at him. They fell to talking: what good philosophy brings, whether it brings a man happiness, what God is. That is God, Justin said, who never changes but is always the same, and who is the cause of the making of all things; and the elder, pleased with the answer, pressed him further, asking how the Greek philosophers could speak truly of God, whom they had never seen or heard.

Not with bodily eyes is the power of the Godhead seen, Justin answered, as men see some beast, but with the mind alone, as Plato teaches, whose doctrine I follow; and there is in us a power Plato calls the eye of the mind, given to man for this, that being cleansed and enlightened by the love of wisdom it may see the divine Truth itself, a Being above all beings, unattainable, unspeakable, alone good and beautiful. The life is long, and it goes on from this conversation through his coming to the faith, his writings and the Apology he addressed to the emperor, which it treats at length.

When the emperor's rescript was granted he went into Asia, where the Christians were then most persecuted, and at Ephesus, in the philosopher's cloak he never laid aside to the end, he published the command and sent it round the cities, and the persecution ceased for a time. There he disputed with the learned rabbi Trypho the Jew and overcame him out of the Scriptures of the Old Law, which is written out at length in Justin's own book, as the Apology is.

After a long while he returned from Ephesus into Italy, preaching Christ apostolically everywhere on the road, overcoming Jews and Greeks in argument and turning them to the faith. At Rome, Crescens the Cynic, a pagan philosopher, set himself against him with great hatred; Justin talked with him often and always beat him and shamed him before everyone. Unable to stand against him, Crescens slandered him with many lies before the Roman court.

They took him as a man guilty of wickedness and tormented him in bonds, and when he was brought to judgement they found no cause in him; and Crescens, afraid he would be let go, secretly prepared a deadly poison and with it killed by deceit the unconquered soldier of Christ. So Justin ended, the true philosopher of the Christians, leaving the Church many writings. The life names no year, and none is recorded here.
