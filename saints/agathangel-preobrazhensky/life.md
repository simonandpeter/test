# Agathangel (Preobrazhensky)

Alexander Lavrentievich Preobrazhensky was born on 27 September 1854 in the village of Mogily in the Tula province, the son of a priest named Lavrenty, one of six children in a household his life describes by what it chose: plain peasant work for the children, a modest village church served conscientiously, poverty accepted without complaint. He remembered his own boyhood in the churchyard among the graves and crosses — those silent but eloquent signs that all is dust, all is ash, all is shadow — begging God with tears to let him one day stand at the altar and offer the bloodless sacrifice for those who had finished their earthly journey.

The Tula seminary in 1871 gave him his first serious trial, because its way of teaching did not match the piety he had been raised in, and for a while he thought of becoming a doctor; it was constant talk with his father that carried him through to the Moscow Theological Academy in 1877. He finished in 1881 with a candidate's degree in theology for a study of the Hexaemeron of the Bulgarian Exarch worked out from a manuscript of 1263, and went to teach Latin in a church school in the Ryazan diocese.

In 1882 he married Anna, the daughter of Archpriest Voznesensky, and within a year he had lost his wife and their newborn child almost at once. «Жизненный путь, избранный мною — не мой жребий» — the way of life I chose was not my portion — so he hurried, he said, to leave the world, take up his cross and join the monastic rank. He was tonsured on 7 March 1885 with the name of [Agathangelus](/saints/agathangelus), the fourth-century martyr who was the disciple of Clement of Ancyra.

Then twenty years in Siberia and the west: inspector at the Tomsk seminary as igumen in 1886, rector at Irkutsk as archimandrite in 1888, consecrated bishop of Kirensk in the Ascension monastery at Irkutsk on 10 September 1889, Tobolsk and Siberia from 1893, Riga and Mitava from 1897, archbishop in 1904, Vilna and Lithuania from 1910. Everywhere he reorganised the schools to one end — that the young should first of all come to love prayer — and because he had grown up in a village parsonage he knew what village priests needed: mutual-aid funds, orphanages for clergy children, a diocesan candle works enlarged. For the council expected in 1905 the Synod asked him to report on the liturgical life of the Russian Church, and he wrote that the grammatical and stylistic errors which had crept into the service books since the seventeenth century should be corrected and parish worship brought back into line with the ancient typika.

He came to Yaroslavl and Rostov in 1913 and was soon made metropolitan. The war of 1914 turned him to hospitals and to sending priests to the army; he sat in the Local Council of 1917–18 and from 1918 in the Higher Church Administration and the Synod under Patriarch [Tikhon](/saints/tikhon-of-moscow). On 12 May 1922, from prison, Tikhon flatly refused the Renovationists' demand that he hand them the supreme church administration and gave Agathangel a letter putting him at its head until a council could meet.

What followed cost him the rest of his life. On 3 June 1922 the «Memorandum of the Three» declared the Renovationist administration the only canonically lawful church authority; on 5 June Agathangel issued his own epistle taking up the temporary government of the Russian Church and telling the faithful to beware of those trying to usurp it. He was arrested at once. At the end of 1922 the sick old man was sent, in a common convoy with criminals, to three years' exile at the settlement of Kolpashevo in the Narym country.

Tikhon's testament of 25 December 1925 named him second candidate for locum tenens of the patriarchal throne. By the time his exile ended the other two had been arrested, and Tuchkov of the secret department of the OGPU came to see him in the Perm prison in April 1926 to offer him the supreme authority — the point of the offer, his life says, being to set the leading hierarchs against one another with intrigue and false information and so leave the Church without a lawful hierarchy. He issued an epistle announcing that he had taken up the office. Metropolitan Sergius, who had agreed to vacate it, then would not give it up.

The correspondence that followed, full of canonical casuistry, only convinced him that Sergius was not the man to hold the episcopate together against the godless power. He wrote that he could not take the office himself «ввиду преклонности лет и расстроенного здоровья», for advanced years and broken health, and advised Metropolitan Peter to hand it to Cyril or to Arsenius instead, not wanting it left with Sergius. After Sergius's Declaration of 1927 he came out, on 24 January 1928 old style, with a confessor's address refusing administrative subordination to him and announcing self-government under Patriarch Tikhon's decree of 20 November 1920. He was threatened with canonical penalties in reply.

His heart began to fail, and whenever an attack came he communicated of the Holy Mysteries first and took medical help afterwards. On 10 May 1928 he called his vicar bishops and drew up a last address saying that they did not break off prayerful communion and sent all who came to them to Sergius for the peace of the Church — while adding that instructions which troubled their conscience and, as they believed, broke the canons they had not been able and were not able to carry out. His life's judgement on him is that the childlike simplicity which made him a confessor also made him incapable of the calculating diplomacy needed to keep administrative power.

He took to his bed in the middle of September and died on 3 October 1928, the 16th by the new calendar. Twelve strokes from thirty churches of Yaroslavl announced it. He was buried only on the seventh day, in a crypt under the Leontievsky church in the Leontievsky cemetery, and his face was as it had been on the first day, light and white and at peace, with a fragrance coming from the coffin. He was numbered among the New Martyrs and Confessors of Russia at the Jubilee Council of Bishops.
