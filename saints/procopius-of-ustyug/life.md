# Procopius of Ustyug

saint.gr's calendar for 8 Ιουλίου says plainly that it has no details of the life of the venerable Procopius, the fool for Christ of Ustyug, apart from one wonder, and then tells it. Once, it says, the venerable one came running through the streets of Ustyug to the church; he knelt before the icon of the Mother of God and cried out, Mother of God, save us, save us. Then he turned to the people who were in the church at that moment and shouted to them to repent and to stop sinning, or else God would stone them. They took him for a madman, beat him and drove him out.

Some time later the sky over the city was covered by an enormous black cloud. All the churches filled with the faithful, and Procopius began to reproach the people and urged them to pray; and they all prayed, and the cloud broke up and the sky cleared. Then all the inhabitants, as though something were pushing them, ran out of the city, and there they saw a vast stretch of ground covered with black stones — meteorites. Had those stones fallen on the city, the page says, not a man would have been left alive.

The stones are there to this day, and the scientists who examined them confirmed that they are indeed meteorites; but how they all came to be in one place they could not explain, and that, the page says, only the servant of God knew, who had foreseen it. He fell asleep in 1303, and his canonisation by the Russian Church took place in 1818. The page gives nothing else of his life and prints no hymn and no synaxarion couplet under his line.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 8 July, the same menologion day the Greek calendar keeps him on, and its first words are the wonderworker of Ustyug and the year 1303 — the year the Greek page gives too, so nothing in the folder’s date changes. What it adds is the life the Greek page said it did not have. He was a Varangian by origin and a merchant by trade; and coming to Novgorod on business he marvelled at the beauty of Orthodoxy and received the Orthodox faith. Wishing to be a perfect Christian he gave away all his property to the poor and set himself to practise every other virtue.

He feigned madness, this book says, so that people should not praise him; but he saw into the hearts and the fates of men, and into natural events which afterwards really came to pass. By his weeping prayer before the icon of the Mother of God he turned a terrible hail away from the town of Ustyug, and so brought the sinful town to repentance. His body was found dead in the street and covered over with snow, and a church was built above the wonderworking relics.

The two books tell that one wonder differently and this life keeps both. The Greek page has an enormous black cloud over the city, the churches filling with the faithful, the saint reproaching the people and urging them to pray, the cloud breaking up, and then a vast stretch of ground outside the city covered with black meteorite stones that would have left nobody alive. This book has hail rather than stones, a weeping prayer before an icon rather than a reproach to the people, and the town’s repentance as the fruit of the deliverance rather than its condition. Neither reading is chosen here.
