# Alexius (Orlov)

Alexius (Orlov) was born on 8 February 1862 at Samara, into the poor and
deeply believing family of a psalmist — a Samara cleric, the short life
says; he passed through the local church schools and finished the St
Petersburg Academy as a candidate of theology, and on 16 August 1895 was
ordained priest, serving in various churches of Samara and then, as
archpriest, at the cathedral. Widowed in 1922, he took the monastic habit
the same year, and on 9 June 1923 was consecrated bishop of Bugulma, vicar
of Samara; at the end of that year he fell into Renovationism, was made
its bishop of Buguruslan with the temporary government of the Samara
diocese, and in 1924, in the Samara cathedral, publicly repented of the
lapse and was received back into the communion of the Russian Church in
his existing rank. The same year he was made bishop of Kurgan, vicar of
Tobolsk; in 1927 bishop of Malmyzh, vicar of Sarapul; and in February
1931 bishop of Enotaevsk, vicar of Astrakhan — where in fact, by the
memoir of A. I. Kuznetsov, he had arrived in September 1930, after the
arrest of Bishop Andrew (Komarov), and carried the whole burden of the
diocese while Andrew was held. He was a modest, engaging man, loved by all
the faithful for it: simple in manner, ascetic in life, serving every day,
chiefly at the church of Prince Vladimir near which he lodged, though the
faithful liked to invite him to other parishes for their patronal feasts;
tall, with a full beard down to his waist, a fair, ruddy face, kind eyes
and a sweet, expressive smile. On the eve of the Meeting of the Lord he
had been asked to serve the vigil at the Tikhvin church and had begun,
when Bishop Andrew, released from prison that morning and met by no one,
went into the sanctuary — and it was Andrew who came out for the Liturgy;
an uproar rose, voices asked where Bishop Alexius was, the people pressed
Andrew to withdraw and let Alexius go on, he could not make himself heard,
and the service was wrecked, which did nothing for relations between the
two bishops. Alexius stayed at Astrakhan as vicar, seldom able to serve
and then only at Prince Vladimir’s, and in April 1931 was removed from
the city; only in 1933 did the Astrakhan people learn from his own letter
why: he had bought a ream of writing-paper each sheet of which carried the
fat printed slogan “religion is a tool for the oppression of the working
masses”, had deftly altered it to “religion is a tool for the consolation
of the working masses”, and had let it go out into the world so amended in
his business letters — someone found it a liberty, and he found himself at
Syzran. Appointed bishop of Syzran, vicar of Samara, on 5 June 1931, he was
almost at once sent on to Siberia, to Omsk, whose bishop he was made on
24 August 1931, and archbishop on 11 August 1933. On 24 April 1935 he was
arrested on a memorandum of the Omsk NKVD which said that the archbishop,
seventy-three and hostile to the existing order, had gathered round
himself like-minded men from the Tikhonite clergy and fugitives — a priest
and a deacon of the Elijah church, a former missionary, a former kulak —
who for several years had systematically carried on anti-Soviet agitation
against the policy of the party and the Soviet power, spreading
provocative rumours of a coming war and the inevitable fall of the Soviet
power, and that on the closing of the Znamensky church he had called an
illegal gathering of supporters at his flat under cover of his name-day
and made an agitational speech there, calling on them to pray that the
Church and the country be delivered from troubles and misfortunes. Within
a month or so a former priest and two Omsk priests were added to the
charge sheet, and so a group of “church wreckers” was formed in the depths
of the NKVD. At the interrogations, which went on until 10 July 1935, he
bore himself with dignity, an honest and fearless man who trusted his
closest circle completely; the record shows that he had explained to his
flock the ruin of schism and Renovationism, had helped exiled and
repressed clergy with money, had helped priests who had fled from exile,
had cared for the material needs of his flock, and had reacted sharply to
the blowing-up of the Elijah church in Omsk. The indictment of 10 July
charged him with leading a counter-revolutionary defeatist group of
Tikhonite clergy, spreading rumours of a coming war between Japan and the
USSR and the inevitable fall of Soviet power, working on the faithful in
Japan’s favour as the defender of Christianity, saying that in a war the
Red Army would rise, and saying, after Kirov’s murder, that many innocent
people had suffered. On 25 October 1935 the special board of the NKVD
sentenced him under articles 58-10 and 58-11 to five years’ exile in
Kazakhstan, and on 23 December 1936 he reached Mirzoyan, the present
Taraz, a tiny district centre near Chimkent. There he helped organise a
community of the “old church” — the patriarchal Church — drew up its
lists, wrote its application to the district executive committee and
advised its illiterate members how to go and register it, and despite the
authorities’ bans performed the rites the faithful asked for. On 15 May
1937 he was arrested by the Mirzoyan NKVD for taking part in a
“counter-revolutionary organisation of churchmen” and put in the Mirzoyan
prison, in the same case as the metropolitans Cyril (Smirnov) and Joseph
(Petrovykh), shot at Chimkent, and other clergy and laity: the indictment
of 23 August 1937 alleged that these exiled churchmen had organised a
counter-revolutionary centre in southern Kazakhstan, with large cadres of
clergy, monks, priests, kulaks and officers, to gather all the
counter-revolutionary forces of the churchmen into a strictly conspiratorial
underground for an armed rising at the moment of the expected foreign
intervention, to restore the patriarchate and set the head of the Church
over the state, with Cyril Smirnov to be counted head of the Russian
Church; that workers’ communities and secret monasteries had been set up
in various places, down to the factories, where bishops, priests and monks
recruited new members and gave the tonsure; and that the centre received
money regularly, through cover addresses, from its branches’ secret
collections among the faithful. His own count was that he had organised
new illegal communities at Mankent and Mirzoyan, had been organiser of
cells and go-between for Archbishop Boris (Shipulin) of Tashkent and
Joseph (Petrovykh), had agitated about the imminent fall of Madrid and had
voiced defeatist sentiments. He did not deny that as a cleric he had
helped to form the old-church community, had drawn up its lists, written
its application and advised the illiterate how to register it, nor that he
had been in touch with Archbishop Boris, who governed the Tashkent
diocese, with whom he was registered as a minister of religion, since by
the old-church rules every minister must register wherever he is; but to
every question about anti-Soviet and counter-revolutionary activity he
answered that he had engaged in none, and when the charge was read to him
said, I admit no guilt in anything. The record of the last interrogation
took two lines: you are a member of the counter-revolutionary
organisation of churchmen and must confess, and tell frankly and in
detail of your counter-revolutionary activity — he refuses frank
testimony, and declares that he does not know. On 26 August 1937 the
troika of the South Kazakhstan NKVD sentenced him to be shot for
anti-Soviet activity — the short life gives the 27th — and on 4 September
1937 he was shot near Chimkent, at Lisya Balka; the place of his burial is
unknown. He was rehabilitated for the case of 1937 by the presidium of the
South Kazakhstan regional court on 5 July 1958, and for that of 1935 by the
Omsk regional procuracy on 22 June 1989, and was glorified among the new
martyrs and confessors of Russia by the Council of Bishops of 13–16 August
2000; the Russian calendar keeps him on 22 August (Julian) and in the
synaxes of the saints of Omsk, Samara and Vyatka.
