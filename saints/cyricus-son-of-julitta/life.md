# Cyricus, son of Julitta

In the city of Iconium in Lycaonia there was a young woman of good family, [Julitta](/saints/julitta-mother-of-cyricus), who came of the line of the older emperors of Rome and was a Christian in her faith. She lived a short while with a lawful husband, bore him a son, and was left a widow; and the child she had borne she enlightened with holy baptism and named Cyricus.

In those days Diocletian the pagan held the empire of Rome and raised a great persecution against the Christians in the lands of his power, and he set over Lycaonia a governor named Domitian, a man wild by nature and without humanity, who rejoiced in the shedding of Christian blood. Seeing that her faith could not be hidden from such a man, and fearing that under torture she might deny Christ, Julitta left her goods, her house, her kindred, her servants and all the beauty of this world, took her son, who was three years old, and two of her more faithful maidservants, and went out of Iconium by night.

She went to Seleucia as a stranger, hiding her good birth, and found the same persecution there under a governor named Alexander; so she left and went on to Tarsus in Cilicia and lived among the poor. In time Alexander came to Tarsus too, and Julitta, being recognised, was denounced to him and seized with her child, while the two maidservants fled and followed at a distance to see her end.

Brought before the governor with Cyricus in her arms and asked her name, her family and her country, she answered boldly that Christian was her name and her good family, and her country the heavenly kingdom of Christ. The governor in anger had the child taken from her and had her stripped and stretched on the ground and beaten without sparing, and the child watched her, weeping, and tore himself out of the hands that held him to get back to his mother.

Seeing the boy was beautiful, the governor had him brought and set him on his knees and stroked his hair and kissed him and spoke gently to him; but the child pulled away and turned his head aside and would not be kissed, and looking at his mother under the blows he cried out, «Sunt creștin! Lasă-mă să mă duc la mama mea!» — I am a Christian, let me go to my mother — and scratched the governor's face with his nails and pushed away from him. The governor, filled with rage, threw him down and struck him with his foot in the ribs, and the child fell on the stone steps and was broken on their sharp corners, and gave up his soul to God, crowned with martyrdom.

Julitta under the beating suffered as though in another's body, and cried nothing but that she was a Christian and would not sacrifice to their idols; and when they raised her from the ground and she saw her son lying dead in his blood before the tribunal she was filled with joy and thanked God that he had been granted to finish before her. She was hung up and her body raked with iron combs and her wounds sprinkled with molten pitch, and at the last, her endurance unbroken, the governor condemned her to the sword.

She went to the place of execution rejoicing as to a wedding, asked a little time for prayer, and knelt and gave thanks that her son had been called before her. The executioner sharpened his sword and struck her neck, and both bodies were left unburied outside the city for the dogs and the beasts. In the night the two maidservants came and carried them far off and buried them in the earth; and one of those women lived until the days of Constantine, the first Christian emperor, and then showed the Christians the place. The relics were taken up whole and fragrant and gave healings, and the account of the passion was written down.
