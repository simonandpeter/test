# Syra of Persia

Syra lived in Persia in the sixth century, the daughter of a famous
fire-worshipping priest of Karkh-Seleucia in Elymais — a kinswoman, the
Prologue says, of Mary Golindukha. Her father, fearing the influence of
Christianity on his daughter, sent her after her mother’s death to the city
of Fars to be brought up by a priestess, who taught her the priestly
science; she became a priestess of the fire-temple with an honourable
career before her. But once, talking with poor Christian women, Syra
believed in Christ the Saviour and began to live as a Christian, learning
prayers and psalms, fasting and reading Christian books. Falling ill she
did not look for a cure in magic but came to the Christian church and asked
the presbyter for a little dust from the church, hoping for healing; the
presbyter, knowing her as an idol-worshipper, refused, and she, not angry,
knowing her unworthiness, touched the priest’s garment with faith as the
woman with the issue of blood once touched the Saviour’s, and was healed at
once. Her family guessed that she meant to become a Christian and set her
stepmother to dissuade her; pretending to be a secret Christian herself the
stepmother coaxed her to keep her faith hidden and serve the fire outwardly,
and Syra wavered over baptism until a dream showed her the joyless lot of
her kin after death and the bright mansions prepared for Christians, and she
went to the bishop asking to be baptised. The bishop put her off, afraid of
giving the magi a pretext to persecute the Christians and thinking that she
might fall away from Christ for fear of her father’s anger, and advised her
first to confess her faith in the Saviour openly before her family. So one
day, during the morning sacrifice, she scattered the sacred fire the
Persians honoured as a god, overturned the altar and cried aloud: I am a
Christian; I renounce the false gods and believe in the true God! Her father
beat her until he was exhausted and threw her into prison, then with tears
and endearments begged her to keep the old faith, and when she would not
bend denounced her to the chief magus, then to the governor, and to King
Chosroes the Elder. She was long tortured in prison, and the Lord
strengthened her and she stood firm for the faith of Christ; once, bribing
the gaolers, she went out to the bishop and was baptised, and the Lord gave
her the gift of wonders. When the Persians put her under the watch of Jews
they mocked her — where is the fable they tell of you, that the bonds fall
off your neck and hands and feet of themselves? let us see them fall now —
and at those words she prayed to the Saviour in the depth of her heart and
the bonds fell from her at once, and not only once. Worn out by the
tortures she fell mortally ill, and prayed the Lord not to let her die of
sickness but to grant her the martyr’s crown, and God heard her and healed
her; seeing her well, the gaoler and the governor of the prison wanted to
dishonour her, and the Lord struck the one with sickness and the other with
death. She was sentenced to be strangled, and the execution was done with
refined cruelty — from time to time they slackened the rope and asked
whether she would change her mind and live, and barely alive she refused
and asked them to finish. Her body was thrown to the dogs, which did not
touch it, and the Christians buried it, in 558, under Chosroes I, in her
eighteenth year; the Prologue’s verses call her young as dew but old in
wisdom, rich in faith, hope and meekness, shining in the dark prison with a
strange light.
