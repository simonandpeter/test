# Syra of Persia

Syra lived in Persia in the sixth century, the daughter of a famous fire-worshipping priest of Karkh-Seleucia in Elymais — a kinswoman, the Prologue says, of Mary Golindukha. Her father, fearing the influence of Christianity on his daughter, sent her after her mother’s death to the city of Fars to be brought up by a priestess, who taught her the priestly science; she became a priestess of the fire-temple with an honourable career before her. But once, talking with poor Christian women, Syra believed in Christ the Saviour and began to live as a Christian, learning prayers and psalms, fasting and reading Christian books. Falling ill she did not look for a cure in magic but came to the Christian church and asked the presbyter for a little dust from the church, hoping for healing; the presbyter, knowing her as an idol-worshipper, refused, and she, not angry, knowing her unworthiness, touched the priest’s garment with faith as the woman with the issue of blood once touched the Saviour’s, and was healed at once. Her family guessed that she meant to become a Christian and set her stepmother to dissuade her; pretending to be a secret Christian herself the stepmother coaxed her to keep her faith hidden and serve the fire outwardly, and Syra wavered over baptism until a dream showed her the joyless lot of her kin after death and the bright mansions prepared for Christians, and she went to the bishop asking to be baptised — the Sretensky calendar’s life runs on past the point read here. Her heartless father gave her to cruel tortures, in which heavenly visions heartened and comforted her, and she was strangled with a rope and drowned in 558, under Chosroes I, in her eighteenth year; the Prologue’s verses call her young as dew but old in wisdom, rich in faith, hope and meekness, shining in the dark prison with a strange light.
