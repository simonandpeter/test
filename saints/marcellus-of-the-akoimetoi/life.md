# Marcellus of the Akoimetoi

Marcellus was of the city of Apamea in Syria, of rich and well-born parents. Left an orphan from his early childhood, he did not turn aside to unseemly indulgence, nor spend his father's wealth on the pleasures of the body, as young men do, but went first to Antioch the Great for the learning of philosophy; and finding an able and learned teacher he quickly mastered the whole of it. Then he desired the spiritual love of wisdom as well, and wished to go to Ephesus, hearing that there was there a multitude of men perfect in good works.

Leaving Antioch, he first divided among the poor the whole inheritance he had from his parents in Apamea, and then, overlooking all worldly things, came to Ephesus. There, living beside a lover of God, he was schooled in the virtues by the servant of God Promot, who was in such perfection that he went into the churches of the Lord through locked doors for the night prayer.

From him and from the other holy men who were there the blessed Marcellus gathered much profit, weaving himself a crown as out of many kinds of flowers, and learning every kind of virtue, since he had before him there many patterns of the life that pleases God. Living with them in voluntary poverty, he earned what his body needed by the labour of his own hands, for he knew how to write beautifully: he spent the whole night in prayer and the day in the copying of books, and of the price he took for them he spent on his own needs only what he had to and divided the rest among the poor. Such were the beginnings of his life of good works.

Hearing of abba Alexander in Byzantium, that he was most wise in word and great in deed and sufficient for the salvation of many souls, Marcellus left Ephesus and hastened to go to him. At that time the blessed Alexander was living with his brethren beside the church of Saint Menas; and afterwards, by the edge of the Black Sea, he built a most honoured monastery, in which he set up a new rule of life.

doxologia.ro's life for him is long, and its middle — some thirty thousand characters, on his years in that monastery and his abbacy of the Unsleeping — was not read. What the page prints at the end is his repose: he told his disciple Luchian that after his own departure Luchian would soon come after him, and the abbots and brethren of the monasteries round about, with bishops and nobles from Constantinople, gathered to visit him and give him the last kiss.

Giving each of them the teaching that was fitting, and speaking much for the profit of the soul and about the eternal life, he asked them to withdraw from him, saying that he wished to sleep a little; and when they had all gone away he fell asleep with the blessed sleep and the eternal rest, giving his soul into the hands of God, and they laid him with honour in the church he had himself built.

The blessed Luchian wept over his grave, and on the fifth day the venerable one appeared to him in a vision, saying: «Why do you grieve? Do you not believe that I have prayed God for you, that you too should be with me without delay?» Three days after that vision Luchian also rested in the Lord, having lived eight days after his father and teacher. So Marcellus fulfilled his own prophecy even after his death, taking his beloved disciple quickly after him as he had promised. The day's line gives no year for his birth or his death, and the troparion the site prints for him, in glasul 8, is the common of a venerable father with his own name set in it.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 29 December, and puts him in a century. saint.gr says Marcellus lived in the fifth century, in the patriarchate of Gennadius I, which it dates 458 to 471, and under the emperor Leo I called Makelles. He succeeded in his life, it says, because by the grace of God he understood that worldly splendours appear and vanish like flowers, and he was persuaded that the only true and gainful life is the one given to the service of the good, upon the road of Jesus Christ.

He was of Apamea, a city of Syria, and his family was well enough off; and because his parents loved learning they adorned their son with much education. But the young man's heart had in it a lively and unsleeping flame of piety, and worldly offices did not interest him. In that mind he went to Ephesus, entered a monastery and became a monk; and from there he went to Constantinople, to the Monastery of the Unsleeping, where the abbot was [Alexander](/saints/alexander-the-sleepless).

There he was quickly distinguished for his virtues and much loved by the brethren of the house for the humility he kept, although he was a man of study and of great intellectual worth. When the abbot Alexander had died, and after him his successor Jacob, the love and the esteem of the brethren made Marcellus abbot; and his government, the page says, was excellent. So, with that divine and holy life of his, Marcellus fell asleep and was laid to rest in his own monastery.

The Охридски пролог keeps him on 29. децембар ст. ст., „Преподобни Маркел, из Апамије Сиријске.“ — the venerable Marcellus, from Apamea of Syria — and gives him five sentences. «Игуман обитељи „Неусипајушчих“ у Цариграду»: abbot of the monastery of the Sleepless Ones at Constantinople. A seer, a healer and a great wonderworker; he talked with the angels, and beat the demons easily and drove them off.

After his death he appeared to his brother-monk свети Лукијан and told him that he had begged God to take him too, and soon, into the heavenly kingdom. That name is left here as the book prints it: the entry gives the man nothing but his name and the vision, and nothing read says which of the Lucians this corpus keeps he might be. «Овај свети и славни муж упокојио се 486. године» — this holy and glorious man reposed in the year 486, which is the first year this folder has carried; it had only the fifth century before, and 486 sits inside it.
