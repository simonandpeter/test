# Sabbas the Vatopedine

Sabbas was born at Thessalonica about 1280, saint.gr says, of very virtuous parents who in the end became monastics themselves, and his own excellent biographer and acquaintance, [Philotheus Kokkinos](/saints/philotheus-kokkinos), praises them handsomely. In the world he was called Stephen. He had a good schooling and loved virtue, chastity, self-restraint and humility warmly from early on, so that he was dear to everybody. While still young he left country, kindred, standing and a father's love and came to the Holy Mountain.

He was put under obedience to a venerable and experienced elder in a Vatopedi cell near Karyes, and bore the elder's severity patiently: hunger, thirst, vigils through the night, standing and prayer went with him always. Out of his great humility he refused to accept the priesthood, and when he saw that he would not be heard he hid himself on the day of the ordination where they could not find him. When the Catalan raids came his elder withdrew with other fathers to a monastery of the Mother of God at Thessalonica, and Sabbas, who did not want his parents' attentions or the flattery of friends and kinsmen, went off instead to Lemnos, Lesbos and Chios, and by way of Asia Minor, Ephesus, Patmos and other islands came to Cyprus.

There, after warm and tearful prayer, he took up the hard road of folly for Christ's sake, throwing off all his clothes and wandering the mountains homeless and naked, and in the towns unknown, silent, without food and unapproachable. He ate wild herbs once a week and had no bed, roof, clothing, friendship or acquaintance. Some took him for a madman and drove him off, some began to revere him, and some beat him without mercy; once he came out bleeding from a Latin monastery where the monks at table took him for an impostor. When the Cypriots began to honour him as a saint while he still lived, the honour troubled him, and he left the great island.

He came after many adventures to the Holy Land, venerated the Tomb and the other shrines, went to Sinai as a beginner and a nobody, and shut himself up in the hermitages beyond the Jordan. Sent later on a peace-making errand to Constantinople, he foretold it would come to nothing and gave himself to prayer there for six years; he spoke sharply against the leader of the Zealot rising at Thessalonica, and after heavenly revelations stood up for the dogmas against Barlaam the Calabrian and Gregory Akindynos. He was counsellor to the emperor John VI Kantakouzenos, who proposed him to the Church as patriarch; the Church agreed to a man and Sabbas refused, and hid from the ordination although the emperor came in person to beg him for days. He spent his last six years almost enclosed in the monastery of Chora, foresaw his end, and reposed in peace about 1349.

The page adds that in October 1840, when the bone-house of the cemetery at Vatopedi was being repaired, the fragrant bones of a monk were found and given the name Eudocimus, and that the ascetic James told the monastery's assembly they were Sabbas the Vatopedine's; the elder Daniel of Katounakia reports that Sabbas appeared to a Vatopedine cell-dweller whom he healed and told him, I am not called Eudocimus but Sabbas the monk — only, tell the fathers of the holy monastery to go on calling me Eudocimus. His memory, the page says, is honoured on 11 October and on 15 June, on the Wednesday of Bright Week with the saints of Sinai, and on 10 July with the saints of Vatopedi. A full sung service in his honour was composed by F. A. Tzelepis, and the page prints no hymn of its own.
