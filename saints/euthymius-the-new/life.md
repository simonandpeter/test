# Euthymius the New

He was born in the region of Ancyra in the year 823, of believing parents, and at his baptism received the name Nikitas. At seven he was left by his father; at seventeen he married Euphrosyne and had a daughter, Anastaso. Wishing a life of ascetic labour, he left his wealth to his relatives and went away secretly to Olympus in Bithynia, where he was a disciple of holy ascetics and took the monastic habit, changing his name to Euthymius.

After twenty-five years of that labour he came to the Holy Mountain to continue his monastic ascent with greater severity. His first three years there he spent shut in a cave near what is now Nea Skiti, with acorns, wild fruit and chestnuts for his food. Always obeying the counsel of the elders who were bearers of signs, he came to be famed as a great ascetic. For a short time he lived outside Thessalonica as a stylite, strengthening many by his gentle word and healing them by his grace.

The troubles that came upon him from the crowds turned him back to the quiet of Athos, and even there his admiring monks would not leave him the stillness he loved, so that he was forced to withdraw again into unknown and untrodden places: to the island of Saint Eustratius, and then, after pirate raids, to what is now Vrasta in Chalkidiki. There he built a lavra and gained many disciples.

About the year 870 he founded a monastery of monks, the monastery of the Doves, near Thessalonica, and later a second, of nuns, where his mother, his sister, his wife and two grandchildren withdrew from the world. Knowing his end beforehand he retired to the little island of Hiera, at the mouth of the Pagasetic gulf, and there the much-labouring and wonderworking father gave up his spirit, in 898. His honourable relics were moved at once, in 899, to the metropolis of Thessalonica as a chosen blessing; in 1986 they were in the monastery of the Doves, where they are venerated to this day.

His life was written with love by his disciple Basil, bishop of Thessalonica, who also composed his service. According to his biographer he was a great ascetic whose fame reached far beyond the Holy Mountain early; when he came into Thessalonica the whole people met him as they would have met Elijah the Tishbite, kissing his hand and believing that they were sanctified, and holding that in the holy kiss each passed a blessing to the other. Many he set upright in virtue, and many his teaching made monks, freeing them from the sicknesses of soul and body.

His disciples were marked out by their heavenly and virtuous manner of life, the page says, and lived in the body as men without a body; the people of Chalkidiki received him as an angel come down from the tents of heaven, and those who heard him wondered at his wisdom and were amazed to say how the Holy Spirit moved his tongue as it moved the tongues of the apostles. Among his disciples it names Theostiriktos, Onuphrius, Ignatius, George, Ephrem, Paul, Basil, his grandchildren Methodios and Euphemia, who were tonsured in the two monasteries he had founded, his biographer Basil, and his own brothers Symeon and Theodore. The page has no Tropar tab, so no hymn is recorded here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on 15 October, as the Romanian does, and saint.gr gives his family and his houses. He was born in the days of the emperor Leo V the Armenian, in a small town of Galatia called Opso near Ancyra; his parents were rich and devout and were called Epiphanios and Anna, and he had two sisters, Maria, who was a presbytera, and Epiphania. Coming of age he married — his wife was called Euphrosyne — and had a daughter, Anastaso; and because he desired the monastic life, having settled his family affairs, he went to a monastery on Olympus in Bithynia, near the venerable Ioannikios, where after probation he was made a monk in 842, taking the name Euthymius for the Nikitas he had been called before.

After a good many years of ascesis in that community he left for the Holy Mountain; from there he returned to Olympus, and after many adventures and journeys he founded the monastery of Peristerai near Thessalonica in 871, settled there, and by the excellence of his spiritual life made it an excellent spiritual centre. So, having lived ascetically and in a way pleasing to God, he died in peace — on 15 October 894, this page says, where the Romanian calendar gives 898. His biography, it adds, was written by Basil, bishop of Thessalonica, who was also the saint's own disciple, and part of his relics is at the monastery of Peristera at Thessalonica. saint.gr also keeps a second entry for him on 14 October, which holds no life, and notes that certain synaxarists put his feast on 15 November.

The Russian calendar keeps him on the same menologion day, 15 October, and its life fills in the years between Olympus and Thessalonica. It calls him Nikitas in the world, a native of Ancyra in Galatia, the son of Epiphanius and Anna, who were virtuous Christians; meek, honest and obedient from childhood; left without his father at seven, and soon his mother's support in everything. After his military service he married at her insistence, and when a daughter had been born he left the house secretly to enter a monastery. Fifteen years he laboured on Mount Olympus, learning the monastic struggle from the elders there.

Then he moved to the Holy Mountain. On the road, learning that his mother and his wife were in good health, he sent word that he had become a monk, and sent them a cross, calling them to follow his example. On Athos he took the great schema and lived three years in a cave in complete silence, wrestling with temptations; and for a long time afterwards he stood on a pillar not far from Thessalonica, counselling those who came to him and healing their sicknesses. He so cleansed his mind and heart, the page says, that he was granted divine visions and revelations.

At the Lord's command, this life says, he founded two monasteries in 863 on Mount Peristera near Thessalonica, and governed them for fourteen years while remaining in the rank of deacon; in one of them his mother and his wife were tonsured. Before his death he withdrew to a little island near Athos and there reposed in 889, and his relics were brought to Thessalonica. The three calendars give three years for the founding of that house — 863 here, about 870 on doxologia, 871 on saint.gr — and three for his death, so neither is settled on this page. The Russian life closes by explaining the epithet: he is called the New to distinguish him from [Euthymius the Great](/saints/euthymius-the-great), whom the same calendar keeps on 20 January.

The Serbian calendar keeps him on the same day, and the Ohrid Prologue tells it in four lines. Born at Ancyra in 824 of righteous parents, Epiphanius and Anna; he served in the army, married, and had one daughter, Anastasia — whom it names, where the Russian life does not. He struggled sharply and long in the monasteries of Olympus and then on the Holy Mountain; for a time he lived as a stylite near Thessalonica; and near Thessalonica he founded one monastery of men and one of women. He reposed on the island called Sveshtenyi, near the Holy Mountain, at the end of the ninth century, and his holy and wonderworking relics rest at Thessalonica.
