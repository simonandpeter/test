# Lucia of Rome, companion of Rix

Doxologia's calendar for 6 iulie names her and prints only a short notice, which is all this folder holds. The holy martyr Lucia, it says, was seized by Rix, the deputy of the governor of the province of Campania in Italy, who tried to compel her to sacrifice to the gods. She refused outright, and confessed our Lord Jesus Christ with such courage that Rix himself received the Christian teaching and holy baptism.

The governor, hearing what had happened, ordered the two of them beheaded, and so they received the crown of martyrdom. The page gives no year, no place of burial and nothing of her life before her arrest. She is not the Lucia the Greek calendar keeps on 17 September, the widow of Rome betrayed by her son Eutropius, whom this corpus holds under her own name.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 6 July, and heads her «Αγία Λουκία η παρθενομάρτυς που μαρτύρησε στην Κομπανία (; - 301)». saint.gr says she was seized by [Rix](/saints/rix-the-vicar) the vicar, who pressed her to sacrifice to the idols and to deny Christ; that she was not persuaded, but instead led Rix himself to the faith of Christ, and was counted worthy by him of great honours and settled in a quiet house, where she gave herself to prayer and fasting. She asked Rix to go to Campania in Italy and to be martyred there for Christ, and he left wife, children, wealth and all worldly glory and went away with her. In that city both were arrested; before the governor they confessed Christ with courage and were beheaded, and with them many other holy martyrs — the company the same day keeps as the Twenty-four Martyrs.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her on the Julian 6 July, the same menologion day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep her on, and in five sentences it gives her the year and the story neither of them had. In Campania, it says, a barbarian emperor Auceius took her captive. He wished to live with her and she resisted him, and he then left her in peace to pursue her ascesis. She converted the emperor himself to the faith, after he had gained a victory in war through her prayer. And at the last she suffered for Christ together with the emperor at Rome, about the year 300.

That companion is named differently here than in the books this folder was made from. They give her a vicar of Campania, and this one a barbarian king called Auceius: the same province and the same shared martyrdom, with neither the name nor the rank the same, and this reading makes no claim that the two are one man.
