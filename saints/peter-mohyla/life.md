# Peter Mohyla

Peter Mohyla was born at Suceava on 21 December 1596, a son of Simion Movilă — later prince in Wallachia and in Moldavia — and of his wife Marghita. Through his grandfather Ioan, who had married a daughter of Petru Rareș, he was that prince's great-grandson; one uncle, Gheorghe, was Metropolitan of Moldavia, and together with another uncle, Ieremia, prince of Moldavia, founded the monastery of Sucevița. He was taught letters from childhood and brought up in the fear of God, and he loved to go to the Bukovinian monasteries, above all to the relics of [John the New of Suceava](/saints/john-the-new-of-suceava), for whom he kept a devotion all his life.

After his father was killed in 1611 by the wife of his brother Ieremia, who coveted the throne, the family wandered into Wallachia and settled for good in Poland, where they had many acquaintances and perhaps kin. There Peter studied at the Orthodox brotherhood school at Lvov under a very able deacon, from whom he took not only theology but Slavonic, Ukrainian, Polish, Lithuanian and French, and above all the Greek and Latin he was passionate about; it seems he studied at the lay Academy of Lvov as well, and he took courses at the Sorbonne. As a nobleman's son he also trained as a soldier and served young as a junior officer in the Polish army, fighting at Țuțora near Iași in 1620 and at Hotin in 1622.

The Orthodox of Ukraine were then in a hard case, above all under the fanatical king Sigismund III, who had stripped Orthodoxy of the right to exist and confiscated many churches and the private property of the Orthodox. Unable to bear it, Peter withdrew in 1622 to the east of the kingdom, bought a house and some land at Rubejovka not far from Kiev, and raised there a wooden church. Though he visited the Lavra of the Caves often enough, he preferred to stay in that village, praying and reading much and living a contemplative life.

After five years of retirement and study of Scripture and the Fathers he entered the brotherhood of the Pecherska Lavra, was tonsured in November 1627 under the same name, and within a few months was made superior and raised to archimandrite, at thirty-one. His election by the Lavra's brotherhood — the house was then a stauropegion of the Ecumenical Patriarchate — was confirmed both by King Sigismund, who persecuted the Orthodox, and by Patriarch Kiril Lukaris of Constantinople.

From his first years as a monk to the last moment of his life he wore in secret, under his ordinary clothes, a shirt woven of very coarse hair with iron studs set inside it, and on his body an iron belt with sharp teeth that left him many sores and often swelled his flesh; he prayed much, made many prostrations and shed rivers of tears. The asceticism did not hinder the scholarly and administrative work he had taken on for the good of Orthodoxy. In five years as superior he went on printing books to defend Orthodoxy against the Catholic proselytism that had begun to lay these lands waste immediately after the Union of Brest-Litovsk of 1596.

He founded a college, first at the Lavra and then at the Bratska monastery, out of which the famous Kiev Theological Academy grew in 1633; he gave the Academy his own estates at Rubejovka, bought before he became a monk. On 16 August 1628 he signed the declarations of the ecclesiastical synod of Kiev condemning the clergy who had gone over to the Union of 1596, and in 1632 he supported the election as King of Poland of Wladislav I, who recognised the rights of the Orthodox dioceses of the Metropolitanate of Kiev and maintained the Orthodox brotherhoods.

When the old Metropolitan Iov Borețki died, Peter was elected Metropolitan of Kiev, Halych and all Rus' — for his princely origin, but still more for his theological training and the support of those who saw in him the saving of an Orthodoxy threatened by the Latin Jesuits. He was consecrated bishop on 28 April 1633, the council headed by Patriarch Teofan of Jerusalem as delegate of the then Ecumenical Patriarch Kiril Lukaris. As metropolitan of the Ukrainian and Russian Orthodox Church within Poland he worked hard for thirteen years, chiefly at printing church books: over fifty are known to have been seen through the press by him.

Of the service books the most important is the Euchologion or Trebnic of 1646, which carries an original part of its own explaining the liturgical and dogmatic sense of the services and how priests should prepare to celebrate them; he also printed a Liturgikon (1629), a Gospel book (1636), an Apostol (1638) and a Psalter (1640). Among his books in defence of Orthodoxy is Lithos (1644), which contests the papal primacy and the Filioque by an irenic and objective exposition of the Orthodox dogmas.

His most important work is the Orthodox Confession, revised and corrected by Meletie Sirigul, the most learned Greek theologian of the seventeenth century, who was present at the Synod of Iași. That synod was held in the refectory of the monastery of the Three Holy Hierarchs, with delegates of the Ecumenical Patriarchate, of the Church of Kiev and of the Church of Moldavia, the last headed by Metropolitan [Varlaam](/saints/varlaam-of-moldavia). The Confession's recognition by the Synod of Constantinople in 1643 made it a Confession of the whole Eastern Church; it was signed first by the Ecumenical Patriarch Partenie I and later by the patriarchs of Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem.

It was printed in popular Greek at Amsterdam in 1677 and for the first time in Romanian at Buzău in 1691, translated by Radu and Ștefan Greceanu, and was long used as a textbook in the theological schools of Russia and of the Romanian lands. As a builder he restored the cathedral of Holy Wisdom in Kiev and the two monasteries that depended on it, renewed the great church of the Pecherska Lavra and rebuilt the church of the Saviour from its foundations. He instituted vicars and archpriests to guide the clergy, and a consistory to judge their faults.

Far from the places of his birth, he did not forget the Romanian people he came from and helped them whenever there was need: he sent printers to Târgoviște, Câmpulung and Govora, and to Iași, besides a press, four professors for the new Academy that the prince Vasile Lupu founded on the Kievan model. In 1645 he came to Iași and blessed the marriage of Vasile Lupu's daughter Maria to the Polish nobleman Ianusz Radziwill. On the frontispiece of many of his printings stands the arms of the Movilești or of the Romanian lands, and he called himself a son of the voivode of the land of Moldavia.

He went to the Lord on 22 December 1646 and was buried in the great church of the Pecherska monastery, having spent his whole fortune on restoring churches and founding and keeping up schools. For these merits he was canonised by the Orthodox Church of Ukraine in December 1996. In 1997 the Holy Synod of the Romanian Orthodox Church made that canonisation its own and asked the Archdiocese of Iași to see to the composing of his service and the painting of his icon; in 2002 the same Synod set his commemoration in the Romanian calendar on 22 December, the day of his death. On 13 October 2002 his canonisation was solemnly proclaimed at Iași, at the feast of St Parascheva, and his icon shown to clergy and people while his troparion was sung.
