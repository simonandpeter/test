# George of Rapsani

saint.gr's notice for the new martyr George is the longest of this day, and much of it is the site reasoning from documents and saying where it cannot decide. He belonged to a family of Rapsani that stood high socially and financially. His father was called Hatzilaskaris and was the son of Anastasios Psaltou — though whether Psaltou is a surname or an office of Anastasios, the page says, is not known. His mother's name was Smaragda, daughter of Theodoros Sakellaridis. All of that stands engraved in capitals on the conical lid of an old silver reliquary, made before 1900 to replace a still older and plainer one, which calls him the glorious new martyr George, who contended at Tyrnavos in the year 1818 under Veli Pasha, son of Ali Pasha.

Whether he had brothers or sisters, and how many, is not known either; but a deed of sale of 1852, found with other papers in the old library of the Karavasilis house, shows a Maria A. Hatzilaskareos as the late wife of the priest and steward Christodoulos Karazisis, and from that the page concludes that the new martyr had at least one sister. Tradition had already linked George by kinship to that same learned cleric of Rapsani; and the affinity was completed by a spiritual one, since Christodoulos had been George's teacher — only which of the two came first, or whether one caused the other, the site says it does not know exactly.

George is presented by tradition as a boarder and a graduate of the higher school of Rapsani, where Christodoulos Karazisis also taught. The mountain town was prospering in the eighteenth century, like Tyrnavos and Ampelakia: it had industry, exported an excellent wine, and drew the villages of lower Olympus to it. On that footing the learned and energetic Dionysius, bishop of Platamon and Lykostomio, who had also founded and protected the famous school at Ampelakia, founded the school of Rapsani in 1767; at its height it was compared with the schools of Milies, Zagora, Ampelakia and Tyrnavos. Since George contended in 1818, the page reasons, he must have left the school in 1815 or 1816; and afterwards he taught letters in his own birthplace with a godly zeal and a young man's vigour.

The riverside villages of the district were then inhabited mostly by Ottomans who dealt with, and in a limited way were bound to, the Greek Christians. One of them, a householder of the town of Dereli, wanting his son to get a worthwhile education, sent him to board with a friend at Rapsani; and so the Turkish boy began to learn his letters beside the Greek children at the feet of the schoolmaster George. He settled quickly into the school and competed with his classmates, encouraged by his teacher and perhaps given particular care by him. Because Greek education could hardly be separated in those centuries from Christian catechism, the boy was little by little but steadily influenced by the Christian faith and life — by his teacher's person above all, and by the charm of the church services he attended as a catechumen nobody had enrolled.

The change in him could not go unnoticed: first at home, each time he went to Dereli for the holidays, then among his kinsmen and in the wider circle. It became intolerable when in time the boy not only spoke with esteem of the holy things of the Romans but set them against those of his own people and despised theirs. Those who were enraged looked for the guilty man and found him in the school at Rapsani, and dragged him bound to Tyrnavos, where since 1811 Veli Pasha, son of Ali Pasha of Tepelen, had his seat as the appointed satrap of Thessaly. The charge was specific and, by Ottoman law, of the gravest kind — an attempt to make a Christian of a Muslim child — and that alone, whatever the attempt's outcome, carried a merciless sentence of death by torture.

Tyrnavos had a military court, but the page says it is not known whether George was tried by a military judge, by Veli Pasha in person, by some other mullah, or by a bench of several, as in the case of Gedeon who was martyred at Tyrnavos in the same year. What is known is that the process was short and final. They shut him naked in a red-hot bath, bored him with iron nails, nailed his feet into horseshoes, paraded him through the whole of Tyrnavos, nailed him to a square post of his own height, wound him about with ropes soaked in pitch, naphtha and other oily and spirituous liquids, and gave him to the fire; and by the grace of God, to strengthen him, the martyr suffered nothing. The painted lives of him add other scenes of torture — stranglings, dislocations, blows with the sword, and a red-hot iron crown set on his naked body — and show him carried to burial with his head on his shoulders, a priest named Demetrios censing, who had been summoned and ordered there for the purpose. By another tradition he was beheaded before the tortures already inflicted could kill him; and the page notes in that tradition's favour that his holy head is not among his relics, having been made away with by the Agarenes.

The beginning of the martyrdom, the site says, must have been in some administrative building at Tyrnavos, perhaps, as with the venerable martyr Gedeon, in the ruler's palace; then, since he did not yield to those first torments, he was paraded by Veli Pasha's order through all of Tyrnavos and taken across the Titarisios, and led to the second and harder stage. Other executioners were there — Aga Sevrani and one Frangos. On the Titarisios side there have been barracks from the oldest times to this day, and there, near the long bridge, George contended and was buried; which is confirmed, the page adds, by the bridge being painted at the saint's feet in both of the icon cycles.

That first night the Turkish sentries of the barracks saw a column of light reaching to heaven, standing over the new martyr's grave. The next day they told their superiors, who saw it with their own eyes on the second night and reported it to Veli Pasha, inviting him to see the thing himself if he wished. He, in his arrogance, did not deign to occupy himself with it, but ordered that the martyr's relatives be summoned to Rapsani as soon as possible to dig up and take away his body; which was done. A little later the holy relics were carried with much reverence and compunction from the cemetery of Rapsani, which lay about the church of the Dormition of the Theotokos, to the spacious Karazisis mansion nearby. There they lie to this day, treasured in a room dedicated to that purpose, lit by an unsleeping lamp with a gentle light, and open to every reverent pilgrim.
