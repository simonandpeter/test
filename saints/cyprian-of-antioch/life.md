# Cyprian of Antioch

Cyprian, the Greek synaxarion says, was a rich and well-born philosopher from Carthage in Libya, who lived in the years of Decius (249–251) and practised magic at Antioch. A pagan named Aglaidas fell in love with a Christian virgin called Justa and, when she would not return his love, went to the famous magician; but all Cyprian’s arts proved useless against the girl’s steady refusal, and, owning his craft bankrupt, he burned his books of magic before the bishop Anthimus and asked to be baptised and made a priest.

He rose through every rank of the priesthood and was at last elected bishop of Carthage, taking with him Justa, whom he ordained deaconess and renamed [Justina](/saints/justina-of-antioch). Denounced to Decius for his apostolic zeal, he was exiled to Antioch and imprisoned there, and later at Nicomedia Claudius beheaded him with Justina; devout Christians took up their relics and carried them to Rome, and buried them on the city’s most eminent hill. The synaxarion notes that Michael Galanos, in his Lives of the Saints, tells Cyprian’s life differently and holds all that is said of magic to be historically inaccurate.

The Romanian life, a long one, makes him the son of pagan parents of a Carthaginian family, given as a child to the service of Apollo, put to the sorcerers at seven and sent at ten to sacrifice on Mount Olympus, where he learned to raise winds, thunder and storms at sea and to blight gardens and fields; at twenty he studied at Memphis in Egypt, at thirty with the Chaldeans, reading the stars, and he came to Antioch perfect in every evil, having, he said afterwards, seen the devil himself face to face.

Paid by the young scholar Aglaidas to win Justina, he sent three demons against her one after another, the last in the form of a woman arguing for marriage, and her prayer and the sign of the cross drove every one of them off; a demon in her likeness vanished when Aglaidas spoke her name, and Cyprian himself, disguised as a woman and then as a bird, could not come near her door. His revenge fell on her household, her kin and the whole city in sickness and death among the cattle, until her prayer ended it and the city laughed at him.

Learning that the demons could not bear the sign of the cross, he renounced the devil; when it fell on him to strangle him he cried, “God of Justina, help me!”, signed himself with the cross, and was free. He brought his books to the bishop Anthimus, who at first suspected a trick, then burned them before all the Christians of the city and baptised him. He was made reader on the eighth day, subdeacon on the twentieth, deacon on the thirtieth and priest after a year, and soon afterwards bishop, and he made Justina a deaconess and abbess of a convent of virgins, and turned many pagans from the idols.

Denounced to Eutolmius, governor of the eastern parts, the two were taken to Damascus: Cyprian was hung up and his body scraped, Justina struck on the mouth and eyes, and a cauldron of boiling water left them unharmed, while Athanasius, a pagan priest who entered the fire in the name of Asclepius to shame them, died at once. The governor sent them to the emperor Claudius at Nicomedia, who condemned them to the sword. At the place of execution Cyprian asked that Justina die first, fearing she might be frightened by his death, and she bowed her head gladly; a man named Theoctistus, moved by their innocent death, knelt beside Cyprian, confessed himself a Christian, and was beheaded with him.

Their bodies lay six days unburied until strangers carried them secretly to Rome, where Rufina, a kinswoman of Claudius, buried them with honour, and many of the sick were healed at the tomb. The Greek synaxarion makes Cyprian bishop of Carthage; the Romanian life names no see. The Greek calendar keeps him with Justina on 2 October with two apolytikia, one of them the hieromartyr’s common, and two kontakia; the Romanian keeps him the same day with the hieromartyr’s troparion, a kontakion of his own and one for the two together.
