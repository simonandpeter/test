# Thomas of Constantinople

Thomas was made deacon of the Great Church of Constantinople for his many virtues, his perfect understanding and his right belief, by [John the Faster](/saints/john-the-faster-patriarch-of-constantinople), and was appointed sakellarios by him in the reign of Maurice. After John's death, and after the blessed Cyriacus who was patriarch after him, Thomas was chosen patriarch of the Church of Constantinople in the reign of the tyrant Phocas, and governed his throne well, shepherding the rational flock of Christ with wisdom.

In the days of his patriarchate a great wonder happened in the region of Galatia. In some of the cities there, when processions were made with crosses and litanies, the great crosses that were carried bent of themselves towards one another with an irresistible power, struck together and were broken. The report of it went everywhere, and Thomas called Saint Theodore of Sykeon, a man of foresight and a worker of wonders, out of those parts, and asked him what it meant.

Theodore assured him that the wonder was real, but would not say what it meant, saying he did not know such mysteries; and the patriarch fell at his feet and by that humility forced the old man to tell him what was to come. The bending of the crosses, Theodore said, their striking and their breaking, meant many dangers and a scattering in the Church of God and in the Greek empire: from outside a heavy invasion of barbarians, and from inside, men called by the name of Christ dividing from the faith and driving out, breaking and destroying one another, so that many churches of God would be laid waste; and all of it soon.

Thomas was greatly frightened, and begged Theodore to pray God to take his soul quickly out of his body before the foretold ruin came, so that he might not see such things fall on the Church. A little later he fell ill and sent word of it to Theodore, who had stayed on a while in Constantinople at the church of Saint Stephen; and the answer came back, «there we shall see one another before God». That same day, before the hour of Vespers, the patriarch rejoicing departed from the body and went to the Lord, in the reign of the same Phocas.

After him Sergius, a deacon of that same great church, took the throne; he was Orthodox at first and afterwards became the head of the heresy of the Monothelites. Then the dangers came upon the Church — divisions, scatterings, torments and the persecution of the Orthodox by the heretics — and with them the hard wars with the Persians, who laid the Greek lands waste with fire and sword, took Jerusalem, and carried off the honourable wood of the Cross into Persia. Rather than see it with his own eyes, Thomas had chosen to die; he had shepherded the Church of Christ three years and two months, and had laboured much against the heretics.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and dates his patriarchate to the month. saint.gr puts him in the seventh century and has him succeed Cyriacus, whose memory it keeps on 27 Οκτωβρίου, on the patriarchal throne of Constantinople on 23 January 607. He was marked out early for his reverence, his good sense and his modesty, and also for his firm hold on theology, and it was these gifts that [John the Faster](/saints/john-the-faster-patriarch-of-constantinople), kept on 2 Σεπτεμβρίου, valued when he ordained him deacon and then made him sakellarios.

As patriarch, the page says, Thomas I showed great care and concern for the affairs of the Patriarchate. He built the patriarchal house beside Hagia Sophia — the great triclinium which was called the Thomaites after him — and installed the patriarchal library in the lower part of the building. He stayed on the throne three years, guided his flock with good sense and contended against the heretics, and fell asleep in peace on 20 March 610, being buried on the 22nd of the same month. Its couplet says that Thomas, having left a life that is measured, fittingly found a life that is not measured.
