# Kale

saint.gr says she lived before the fifteenth century and came out of the East, that is to say Asia Minor, and it will go no closer than that. What it has to reason from is the acrostic of her canon, which says the poem is «τοῦ Κρήτης» — of the man of Crete. Both manuscripts that preserve her service gloss that phrase as Andrew, Archbishop of Crete, the poet of the Great Canon, which would put her before his repose in 740; but the page allows that some other bishop of Crete may stand behind it, and proposes Nikephoros Moschopoulos, metropolitan of Crete, whose traces run from 1285 to October 1322, which would allow her as late as the first half of the fourteenth century.

Her family was rich, the page says, and she spent the property on works of charity and well-doing. She was probably not a nun, since nothing in her service says so, and she took the homeless and the destitute into her own house, being given over to the service of the least and the suffering among her brethren. She lived in chastity, in virginity, in asceticism, in fasting and in unceasing prayer; and the mark of her life was love of her fellow men. What moved her, the page says, was the longing to keep the commandments of Christ, to imitate the divine compassion, and to show her love for those around her in every way she could.

Her service records wonders as well. Once, when she had kneaded bread to share out among the poor, God made the bread that was left to her not grow less, as in the Old Testament the widow's meal at Sarepta was not diminished though the Prophet Elijah and the widow and her children were fed from it. After her repose she went on benefiting people without a break, granting healing to the sick by her entreaties to the Lord; the healings are so many that the hymnographer speaks of a sea of wonders and calls her the fountain of them. She heals sicknesses of soul and body, and above all the painful, chronic and intractable ones — rheumatisms and arthritis, paralysis of the joints and deformities of the limbs.
