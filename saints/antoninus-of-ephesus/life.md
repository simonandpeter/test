# Antoninus of Ephesus

When the pagan Decius held the sceptre of the empire of Rome, he came from the city of Carthage to Ephesus, breathing great anger against the Christians there. At his command all the districts round about were gathered into the city to sacrifice to the empty gods, and the Church of the faithful was in persecution, so that many servants of Christ — priests, clerics and the rest — hid themselves wherever they could. The emperor set up idols in the middle of the city and made defiled altars for them, and on the third day ordered every Christian taken and forced to sacrifice.

They were dragged out of houses and caves and driven in dishonour before the assembled people. Those who were fearful and small of soul fell from the faith and worshipped the idols, and the rest wept for them; those who were strong gave themselves fearlessly to every torment and laid down their lives. The bodies of the killed were thrown on dung heaps, or left by the roads, or hung on the walls, and their heads set on stakes before the gates, so that the birds came over the walls and ate them, and the hidden Christians grieved that they could not bury their brethren.

There were then in Ephesus seven young men of the soldierly order, sons of honoured and great citizens: Maximilian, Iamblichus, Martinian, John, Dionysius, Exacustodianus and Antoninus. Though born of different parents they were of one soul in the faith and the love of Christ, and lived together in prayer and fasting. Seeing the evils done to the Christians every day, they broke their hearts with sighing and weeping; and while the emperor was at the pagan feast, they threw themselves on the ground in the church and prayed with dust on their heads.

Informers told the emperor of them, and he had them brought in iron chains, the tears still in their eyes and the dust on their heads. Asked why they had not been at the feast of the gods, Maximilian answered that they had one God, the King who lives in the heavens, of whose glory heaven and earth are full; that to Him they offered the sacrifice of confession and their prayers at every hour; and that they would bring no unclean offering to the idols, lest they defile their souls. The emperor had their soldiers' belts taken from them, the sign of their rank; but looking at the beauty of their youth he pitied them, gave them time to think better of it, had them loosed, and went off to another city.

Having that time free, they took gold and silver out of their parents' houses and gave it away to the poor, openly and in secret, and then agreed together to leave the city before the emperor returned and go into the great cave in the mountain to the east, which was called Ochlon, and there pray God to strengthen them for the confession of His name. They took silver enough for a few days' food. Iamblichus, being the youngest, was appointed to serve them: he went down to the city in poor clothes so as not to be known, gave part of the silver to the poor and bought food with the rest, and quietly found out whether the emperor had come back.

One day he saw the emperor come, and heard his order that all the chief men of the city and the army be ready the next day to sacrifice, and that the seven be searched for and brought with the rest. He ran back to the cave with a little bread and told them. They fell on their faces in fear and prayed, commending themselves to God's help; and since it was evening they ate the bread to strengthen themselves for the torments, and sat talking and encouraging one another to suffer manfully for Christ. With words of praise to God in their mouths they fell asleep, for their eyes were heavy with the grief in their hearts.

God, who always cares for what is useful to His Church, commanded these seven to fall asleep with a strange and wonderful sleep, meaning to work through them in a later time a glorious wonder and to assure those who doubted the resurrection of the dead. So they slept the sleep of death: their souls were kept in the hands of God, and their bodies lay in the cave as though sleeping, incorrupt and unchanged.

The next day the emperor searched for them and, not finding them, called their parents, who said that their sons had taken the gold and silver from them and given it to the poor, and had hidden in the great cave in the mountain Ochlon, and that they did not know whether they were still alive. He let the parents go and ordered the mouth of the cave stopped with great stones, that the youths might see no human face again but perish of hunger and thirst in the dark; for neither he nor the citizens knew that they had already fallen asleep in the Lord. But two of the emperor's chamberlains, Theodore and Rufinus, secret Christians, wrote their passion and their names on two leaden tablets, sealed them in a little brazen box, and set it among the stones at the door, saying that God might one day visit His servants and open the cave, and then their names and their deeds would be known.

Decius perished not long after, and after him the other pagan emperors and persecutors, each in his own time. Constantine the Great rose as emperor of the Christians, and after many years the sceptre came into the hands of the right-believing emperor Theodosius the Younger. In his days heretics rose who said there is no resurrection of the dead: some that those who die in the body die also in the soul, others that the bodies alone perish in the grave and the souls are rewarded without them, asking how bodies of which not even the dust remains could rise after so many thousands of years. Many doubted, and not laymen only but some bishops, and the emperor prayed with fasting and many tears that the Maker of all would Himself be the physician of the hurt done to His Church.

A man named Adolius, who owned the mountain called Ochlon and lived there, set about building a fold for his sheep, and his servants took stones for it from those with which the cave had long ago been stopped, not knowing that there was a cave there at all; and they made a hole in its mouth the size of a man. At that moment the Lord, who raised Lazarus dead four days, raised these seven also as though they had woken out of sleep. They rose and gave God the morning praise, then kissed one another as they used to, for they believed they had only slept a night: their clothes were whole, their bodies unchanged and blooming with health and the beauty of youth.

Talking together, they grieved over the idol worship and the persecution, and thought Decius was still searching for them. Maximilian sent Iamblichus with a silver coin to buy more bread than the day before and to learn what more Decius had commanded. Coming out, Iamblichus saw the stones lying by the door and wondered when they had been put there; and going down towards the city he was afraid to go in, lest he be known and taken to the emperor. But lifting his eyes at the gate he saw an honoured cross above it, very beautifully made.

In the city he gave the baker a silver coin, and the coin was large and bore the likeness and the name of emperors long dead. The baker showed it to another, and he to a third and a fourth, and the men whispered to one another that the young man had found some treasure hidden long ago. Iamblichus, frightened, told them to keep the coin and that he wanted nothing; but they held him fast, and the news of his arrest went through the city and came to the proconsul and to the bishop Stephen, who by God's ordering were together at that hour.

They had him brought with the coin, and marvelled that it was of very old emperors. Asked where the treasure was, he said he knew of none, that the coin was out of his parents' goods, and that he could not understand what misfortune had come on him. Asked whose son he was, he gave the names of his father, his mother, his grandfathers, his brothers and his kindred, and nobody knew any of them; and the proconsul told him he was lying and giving strange names never heard of, since the coin bore the likeness and the name of Decius, an emperor of long ago, and he himself was not thirty years old. Some said he was mad, others that he feigned it to escape; and at last he asked them whether this city was Ephesus or another.

The bishop thought within himself that God wished to reveal some mystery through this young man, and said to the proconsul, Let us go with him and see what wonder he has to show us. They rose and went, and the chief men of the city and a crowd of people followed. At the mouth of the cave they found, between two stones, the little brazen box sealed with two silver seals; and opening it before everyone they found the two leaden tablets, written that seven holy youths — Maximilian the eparch's son, Iamblichus, Martinian, John, Dionysius, Exacustodianus and Antoninus — had fled from the oppressor Decius and hidden in that cave, and that when it was stopped at his command they had ended there as martyrs for Christ. All who read it marvelled and glorified God with a great voice.

Going in, they found the saints sitting full of joy, their faces shining with the light of the grace of God and flowering like flowers. The bishop, the proconsul, the chief men and the people bowed down at their feet and gave glory to God, and the saints told them everything about themselves and about the persecution under the tyrant Decius. The bishop and the proconsul wrote at once to the emperor Theodosius, asking that he send honoured men quickly to see the wonder God had shown in his reign; and afterwards the emperor ordered seven coffins of silver and gold made for the bodies of the saints. That night the saints appeared to him in a dream and told him to let them rest on the ground as they had rested before. A council of many bishops was gathered there and kept a bright feast in their honour, and the emperor, giving many alms to the poor of that country and setting prisoners free, returned to Constantinople glorifying Christ our God.

How many years they slept, from Decius to their rising, the life will not say, because the writers do not agree. The Prologue, the Chronograph and the Synaxarion of the months say a hundred and seventy-two years; George called Cedrenus, the historian of Constantinople, writes in his Synopsis that they slept a hundred and seventy and rose in the twenty-third year of the reign of Theodosius the Younger. The life sets out its anchors and leaves the sum to the reader: Decius perished in the year 254 from the incarnation of God the Word, and Theodosius the Younger took the empire in 408, while the Prologue, under 22 October, says they rose in the thirty-eighth year of his reign.

Antoninus is named in the life only in the roll of the seven and on the leaden tablets that Theodore and Rufinus sealed at the mouth of the cave; nothing is told of him apart from his six companions, and this entry adds nothing to that. His six companions are kept here beside him: [Maximilian](/saints/maximilian-of-ephesus), [Iamblichus](/saints/iamblichus-of-ephesus), [Martinian](/saints/martinian-of-ephesus), [John](/saints/john-of-ephesus), [Dionysius](/saints/dionysius-of-ephesus), [Exacustodianus](/saints/exacustodianus-of-ephesus).

The Greek calendar keeps the seven on the same day, 4 August, and saint.gr sets the story out with its own figures. The seven youths lived, it says, in the years of the emperor Decius, 249 to 251; when the persecutions broke out they shared their property among the poor, left the city and hid in a cave until it should end. When they understood that their pursuers were near, they prayed through the night to die rather than fall into their hands, and God heard them: in the morning they fell asleep and did not wake, and the king walled up the mouth of the cave with a great stone. After 194 years, about 446, under Theodosius the Younger, a heresy appeared at Ephesus which taught against the resurrection of the dead; and then a boy bought bread in the Ephesus market with a coin of Decius. They questioned him — it was Iamblichus — and he led them to the cave, where the others were found alive.

The Russian calendar keeps the seven twice over, and says why. days.pravoslavie.ru names them all in one line on 22 октября ст. ст. — «Семи отроков, иже во Ефесе: Максимилиана , Иамвлиха , Мартиниана , Дионисия , Антонина , Константина (Ексакустодиана) и Иоанна (ок. 250; 408-450).» — and its life closes with the two dates: by one tradition, which entered the Russian Prologue, the youths fell asleep the second time on this day, while a note in a Greek menaion of 1870 has them fall asleep the first time on 4 августа and wake on 22 октября. It adds that they are remembered again in the service of the church new year on 1 сентября.

The same page fills in the household: Maximilian was the son of the governor of Ephesus and the other six the sons of other notable citizens, friends from childhood and all of them in military service, whose soldiers' belts were stripped from them when they confessed Christ before the emperor. Decius let them go while he was away on campaign, hoping they would change their minds. The youngest went into the city in a beggar's rags to buy their bread, and it was he who heard that the emperor was back and looking for them. Two of the officials who watched the cave's mouth walled up were secret Christians, and it was they who sealed among the stones a casket holding two leaden tablets with the seven names and the manner of their death. A Russian pilgrim abbot saw the relics in the cave in the twelfth century, the page says.
