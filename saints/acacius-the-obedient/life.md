# Acacius the Obedient

The whole of what doxologia prints of Acacius is the story John of the Ladder tells in his fourth step, the step on obedience. Leaving the world young, Acacius came to a monastery in Asia, took the monastic habit and lived the ascetic life, a simple man and a wise one; and there was in that house an idle and very angry old man, who — nobody knows how, says John — took as his disciple this monk whose name means guilelessness.

What he suffered from that elder was past most men's belief: not insults and dishonour only, but blows and wounds; and his patience had no bounds. An elder of the place, seeing him daily bearing the worst of it like a bought slave, would ask him, What is it, brother Acacius? How are you today? Have you won a crown? — and Acacius would show him now a blackened eye, now a wounded neck or head. Knowing then that this patience was not the dumb endurance of a beast, the elder would say, Good, good, brother; bear it with joy and you will profit.

He lived under that unmerciful old man not one year, nor two, nor three, but nine; and having come to the measure of virtue appointed by the One who knows all hearts, he went to the Lord to receive from Him the crown of patience and of martyrdom. Five days after he was buried in the fathers' cemetery, his elder came to the other and said, Father, brother Acacius is dead. Believe me, old man, the elder answered, I do not think brother Acacius is dead. If you do not believe me, said the other, come and see.

They rose at once and went to the grave, and the elder called to him as to a living man — as to one who after his falling asleep was truly alive — and said, Brother Acacius, are you dead? And the good and knowing disciple, showing obedience after death too, answered, I am not dead, father. How can a man die who is a doer of obedience? At those words the old man who had been his superior fell terrified on his face in tears, asked the abbot of the lavra for a cell near the grave of his own disciple, and lived out the rest of his life soberly, saying to the fathers, I have done murder.

The page adds what was seen afterwards at the tomb. One harvest, when the monks went out to earn their year's bread, two brothers were left behind, one sick and one to keep the house; the sick man died, and the other, unable to dig, opened Acacius's grave and laid the body on the saint's, reckoning that one obedient in life would be obedient after death. The next day he found the body thrown out; he put it back, and found it out again.

On the third day he argued with the saint: I have heard of you, holy Acacius, that you loved obedience as no other, bearing all manner of wounds and beatings for it; and now I see you turned disobedient, proud and unloving to your brothers. You see that I am weak and alone and cannot dig another grave, and you will not do me this much — so either let him lie with you, or, if you cast him out again, I will pull you out of the tomb and throw you away as a rebel and lay the brother's body in your place. He put the body back and went away, and coming next day he found the brother lying in the saint's grave, and the saint he did not find; and the empty tomb is shown to this day under the name of the tomb of Saint Acacius.

The Greek calendar keeps him on 26 November, where the Romanian keeps him on 7 July, and calls him «ὁ ἐν τῇ Κλίμακι», the one in the Ladder, for the reason the folder's whole life rests on: he is mentioned by Saint [John](/saints/john-of-the-ladder), who wrote the Ladder. saint.gr adds the house. He was a monk in Asia Minor, at the monastery of Kellibara on Mount Latros, and was distinguished for his inexhaustible patience; and it gives one saying of his, that people are mistaken who think he is never angry — he is angry, but against the two greatest enemies, of whom one is Satan, and the other it is needless to tell you, and he would point at himself. In the monastery he had a very difficult superior, and against him Acacius would not say the least thing; the abbot abused him and Acacius loved him, though it grieved him that the abbot's salvation was in danger from the whole of his conduct. Acacius died young, the page says, with a patience that became a proverb and a living hope in God; and it adds that a service for him was written in his honour afterwards.

The Russian calendar keeps him on 29 ноября as well as on 7 июля, calls him of Sinai rather than of the Ladder, and serves under his name the same story at length — the narrative the author of the Ladder set down as his example of patience, of obedience, and of what obedience is paid with. It is told at second hand, from John the Sabbaite, whom the writer says he knows by experience to be free of passion and clean of every lie: in a monastery in Asia there was an old man of very careless life and bold temper who somehow acquired a disciple, a youth named Acacius, simple in character but wise in understanding, and used him so hard that many would not believe it.

He lived nine years with that unmerciful elder and then departed to the Lord, and was buried in the sepulchre of the fathers. Five days later the elder went to a great old man of the place and said that brother Acacius was dead. The great one answered that he doubted it. Told to come and see, he rose at once, went to the sepulchre with the dead man's own teacher, and called to him as to the living — for truly, the page says, he was alive after death — «Brother Acacius, are you dead?» And the obedient one, keeping his obedience after death, answered: «Father, how can a worker of obedience die?»

The elder who had been his teacher fell on the ground in tears for fear, and afterwards asked the abbot of the lavra for a cell beside Acacius's grave and spent the rest of his life there in virtue, saying always to the other fathers: «I have committed a murder.» The day page prints a kontakion under his name in the second tone, which is the first hymn any calendar read has given him, and it turns on exactly that: that by imitating Christ's willing humility he threw the proud tormentor down.

The Охридски пролог takes his life, it says at the outset, from the Лествица — the Ladder of [John of the Ladder](/saints/john-of-the-ladder), who tells it in his own famous book. The young Acacius was a novice under a wicked elder in a monastery of Sinai. The angry elder rebuked him and abused him day after day, often beat him, and tormented and ill-used him in every way. But Acacius did not complain: he bore all of it patiently, in the conviction that it was useful for his salvation; and whenever anyone asked him how he lived, he would answer „Добро, као пред Господом Богом!“ — well, as in the sight of the Lord God.

After nine years of that obedience and that torment Acacius died. The elder buried him, and then went and lamented to another elder, a holy man, saying „Акакије, ученик мој, умре“ — Acacius my disciple has died. „Не верујем,“ the holy elder answered, „Акакије не умре.“ — I do not believe it; Acacius has not died. Then the two of them went to the dead man’s grave, and that holy elder called out: „Брате Акакије, јеси ли умро?“ — brother Acacius, art thou dead? And the obedient Acacius, obedient after death as well, answered: „Нисам умро оче, немогуће је умрети послушноме“ — I have not died, father; it is not possible for an obedient man to die.

At that the wicked elder repented, and shut himself in a cell near the grave of Acacius, where he passed the rest of his life in penitence and prayer. The entry gives no year at all, and the nine years are a span of service and not a date, so the sixth century this folder reads off the Russian calendar stands here untouched and unconfirmed. This book keeps him on 29 November, which is the day of the folder’s Russian feast.
