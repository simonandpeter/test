# Anna of Novgorod

Anna was the daughter of Olof Skötkonung, the first Christian king of Sweden, and doxologia calls her, for the holiness of her life, one of the most venerated saints of the Slavonic Orthodox calendar. The Viking princess was known in her own country as Ingegerd; when she came to Rus the Russians called her Irene, and the name Anna she received only a little before her death, when she took the monastic habit. Her father and her mother, Queen Estrid, were both wholly given to their new Christian faith, and the page quotes that Estrid came of the most distinguished family in Sweden and was famed for her brilliant mind, her loving heart and her generosity.

As a young woman she had much freedom. The hagiographers, the page says, tell that she took part in the assemblies of the people and in ceremonies, was present at receptions in the palace, lived in her own chamber where she was free to receive guests, held estates and managed her own property, had an armed guard at her disposal and travelled through the country as she pleased. In the popular tradition of the Scandinavian north she is remembered as a woman without self-love, kind-hearted, wise, lively, bold, and of great influence over those among whom she found herself.

She proved a princess of peace, persuading her father to make peace with neighbouring Norway and arranging that her sister Astrid should marry the Norwegian king Olav II Haraldsson. She herself had been promised to that king, who was to become the holy Martyr Olav, kept on 29 July and called the protector of Norway for his great work in spreading Christianity there. In the end her father judged it more useful that she should marry the illustrious prince Yaroslav of Novgorod, and Irene, the later Anna, obeyed.

Yaroslav sent envoys to King Olof seeking an alliance to be sealed by marriage with his daughter, who was then not yet twenty. As part of the agreement made before the wedding, Anna asked that the town and district of Ladoga be given her as a wedding gift, with the right to manage it herself and to appoint a deputy to help her govern it; her demands were granted, and in the summer of 1016 she and Yaroslav were married at Novgorod. Soon afterwards the princely court moved to Kiev, which Yaroslav had inherited from his father, the holy Prince Vladimir, kept in the Orthodox calendar on 15 July.

The Grand Duchess of Novgorod took a direct part in the government of the country beside her husband, sometimes going into battle with him, sometimes helping him conclude treaties of peace with his enemies. They had seven sons and three daughters. She helped to bring up several kings' children as well, among them Edwin and Edward, the sons of King Edmund of England, and Magnus, the son of the holy King and Martyr Olav II Haraldsson of Norway; and in 1028 Yaroslav and Anna gave shelter to King Olav himself, when he fled the invasion and revolt in his own land.

Her husband, known to later ages as Yaroslav the Wise, got that name from the prudence with which he governed his people and his great interest in learning. The Russian Primary Chronicle records that in 1037 he gathered at Kiev a number of scribes who translated books from Greek into Slavonic, and that he issued the first code of laws of the Russian lands, known as the Russian Justice; he also had the Chronicle itself written, which the page calls a masterpiece of medieval Slavonic literature and the chief source for early Russian history.

In 1037 the faithful ruling pair built a monastery in Kiev and dedicated it to Saint Irene, Anna's own patron. It was built beside the cathedral of Holy Wisdom, which Yaroslav had raised on the model of the Hagia Sophia of Constantinople, and both belonged to a building programme which, the page says, made Kiev the artistic centre of eleventh-century Rus. In 1045 Anna and her husband were present at the laying of the foundation of the cathedral of Holy Wisdom in Novgorod, which their son Vladimir began and which was finished after her death.

She fell asleep in the Lord on 10 February 1050, having taken the monastic schema a little before her death, showing by it, the page says, her deep piety and a true Christian humility. Her tomb, placed beside that of her son, Saint Vladimir Yaroslavich, in the cathedral of Holy Wisdom at Novgorod, became a source of healing in the course of time. The Church honours her also on 4 October, together with her son. The page carries no hymn.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and heads her page «(; - 1056)». saint.gr says she was born in Russia and was the wife of the prince Yaroslav, that she worked apostolically for the strengthening of the Orthodox faith, and that she left the world to live in a monastery in stillness and prayer, falling asleep in holiness and peace in the year 1056. It adds a warning worth recording: she is often confused with the mother of Saint Vladimir, the enlightener of the Russians, and this happens chiefly because she too had a son of that name. Her memory, the page ends, is honoured also on 4 October.
