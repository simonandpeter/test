# Galaction of Emesa

In the city of Emesa in Phoenicia lived a man of good family, honoured and rich, named Cleitophon, whose wife Leucippe, daughter of Memnon the governor, was barren; and for that she was reproached and at times beaten by her husband. Both were pagans and diligent at the temple of Artemis. A monk named Onuphrius went about the city in rags so that no one should know him for a Christian, begging bread from house to house and teaching the faith where he could. Coming to Cleitophon's gate, he was at first shut out and then let in, and he asked Leucippe why she sighed so from the heart.

She told him of her childlessness and of the gold she had spent on doctors and sorcerers for nothing, and he answered that she was barren because she hoped in Artemis and not in the God who can give fruit to the womb. She believed and was baptised in secret; her husband, hearing it, rejoiced and was baptised by the same old man; and in time she bore a son, whom Onuphrius baptised with the name Galaction, prophesying over him that the child would hate this earthly life and desire the heavenly one.

Galaction was given to letters and learned quickly the making of verses, rhetoric, philosophy and astronomy. When he was twenty-four and his mother had died, his father betrothed him to a very beautiful maiden named [Episteme](/saints/episteme-of-emesa). He would visit her but would not give her the customary kiss, and when she grieved at it he told her plainly why: she was not baptised, and he would not share with the unbaptised. She said she believed his God and wished to be baptised; and because the persecution had killed some priests and scattered the rest, he baptised her himself in the river Chifos, having told her to go there as though to wash. He also baptised a slave of her father's house named Eutolmius.

Eight days later she told him a dream of beautiful palaces where three choirs sang: monks, fair virgins, and comely men with wings of fire. He explained the choirs to her — those who had left riches and wives for Christ, those who had left betrothed and parents, and the angels of God — and when she wished that they too might dance with them, he answered that they might, if they kept their virginity unspotted and renounced the world. She gave him her word that hour; they gave away in secret all they had, and went out by night with Eutolmius.

After ten days they came to the mountain called Publion, where a monastery of ten monks stood and, further off, a small house of four aged solitaries whose eldest was a deaconess. The abbot received them and tonsured them; Episteme was sent to the women, Galaction kept with Eutolmius. There Galaction was never seen standing idle, but always at work for the house or at prayer; his fast was past measure, and some weeks he tasted nothing. So carefully did he guard his purity that in all his years there he would not look on a woman's face, and would not go with the brethren even to see the deaconess or his own sister.

When soldiers came for the monks, all fled but Galaction, whom they found in his cell reading the Scriptures, and they led him away bound. Episteme, hidden on the mountain with the virgins, saw it and begged the deaconess to let her follow, saying that through him she had known Christ, that his own hands had washed her in baptism, and that she could not be parted from him in this age or the next. Blessed and sent, she ran after the soldiers and cried to him to take her with him to the torments, and the soldiers took her too.

Brought before the governor and asked who they were, Galaction answered that they were Christians and monks, and that the gods of the pagans were stone and wood made by men's hands. They were hung up and torn; their hands were cut off, and they blessed God who teaches the hands to war; their feet were cut off, and they asked that their feet might stand in the heavenly courts; their tongues were cut out that they might not blaspheme the gods, and they were silent with their mouths but did not cease crying out with their hearts. Then they were taken out of the court and beheaded, and their bodies left unburied.

Eutolmius the monk, once the slave of Galaction's father-in-law, followed them from a distance in changed clothes; and seeing their end he took away the relics of his master and mistress by night, wept long over them, and buried them with honour. Afterwards he wrote their life and their passion, and it is his account the Romanian calendar prints. The page gives no year.
