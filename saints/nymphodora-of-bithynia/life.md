# Nymphodora of Bithynia

Nymphodora was one of the three sisters Menodora, Metrodora and Nymphodora, of Bithynia, sisters in the flesh who became sisters in the spirit too, the Romanian Lives say, choosing with one soul to serve God rather than the world and its vanities. The radiance of their virginity and the beauty of their souls and bodies made them the boast of the Christians, and the cares and habits of the world did not occupy them; their one care was to adorn themselves with modesty and sobriety, not with braided hair or gold or pearls or costly clothing (1 Timothy 2:9).

For the love of Christ they left their homeland and went to live on a hill near the hot springs of Pythia, where they practised ascesis and cultivated their chastity still more, and so were counted worthy by God to heal sicknesses, and crowds ran to them. When the governor Fronto learned of it he sent and seized the three sisters, and seeing the prudence and good sense, and the fearlessness, with which they faced him, had them tortured with the most horrible tortures; they bore them bravely and so gave up their souls in glory to their bridegroom Christ, in 290.

The governor wished to burn their bodies, but the flames burned him instead and then a torrential rain put the fire out, and the Christians buried the three virgins with reverence. Their verse says the three, named for gifts, gave the gifts of their flesh to torment and died beaten on the tenth; the Greek and Romanian calendars keep them on 10 September with a troparion and kontakion — beheaded by the sword, the Romanian troparion says, they now follow the Lamb.

The Ohrid Prologue, for the Serbian calendar, tells it so. [Menodora](/saints/menodora-of-bithynia), [Metrodora](/saints/metrodora-of-bithynia) and Nymphodora were three sisters by birth from a place in Bithynia in Asia, brought up in the Christian spirit, who left the town for the wilderness to lift their minds to God and to live out their lives in purity and virginity as true brides of Christ. They gave themselves to great labour, fasting and prayer until God adorned them with the gift of wonderworking, and when the sick began to be brought to them for healing they became famous against their will.

A prince named Fronto heard of them and dragged them to judgement, and marvelled at their faces: great fasters as they were, with bodies dried out, their faces shone with inward peace and the grace of God. He flattered them first, promising to send them to the emperor to be married to his nobles, and when nothing moved them he had Menodora tortured first and her sisters thrown into prison. When he cried to her, wounded and bloodied, to sacrifice to the gods, she answered: could he not see that she was doing nothing else than offering her whole self as a sacrifice to her God?

When she had died under torture he brought out the other two, set them beside their sister’s body and urged them to deny Christ, and when they stood unshaken he killed them too with cruel torments. Then thunder struck from heaven and killed the godless Fronto and his servants, and the Christians buried the martyrs with honour. They suffered, the Prologue says, between 305 and 311, under Maximian Galerius.
