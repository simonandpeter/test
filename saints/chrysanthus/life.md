# Chrysanthus

Polemius, a man of high and noble birth from the city of Alexandria, came to Rome with his son Chrysanthus, was received with honour and given a senator's place by the emperor. He put his only son to all the books and then to philosophy; and Chrysanthus, having gone carefully through the Scriptures, the Gospel and the books of the Apostles, told himself that it was not wise to turn back from the light of truth to the darkness of the pagan writings, and that the treasure he had found must not be taken from him.

He looked for a teacher, wanting not the wise rhetoricians he had studied under but plain men, like the unlettered fishermen who had caught the whole world for Christ. He heard of a Christian named Carpophorus, learned in the Scriptures, hiding in a cave in the mountains; sought him out with tears, found him — a priest — and was taught by him for some months, understood the mysteries of Scripture and was baptised. Seven days after his baptism he began to preach Christ openly to the people.

His kinsmen warned his father that the thing would fall on their own heads, and Polemius shut him in a cold dark room and starved him, giving him a little food at evening. Chrysanthus took the prison and the hunger not as punishment but as training in fasting and stillness, and was better pleased there than in bright and comfortable halls. So the household advised a different cure: let him be given pleasures and a beautiful, clever wife. His father dressed him in cloth of gold, put him in a splendid palace and shut in with him his handsomest maidservants, charged to draw him to the love of the flesh; and afterwards they brought him [Daria](/saints/daria), a learned virgin from among those who served the goddess Athena.

Daria, instead, was won: Chrysanthus told her that if she kept her soul and body pure and adorned her heart as she had adorned her body, angels would be her companions, apostles her friends and martyrs her neighbours, and Christ Himself her Bridegroom. They lived together in a spiritual union, and so many men and maidens followed them into virginity that Rome rose in an uproar — the betrothed were being taken away, and who would then bear children? The prefect ordered them both seized and tortured unless they sacrificed, and gave Chrysanthus to a tribune named [Claudius](/saints/claudius-husband-of-hilaria).

The soldiers bound him with raw ox-sinews until they cut to the bone, and the bonds fell away faster than a word can be spoken; the shackles and the stocks crumbled to dust, the filth poured over him turned to fragrance, the green hide they wrapped him in did not dry in the day's heat, the iron chains broke, and the dark cell shone as though many candles burned. Claudius came, questioned him, had him beaten with staves that turned soft as rushes in the beaters' hands, and then stopped, saying that he had tried every sorcery there is and this was the power of God. He fell at the saint's feet with all his soldiers, and with his wife [Hilaria](/saints/hilaria-wife-of-claudius), his sons Jason and Maurus and his whole household was baptised.

The emperor Numerian had Claudius drowned with a stone about his neck and his sons and soldiers beheaded, and ordered Chrysanthus and Daria destroyed by torments. Chrysanthus was hung naked on a tree and lit candles brought to burn him, and the wood broke, the bonds shattered and the candles went out; the hands of those who tried to touch Daria shrivelled. Taking the wonders for magic, the emperor had them both carried out of the city to the Salarian way, thrown into a deep pit and buried alive under stones and earth; and singing and praying they went down into it, and were received by God as a living sacrifice.

Many wonders and healings were worked at the place, and a great crowd of Christians used to keep the martyrs' day in a cave nearby and commune there. The tyrant had the cave's mouth stopped and the earth thrown over it, and the whole assembly was martyred within, among them [Diodorus](/saints/diodorus-the-presbyter) the presbyter and [Marianus](/saints/marianus-the-deacon) the deacon and many clergy, more than could be counted. The account, the page ends, was written down by the brothers Varinus and Armenius at the command of Stephen, pope of Rome, and sent into every city.

The Greek calendar keeps the two of them on the same day and fixes the year. saint.gr sets them in the reign of Numerian (243–284) and makes Chrysanthus a man of Alexandria, son of an eminent idolater named Polemon; he was catechised in the Christian faith by some Christian and baptised, and when his father learned of it he shut him up and, to tear him from the faith, gave him the beautiful [Daria](/saints/daria) to wife, an Athenian and an idolatress. Instead of drawing her husband to idolatry, the page says, the opposite happened: she too believed in Christ and was baptised. They were denounced to the eparch Celerinus, who handed them over to the tribune [Claudius](/saints/claudius-husband-of-hilaria) — and it was the martyrs' endurance and persistence under torture that astonished Claudius, so that he believed in Christ together with his wife [Hilaria](/saints/hilaria-wife-of-claudius), his sons [Jason](/saints/jason-son-of-claudius) and [Maurus](/saints/maurus-son-of-claudius) and the soldiers of his guard. Then Chrysanthus and Daria were thrown into a pit and buried alive; it was the year 283.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the company on the same menologion day and tells the whole of it in one entry. Chrysanthus was the only son of a great boyar, Polemius, who had moved from Alexandria to Rome. As the son of rich parents he went through all the schools of the world, with the most learned men for his teachers; but worldly wisdom confused him and left him uncertain what the truth was, and he grieved over it. Then a written copy of the Gospel and of the Acts of the Apostles came into the young man's hands, and reading them he was lit up by the truth. He wanted a teacher, and found one in a priest named Carpophorus, who instructed him and baptised him.

His father was not pleased, and tried everything to turn him from the faith of Christ. Failing in that, the wicked man first tried to corrupt him, shutting him up alone with shameless girls; but Chrysanthus overcame even himself in this and kept his purity. Then his father forced him to marry a pagan girl, [Daria](/saints/daria) — and Chrysanthus counselled her to accept the faith of Christ as well, and to live as brother and sister though married in appearance. When his father died he began to confess Christ freely and to live as a Christian, he and his whole household.

In the reign of the emperor Numerian both he and Daria were terribly tortured for the faith. But their torturer [Claudius](/saints/claudius-husband-of-hilaria) himself, seeing the patience of these honourable martyrs and the wonders shown at their torture, received the faith of Christ with his whole house; and so Claudius was drowned in water, his two sons were beheaded, and his wife, having said a prayer, breathed her last at the place of execution. Daria held out so far under the torments that the heathen shouted: Daria is a goddess! At the last it was ordered that Chrysanthus and Daria be buried under stones in a deep pit, and a church was raised on that place afterwards.

Near that pit was a cave, in which some Christians once gathered for prayer and communion in memory of the holy martyrs Chrysanthus and Daria; the heathen, learning of it, fell on the place and shut the cave up, and so drove those Christians out of this world by death into a better one, where Christ the Lord reigns in eternity. These glorious martyrs, Chrysanthus and Daria, and the rest with them, among whom [Diodorus](/saints/diodorus-the-presbyter) the priest and [Marianus](/saints/marianus-the-deacon) the deacon, suffered for Christ at Rome in 283 and 284 — the years these folders already record.
