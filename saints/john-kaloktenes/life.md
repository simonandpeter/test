# John Kaloktenes

John was born in the middle of the twelfth century at Constantinople, of noble parents — Constantine Kaloktenes and his wife Maria — who had been childless for years and had dedicated him to the Church from his conception. His mother brought him up carefully and saw to his education, and the synaxarion the page quotes says that, going on from learning to learning by his great diligence and quick wit, he became the pattern of a true student. The age he was born into was a hard one: the empire under Manuel the First Komnenos was threatened on every side, and the Normans, having taken Corfu and sailed round the Peloponnese, came up the Corinthian gulf and fell upon Thebes.

Thebes was then at a height of prosperity, a great commercial and industrial centre famous for its silk works, the capital of Sterea Hellas and the seat of a metropolitan who bore the title Exarch of all Boeotia. The Normans found the city undefended, because the imperial troops were seeing to Constantinople, and took it; gold, silver and precious stones were all carried off, warehouses, private houses and churches all stripped bare, and many of the citizens taken captive, above all those with a name as skilled silk workers, who were carried to Palermo in Sicily and taught the craft to the people there.

At twelve his father handed him to the Grand Domestic of the Byzantine court, the head of the armies, to be trained as a soldier; but his own inclination to the Church rather than the army, his mother's influence and the vow his parents had made obliged the Grand Domestic to enrol him instead among the hieromonks. He studied the sacred letters, rhetoric and philosophy and the outside sciences; and for his great piety and his love of the Mother of God he was counted worthy, one day as he read the salutations, to hear her voice: to his «Χαίρε, Νύμφη, Ανύμφευτε» came the answer «Χαίροις και εσύ των Θηβών προστάτα» — rejoice thou also, protector of Thebes.

Meanwhile the state of the Church at Thebes was desperate. Its metropolitan had died of grief at it and under the Norman oppression; the new one elected at Constantinople died on the road; and the Patriarchate had not taken the city's condition seriously until the Thebans pressed it, and then chose for the see of all Boeotia the man most fit for it, John, who was living as a monk in a monastery of the capital. He who had already been chosen by the Mother of God as protector of Thebes could not refuse the election, and it gave the Thebans hope and strength.

He was a man of the spirit and of action both. He found the city and the country ruined, and began at once. He raised the church of the Theotokos out of his own patrimony, and his fatherly teaching and his continual almsgiving and help brought many Jews and Armenians to the Christian faith — the page's own account of those years is that some two thousand Jews had by then come to the town, offering work and money, and that he had two fronts to fight, the want of goods and the wearing away of the faith. From that time the faithful people gave him the name of the New Almsgiver.

Besides that he made many works of common utility. He diverted the river Ismenos, which brought fertility and power to the plain of Thebes and to the suburbs of Pyri and Saint Theodore, setting water mills going, and he laid the foundations of aqueducts; the river was later called the Agiannis, and at the point of the diversion the Thebans built a church in his honour whose ruins survive. He built bridges. His Ioanniad, named in imitation of Basil's Basiliad, was famous: he founded an old people's home, poorhouses, hospitals and schools, and with the water brought into the town he saw to the planting of trees, mulberries above all, for the silk. He oversaw every work himself, daily. Boldest of his works was the Parthenon, a school for women, where they learned letters and crafts and helped the destitute with their handiwork.

It is no exaggeration, the page says, that in that hard time he not only saved the city from a ruin so complete that we should not find its foundations today, but raised it to a place of eminence and made it a great industrial and commercial centre; and for that the Thebans had declared him their protector while he was still alive. He took part in the Synod of Constantinople of 1166 under Manuel Komnenos, and its acts preserve his teaching on the Son as fellow creator with the Father, and on the words "my Father is greater than I" as said by Him according to the human nature He took, in which He shared flesh and blood with us.

He fell asleep in peace between 1182 and 1193, and when his holiness was recognized a magnificent church was raised where his relics were laid. That church does not survive; a beautiful one was built on its foundations at the end of the last century and stands to this day. There are rumours, the page says, that his honourable relics are in Italy. And the memory of his works of infrastructure is kept to this day among the people of Thebes and of the country round it.
