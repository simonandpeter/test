# Thais of Egypt

Thais was an Egyptian, the daughter of a woman of bad life who taught her the same trade and put her into a house of prostitution. She was very beautiful and her beauty was talked of everywhere; men brought her gold and silver and costly clothes, some ruined themselves for her, and others fought over her until the threshold of her house was bloodied. An elder named Paphnutius heard of it, put on lay clothes, took a gold coin and went to her.

He asked whether she had a more hidden room, where nobody could know of them. She answered that if he was ashamed before men the doors were locked already, but that if he feared God there was no place hidden from him, for God sees even under the earth. He asked whether she too knew of God. She said she knew of God, and of the kingdom of the righteous and the punishment of sinners. Then, said the elder, why do you ruin men and destroy so many souls, since you will be answerable for their sins as well as your own?

Thais fell at his feet weeping and asked for three hours. She gathered everything she had earned by her sins, some forty pounds of gold, carried it into the middle of the city, lit a fire and burned it all in front of the people, calling out to everyone who had sinned with her to come and see their gifts burn. Paphnutius took her to a convent, shut her in a small cell with the door nailed up and only a window for bread and water, and told her she was not worthy to say the name of the Lord, but should look east and say: God, who made me, have mercy on me.

She stayed three years so. Then Paphnutius went to the great [Anthony](/saints/anthony-the-great) to learn whether God had forgiven her, and Anthony set his disciples to pray all night for an answer. It was shown to the one they called Paul the Simple, who saw the heavens open and a bed of glory with rich coverings, guarded by three maidens, and a crown lying on it; and when he said it must be prepared for his father Anthony, a voice said it was for Thais who had been a harlot. Paphnutius went to bring her out, and she begged to be left to weep longer; fifteen days after she came out she fell ill, and on the third day of the illness she died in peace.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 8 October, and puts the blame where the Romanian life put it. saint.gr says that Thais's beauty was of the rare kind, and that the one who plotted against her honour was her own mother — a coarse woman who wanted great wealth and did not scruple to use her daughter to get it, so that Thais was drawn into the road of dishonour at barely seventeen. She became rich and a harlot both; honest people loathed her, no family door was open to her, and the very men who used her would never have brought her to meet their mothers and sisters, for she had been reduced to an object of carnal satisfaction and nothing more.

Then she fell into great sorrow; and the Church, says the page, may have lost a sheep but never stopped looking for it. When Paphnoutios of Sidon learned the state of her soul he prayed and resolved to work for it, and he did not miss. He visited her, and by the grace of God the miracle came: hot tears of repentance ran down her cheeks. She threw all her wealth into the sea, because the price of dishonour did not deserve to be used in the holy work of almsgiving. From then on she lived poor but rich in faith, in repentance, in sobriety, in prayer, in obedience, in humility and in kindness; she was received into a devout company of women, and she died caring for the sick and the helpless.

The Russian calendar keeps her on its own 8 October, «Прп. Таисии (IV)», and reaches two lives which tell one story. Brought up by her mother in a spirit far from Christian piety — the longer life calls the mother a woman of bad conduct — she led a vicious and dissolute life, was famous for her beauty, and drew many men onto the path of sin and, the longer says, to their ruin. The report of the harlot Thais spread through the whole of Egypt and came to the elder Paphnutius, a strict ascetic who had turned many of the lost to salvation.

Paphnutius dressed in worldly clothes, came to her, and asked her to appoint him a meeting in a place where not only men but God himself would not see them. She answered that this was impossible, because God is everywhere, sees everything and knows everything; the longer life says she was a pagan but not without elementary notions of God, and that she smiled as she said it. Seeing in her soul a spark of the fear of God, the elder made himself known to her. He showed her the whole weight and foulness of her sins and told her of the answer she would have to give before God for the souls of the people she had seduced and destroyed.

His words so moved her that she gathered all the riches she had got by that shameful way, burned them in the city square — the longer life says that by this she publicly condemned the life she had led — and went with the elder to a monastery, where she spent three years enclosed. Turning to the East she repeated one short prayer without ceasing: «Создавший меня! Помилуй меня!» — thou who madest me, have mercy on me.

The shorter life ends on her words to the elder before her death. From the minute I came into the cell, she said, all my sins were constantly before my eyes and I was washed in tears remembering them — and Paphnutius answered: it is for this, and not for the strictness of your enclosure, that the merciful Lord has had mercy on you. The longer life ends differently: she died about the year 340, and the monk [Paul the Simple](/saints/paul-the-simple) had a vision that the Lord had forgiven her sins and had counted her worthy of the kingdom of heaven, at which the elder Paphnutius rejoiced greatly, for he had succeeded in saving a soul that had been lost.
