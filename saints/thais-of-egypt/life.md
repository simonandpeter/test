# Thais of Egypt

Thais was an Egyptian, the daughter of a woman of bad life who taught her the same trade and put her into a house of prostitution. She was very beautiful and her beauty was talked of everywhere; men brought her gold and silver and costly clothes, some ruined themselves for her, and others fought over her until the threshold of her house was bloodied. An elder named Paphnutius heard of it, put on lay clothes, took a gold coin and went to her.

He asked whether she had a more hidden room, where nobody could know of them. She answered that if he was ashamed before men the doors were locked already, but that if he feared God there was no place hidden from him, for God sees even under the earth. He asked whether she too knew of God. She said she knew of God, and of the kingdom of the righteous and the punishment of sinners. Then, said the elder, why do you ruin men and destroy so many souls, since you will be answerable for their sins as well as your own?

Thais fell at his feet weeping and asked for three hours. She gathered everything she had earned by her sins, some forty pounds of gold, carried it into the middle of the city, lit a fire and burned it all in front of the people, calling out to everyone who had sinned with her to come and see their gifts burn. Paphnutius took her to a convent, shut her in a small cell with the door nailed up and only a window for bread and water, and told her she was not worthy to say the name of the Lord, but should look east and say: God, who made me, have mercy on me.

She stayed three years so. Then Paphnutius went to the great [Anthony](/saints/anthony-the-great) to learn whether God had forgiven her, and Anthony set his disciples to pray all night for an answer. It was shown to the one they called Paul the Simple, who saw the heavens open and a bed of glory with rich coverings, guarded by three maidens, and a crown lying on it; and when he said it must be prepared for his father Anthony, a voice said it was for Thais who had been a harlot. Paphnutius went to bring her out, and she begged to be left to weep longer; fifteen days after she came out she fell ill, and on the third day of the illness she died in peace.
