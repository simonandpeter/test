# Leo (Ershov)

Leo Ershov was born on 12 February 1867 in the town of Krasnoufimsk in
Perm province, the son of an Old Believer merchant. In 1881 he finished
the two-class town school and took up trade himself; he had great
knowledge of the schismatics’ doctrine, was respected among them and was
a reader in the Fedoseyev sect, but serious spiritual searching and the
striving to find the truth led him gradually to the conviction that it is
the Russian Orthodox Church that keeps the apostolic teaching pure, and
at twenty-seven, on the feast of the Meeting of the Lord, “by personal
sincere conviction of the truth, holiness and Orthodoxy of the
Greek-Russian Orthodox Church and his own wish” he was joined to the
Church by chrismation. Two years later, in 1896, he was ordained priest
and sent to the Holy Trinity cathedral of Krasnoufimsk; shortly before he
had married Nadezhda Mikhailovna, and between 1897 and 1902 they had
three children, Sergius, Lydia and Helen. In 1901 he was given the
nabedrennik for his services, and beside his pastoral work took up the
mission — first as anti-schismatic missionary for the Krasnoufimsk and
Kungur districts, and from 1904 as diocesan missionary; knowing the Old
Believers’ ideas thoroughly and having joined Orthodoxy by conviction, he
worked so successfully that he was given the violet velvet skufia, the
council of the Perm brotherhood of St Stephen thanked him at its general
meeting for outstanding missionary work, and in 1914, in consideration of
it, he was elected an honorary member of the brotherhood of St Stephen and
his successors Gerasimus, Pitirim and Jonah. No less active and
successful was his teaching: for more than ten years he ran the parish
school of Krasnoufimsk, received the archpastoral blessing with a
certificate in 1905 for his gifts to it, taught the Law of God there, at
the Krasnoufimsk Cyril and Methodius school, at the schools of two
neighbouring villages and, in 1910–1914, at the town’s two-class girls’
school, sat on the district branch of the diocesan school council from
1911, was twice elected candidate delegate to the diocesan school
congresses, and wore the silver medal of the church schools’ twenty-fifth
anniversary. For his spiritual discernment, it seems, the clergy chose him
repeatedly as confessor — of the first deanery district in 1904, and
unanimously of the Krasnoufimsk deanery in 1910 — and the same year as a
member of the audit commission, three years later as deputy to the
diocesan congresses of clergy, and in 1910–1916 of the deanery council;
for all this he was given the kamilavka. In 1917 he was a pastor of fifty,
known for his work as confessor and missionary, his teaching and his
public work, decorated and respected by clergy and flock — who knows what
more he would have given the Church; a year later his life was cut short
by a martyr’s death at the Bolsheviks’ hands in the troubles of the civil
war. In the summer of 1918, beside the fighting in many Urals towns, mass
peasant risings against the Bolsheviks broke out in almost every district,
provoked by the requisition squads’ plain robbery, the ban on free trade
and forced mobilisation into the Red Army; the Krasnoufimsk district,
where hostility to the Bolsheviks prevailed, was not left out. In the
first days of July the Reds announced the mobilisation of the men born in
1894–1898 in the Krasnoufimsk, Kungur and Osa districts; all the peasants
but a few villages refused to give soldiers, an order went out to shoot
without mercy all who would not obey, and mass unrest began — a military
staff and a People’s Army at Bolshiye Klyuchi, a mobilisation of all men
under forty-five at Achit, the district’s peasant centre, and within days
the rising took in nearly the whole district. The rebels marched on
Krasnoufimsk and won a first battle six versts from it, but short of arms
fell back to Achit, then left it and retreated towards Ekaterinburg; a
wave of reprisals followed, and the clergy, whom the Reds habitually
accused of counter-revolutionary agitation for their sermons and talks
with parishioners, could not escape arrest. Fr Leo spoke out fearlessly
against the mass arrests and shootings the Bolsheviks were carrying out,
and for that he was arrested: one Sunday during the service Red Army men
burst into the church firing at random, pushed through the worshippers to
the sanctuary, and when he promised to go with them but asked to finish
the service, he bravely completed the last Liturgy of his life, and after
the people had venerated the cross went to the door — where one soldier
began tearing the pectoral cross from him and another pulled his beard and
beat him, and they dragged him bodily out of the church. On the porch he
saw the priest of Verkh-Suksun,
[Alexander Malinovsky](/saints/alexander-presbyter-martyr-1918-20-august),
ringed by soldiers; the two priests tried to embrace, which so angered
the soldiers that they beat them about the head with rifle-butts. Both
were put in the Krasnoufimsk prison, and a fellow-prisoner of their cell
remembered the days dragging on in the usual dull prison way, the
governor’s kindnesses for which he was soon dismissed and jailed with them,
the peasant rebels in the other cells losing several of their number
almost every night until by 1 September those cells were empty, and the
new haul of dozens that filled them again in the night of 1 September:
in the night of 2 September, besides the Socialist Revolutionary Vershinin
and Novgorodtsev, there were shot from our cell the officers Skornyakov,
Vasev and Nikiforov and two priests, Malinovsky and Ershov. Fr Ershov died
honestly at his post, having raised his voice against the mass arrests and
shootings; in the morning we knew the details — the condemned had been
led from the cell, shackled together in twos and threes, and shot outside
the town in the so-called Cold Ravine. On 24 September, after the Whites
entered Krasnoufimsk, both priests were given the funeral service with
honour in the Holy Trinity cathedral and buried in one wide coffin by its
apse. In 2000 he was glorified in the synaxis of the new martyrs and
confessors of Russia for the Perm diocese, and in 2002 his relics were
found, resting by the north wall of the vault beside the altar of the
Krasnoufimsk cathedral: a man of middle height, the wrist of his left hand
shattered, the ribcage and right temple damaged — beaten or tortured, it
seems, before death — with locks of grey hair and beard, parts of a
vestment greened by its oxidised copper thread, a Gospel, an inlaid wooden
pectoral cross and a small silver baptismal cross. Since 5 December 2002
his relics rest openly in the Holy Trinity cathedral, where a record is
kept of the wonders and healings by prayer to him and to the hieromartyrs
Alexander Malinovsky and Alexius Budrin; the Russian calendar keeps him on
20 August (Julian) and in the synaxes of the saints of Ekaterinburg and
Perm.
