# Prochorus the Lebednik

The venerable Prochorus came from Smolensk in Russia and took the monastic life at the monastery of the Caves at Kiev, tonsured by the abbot John, who had felt his godly zeal. He struggled with great severity and exactness in the principles of the monastic state, and what marked him out was his fasting: he never tasted any food but water and a bread made of a bitter plant, called lebeda in Russian, which is why he is called the Lebednik; and God, seeing his ascesis and his love, turned the bitterness of that weed into sweetness.

The years he lived in were hard for Russia and her people. The disturbances of war brought famine, and death threatened the inhabitants of Kiev. Tirelessly the venerable one gathered that bitter weed, baked loaves of it daily and shared them out among his hungry brothers. Some wanted to copy him and make the bread themselves, but they could not eat it, it was so bitter; and then they understood that something wonderful was happening with the holy ascetic, who did this work by the grace of God.

In his time came the civil war between the prince Michael Svyatopolk and the princes of Vladimir and Peremyshl. Provisions reached Kiev with difficulty, and the trouble grew when there was no salt left. Then the venerable one gathered all the ash from the monks' cells and began to pray, and the ash became salt, which he shared out and which never ran out.

The merchants complained to the hard prince Michael Svyatopolk, who ordered the salt carried to his court so that he could sell it himself; and then the salt became ash again, and the truth was uncovered, that God's name might be glorified. The prince, broken, went to the monastery of the Caves and fell down humbly before the venerable one, who prophesied his victory over the Polovtsy. Prochorus fell asleep in peace in 1107, and the prince buried him in tears near the monastery of Saint Antony of the Caves.

The Serbian church keeps him on the same day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović calls him the wonderworker of the Caves and explains the by-name at once: in all his time in the monastery he never tasted bread, but fed on goosefoot, kneading it his own way and making a kind of loaf of it. Given with his blessing, the Prologue says, that loaf was sweet as honey; stolen from him, it was bitter as wormwood.

It tells the salt the same way. When salt ran out in Russia he gave the people ash instead, and the ash he handed out with a blessing became salt, while ash anyone took for himself stayed ash. The prince had the whole of it carried from the cell to his palace without asking and without a blessing, and those who tasted it there found it was ash and not salt; so the holy man told the people who kept coming to him for salt to go to the palace, and when the prince threw the ash out of his house to take it and carry it home as salt. They did, and it was salt again. The prince was filled with reverence and love for him after that, the Prologue says, and when he died in 1107 laid him in the grave with his own hands beside the great Russian saints of the Caves.
