# Irene of Chrysovalantou

Irene of Chrysovalantou, who received apples from Paradise, was born in the ninth century, of a rich family of Cappadocia. In her time, after the death of her husband Theophilus, [the empress Theodora](/saints/theodora-the-empress) governed the empire as regent for her young son Michael; she upheld the Church against the iconoclast heresy and helped to restore the veneration of the holy icons, the victory kept on the first Sunday of Lent.

When Michael was twelve, Theodora sent messengers through the whole empire to look for a virtuous and well-bred girl to be the wife of the future emperor, and Irene was chosen and accepted the marriage. On the road to Constantinople she crossed Mount Olympus in Asia Minor and asked her escort to stop, so that she might have the blessing of Saint Joannicius, who lived on that mountain and received only the worthier pilgrims for counsel. He had the gift of foresight and knew that she was coming and what would happen in her life; and he received her and told her to go on to the capital, where the convent of Chrysovalantou stood in need of a superior for its community of virgins. Astonished at such counsel, she fell to the ground and asked his blessing, and he blessed her, gave her much spiritual advice and let her go on her way.

At Constantinople her kinsfolk, who were nobles of the emperor's court, met her with great honour; and since the steps of a man are ordered by the Lord, God disposed that Michael should marry another. What the page tells of her years in the convent of Chrysovalantou, of the apples brought to her from Paradise that gave her feast its name, and of her gifts and her struggles, is longer than what is given here.

At the end, having asked God to guard her flock from the assaults of the devils, she smiled when she saw the angels who had come to receive her soul; then she closed her eyes and fell asleep in the Lord. She was over a hundred and one years old when she died, and her face was young and beautiful. A great crowd came to her burial, rich and poor, and shared in many wonders; during the funeral service itself the convent was filled with a fragrance that could not be described, and countless miracles happened afterwards at her grave.

There are today several churches and monasteries raised in her honour, and many wonder-working icons of her. A chapel in her honour was built on Mount Olympus, near the monastery of Saint Ephrem the Syrian, and beside it the nuns planted a thuja in memory of the cypresses that bowed to the saint; without anyone grafting it, the thuja turned into a fruiting apple tree, and the nuns give those apples to pilgrims as the blessed fruit of Saint Irene. In some parishes it is the custom on her day to bless apples and give them out to the faithful.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 28 July. saint.gr says the venerable Irene lived in the years of the empress [Theodora](/saints/theodora-the-empress), who set the holy icons up again. She came from Cappadocia and was marked out not only for her piety but for her beauty of body and her noble upbringing, and she had been asked in marriage by a distinguished man of the palace and had set out for Byzantium. On the way, however, she passed by the monastery of Chrysovalantou, and was so drawn by the company of the nuns that she took the great decision to stay with them: she rejected the glories of the world, went back to her own country, sold what she had, helped many poor people with it and laid the rest of the money up in the monastery.

She became a nun, and her life inside the monastery was very ascetic and holy. When the abbess died, Irene was appointed her successor in spite of her refusal, and from that new place she carried out her duties excellently. God moreover endowed her with the gift of prophecy and of working wonders, and by her prayer she freed many from demons. She had a foreknowledge of her death, and died in peace, full of joy at the welcome journey to heaven before her. Above the notice the page sets a distich: «Eιρηνικώς έζησας Eιρήνη πάλαι, Kαι νυν κατοικείς ένθα ειρήνη βρύει» — peacefully you lived of old, Irene, and now you dwell where peace springs up.
