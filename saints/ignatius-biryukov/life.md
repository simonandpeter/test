# Ignatius (Biryukov)

Ignatius — John Adrianovich Biryukov in the world — was born on 25 May 1865
at Biryuch in Voronezh province, the son of the peasants Adrian and
Catherine Biryukov; his father was a good smith besides. He went to the town
school at Biryuch, sang in its choir and came to love singing, church
singing above all: the family belonged to the Dormition church, where his
elder brother Michael led the choir and John and four more brothers sang in
it, and the singing had a great and gracious influence on a boy who was by
nature mischievous, until the reading of church books, and of the lives of
the saints most of all, turned him toward monasticism. His mother, who loved
him best of all her children and called him her little swallow, begged him
not to go far but to the Valuyki monastery nearest the town, and on 9
October 1878, when he was thirteen, his parents prayed and blessed him to
enter it. The superior, the archimandrite Ignatius (Alexeyevsky), took the
boy as his cell-attendant and gave him the obedience of the right choir, to
read and sing every day; the fathers loved the meek, well-behaved novice and
his skilled singing and reading, and when the choirmaster died the superior
set John over the choir. The monastery kept a free school for orphans in the
settlement below it, fed, clothed and taught them, and the children sang in
the choir too — the right choir was twenty voices, basses and tenors from
the brethren and trebles and altos from the school. As choirmaster he
studied the theory of vocal and instrumental music, learned the violin and
the harmonium well enough to teach singing by, and in 1888 published a book
of the dogmatic hymns of the eight tones in znamenny chant; a few
compositions of his own he was too humble to print, and they stayed in
manuscript. His passion for the singing drove him past his strength, and the
reproofs of his superior did not stop it, until he fell ill with
tuberculosis and began to bleed; the doctor from Valuyki prescribed a long
leave, and the archimandrite blessed him to spend seven months on pilgrimage
to the holy places and Athos.

In January 1894 he went to Kiev, took a passport for Jerusalem and Athos,
and sailed from Odessa with the pilgrims, two weeks to Jaffa by way of
Constantinople, where Saint Sophia struck him most, Piraeus, Smyrna,
Alexandria and Beirut; from Jaffa the narrow railway brought them to
Jerusalem on 22 February. Having venerated the Sepulchre and Golgotha and
taken the blessing of Patriarch Gerasimus, he went with four hundred Russian
pilgrims under the Mission’s guard for three weeks through Bethlehem, Hebron
and the oak of Mamre, Ein Karem, the Mount of Temptation, Jericho, the
Jordan, Nazareth, Tiberias, Tabor, Cana, Nain and Samaria, then to Olivet,
Gethsemane and the sheep pool, the lavra of Sabbas, the mosque on the site
of the temple and the Armenian and Latin houses; he communicated in the
Mission’s Trinity church on Great Thursday and watched the Patriarch wash
the feet in the square before the Anastasis, and kept Pascha in the
Anastasis at a liturgy of every Orthodox nation that ended at five in the
morning, his soul as full of thanksgiving as it had ever been. On Thomas
Sunday he took the Patriarch’s blessing and sailed for Athos, where he spent
three months in the Russian houses singing and copying music, and where a
storm off the coast nearly drowned him and the monks in an open boat — he
wept and prayed, and the boat held, and the memory of it made him shudder
for the rest of his life. He came home by Pochaev and Kiev on 25 August,
well and strong again; the superior, fearing for his health, took him off
the choir altogether, made his pupil the monk Nicephorus choirmaster, and
set him in turn under the cook, under the refectorian, as bell-ringer, and
again as cell-attendant and teacher of the monastery school. Entered
formally among the novices in 1896, he was tonsured by the archimandrite on
20 December 1897, in his thirty-third year, with his superior’s own name,
ordained hierodeacon in 1898 by Bishop Joseph (Sokolov) and hieromonk two
years later by Archbishop Anastasius (Dobradin) of Voronezh at the Mitrophan
monastery, and took the choir again. In 1900, the archimandrite being
seventy-seven, the diocese made him assistant superior; in 1908 and 1910,
when his health broke again, he was sent travelling, to the Trinity and
Alexander Nevsky lavras and Valaam, and to New Athos, Sukhumi, Komany,
Novorossiysk and Krasnodar. On 3 February 1912 the old archimandrite died
and the brethren chose Ignatius superior; he was made igumen on 19 April
1912 and archimandrite afterwards.

When the monastery had been closed he wrote its history down so that a true
record should survive: founded in 1615 by the elder Cornelius three versts
from Valuyki, on a peninsula between the Valuy and the Oskol under wooded
hills, with a hundred desyatins of wood and eighty of meadow; about a
hundred brethren, twelve hieromonks, six hierodeacons, four hieroschemamonks
and two schemamonks of true monastic life; workshops for shoes, clothes,
joinery, smithing and locksmithing, a water mill and a steam mill, two
gardens, a candle works, a bakery, a kitchen and one table for all, the
superior included, eight horses, twenty milch cows, three hundred hives, a
painting shop and an icon stall, a medical assistant, a dispensary and a
sick room; three churches, matins at three in the morning and the whole day
set down hour by hour; and the old archimandrite’s dying charge to his
successor never to forsake those in need, strangers, beggars and the poor —
which, he wrote, I always tried to keep exactly, until by God’s judgements I
became a stranger myself. In the First World War he proposed that the
monastery keep the families of the men of the villages round who had gone to
the front, and it clothed, fed and paid them the whole war, and took the
wounded from the hospitals into its guest-houses at its own table. After the
abdication the monastery was searched and its superior held a week and
released. In March 1918 the Valuyki executive committee demanded all the
monastery’s cash by the next morning, a contribution of eight hundred
thousand roubles from the house and ten thousand from the brethren; he
called the brethren together — let every soul be subject to the powers, he
told them, there is no power but of God — and with the committee’s man
counted the whole of it, ten thousand one hundred and five roubles, and
delivered it in the morning. Three days later thirty armed men broke in at
ten at night in search of arms and gold, searched every cell, drove the
monks into theirs at gunpoint, opened the cathedral and brought him there,
and while he knelt and prayed held two revolvers to his head and fired over
it to make him say where the gold was hidden — there had never been any, and
the few silver vessels and the last fifty-two roubles from the candles were
all they found — then stripped him on the porch and left him standing in the
frost while they searched the guest-house, and went at two in the morning.
In 1924 the authorities closed the monastery; he went to relations at
Biryuch, but how long can a man live at home after forty years in a
monastery? On 4 January 1926 he came to Voronezh, where the great hierarch
Peter (Zverev), whom the people loved, had arrived the year before, lay two
months with typhus, and was set by the archbishop to lead the people’s choir
in church, the work that fitted his gift and the archbishop’s wish to draw
the people into the service. In the summer of 1929, his health failing, he
took leave, stayed with a niece at Zadonsk, and, as if foreseeing his
arrest, went round every place his life had touched — two days at Valuyki
with a nephew, both of them spent long hours in prayer at the walls of the
closed monastery where his boyhood, youth and manhood had passed — and three
days at the shrines of Kiev; back in Voronezh he served a week and asked to
be retired. Early in 1930 the OGPU opened a case against the Voronezh clergy
and he was arrested with many others; he kept Pascha of 1930 in the prison,
in a cell with priests, monks and laymen, among them the archpriest
Alexander Arkhangelsky and the priest Theodore Yakovlev, serving morning and
evening from Great Thursday by the few books they had and by memory, a towel
for a stole and one cypress cross among them, blessing water on Great
Saturday, reading the Acts in turn until midnight, singing Christ is risen
at twelve, and serving the Paschal liturgy after the morning count, the
whole cell communicating, kissing the cross that Ignatius held, and sharing
the blessed bread and the parcels the priests received. He was charged with
being the real leader of the monastics of the Central Black Earth region and
through them of agitation against the collective farms and the Soviet power,
admitted nothing, and on 28 July 1930, in a case of thirty-eight, was given
ten years in the camps by the OGPU college, changed a year later to exile in
the Northern region for the rest of the term; he was sixty-six, the exile
was past the strength of a gravely sick man, and after a year and a half, on
27 September 1932, the feast of the Exaltation of the Cross, he died. The
life is by the igumen Damascene (Orlovsky) from the Voronezh FSB archive;
the Church keeps him on 15 September of the old style, 28 September, and in
the synaxis of the Voronezh metropolia.
