# Conon of Isauria

Isauria, the chief city of the country of the same name, received the holy faith in our Lord Jesus Christ from the Apostle Paul, the preacher to the nations, who preached there and brought some to believe. In the days of that apostolic preaching there was a man named Nestor, living in a village called Vidania some eighteen stadia from the city, who with his wife Nada had a son: the Conon of this story.

When the boy was grown his parents wished to marry him and betrothed him to a beautiful maiden named Anna. But before the wedding the Archangel Michael appeared to Conon in the shape of a man full of light, taught him the holy faith, took him down to a stream and baptized him in the name of the Holy Trinity, gave him the divine Mysteries, signed him with the cross, and became invisible.

On the wedding day, when bride and bridegroom had been brought to the chamber, Conon set a lighted candle in a vessel and asked the maiden which was the better, the light or the darkness; and when she answered that light was better than darkness he taught her about the keeping of virginity and a pure life in the faith of Christ, and she agreed with him in everything. Afterwards he persuaded his own parents to leave the idols, and baptized his father and his mother. His mother Nada departed to God in a good confession, and the holy maiden Anna followed her.

There was in that country a dreadful dark cave among trackless mountains, a temple of the idol Apollo and a dwelling of demons. When the feast came round and the whole city was making ready to go there, Conon asked them who Apollo was and what his power might be, and mocked them: had they ever heard a voice or a word from their god? The people fell into uproar, some rushing at him to kill him, and they demanded a sign. He proposed that the young men on horseback and on foot should race him, an old man weak in body, to the cave; and when they came he was there before them, and had waited long.

Standing before the cave he commanded the idol aloud to say which was the true God, itself or his Lord Jesus Christ; and at the wonder that followed the whole people cried out with one voice that the God of Conon was the one true God. In another cave in that same country lived a destroying demon which fell on travellers like a robber; Conon went there with the people following at a distance, rebuked it, and it came out where all could see it in the shape of an unclean old woman, trembling and shaking.

He lived on in his village of Vidania, in his father's house, working wonders and healing the sicknesses of all who came to him in faith. He bound the demons, and by God's command they even delivered him from robbers who broke into his house one night thinking to find riches there, and kept guard over his good name and his fields. Then persecution came upon the Christians and a commander named Magdon arrived in Isauria by imperial order; having first seized Onesius of the village of Usorova, he tortured Conon. The Isaurians gathered armed and ran to Usorova to kill the commander, who fled when he heard of it; and two years after that suffering Conon went over to God, and the whole country mourned him and buried him with his parents.

The demons he had bound he had shut up in vessels. Long afterwards men who did not know what was in them, thinking that gold and silver were hidden there, broke one open, and the demons came out like dark unclean smoke and raised a fearful storm, so that in that village nobody dared go out of doors after sunset. The apparitions were driven off by the saint's prayers, and the other twenty-nine vessels, the life says, remain buried to this day under the foundation of the church of Saint Conon.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and puts him much earlier than a reader might expect. saint.gr has him born about the end of the first century at Badine, a village of Isauria in Asia Minor, and says he lived in the apostolic times; his parents, [Nestor](/saints/nestor-father-of-conon) and Nada, were idolaters at first. He became a Christian young, and when his parents pressed him to marry he agreed with his wife that they should live as brother and sister, dedicated to God. The synaxarion, the page reports, has it that the Archangel Michael guided him — baptised him, taught him the Name of the Holy Trinity, gave him the divine Mysteries to the end of his life, stood by him and granted him the grace of strange wonders. Conon brought his own parents to the Christian faith, and his father Nestor was martyred for Christ; and the Greeks who resisted him and said there was no God but the idols he persuaded and prepared to confess Christ with a loud voice and be baptised. By the grace of God he subdued the demons as well, and some of them by his order guarded the fields while others were shut up in jars.

His idolatrous fellow townsmen bore his missionary work and his influence over the heathen very badly. One day he went into a temple of the idols and prayed warmly to the Lord, and the result was that all the idols of the temple broke in pieces; for that he was seized by the archon Magnus and tortured, and when he was let go the Christians cared for him and surrounded him with their respect. Two years later he fell asleep in peace. The page adds that in the years of the iconoclasm Christians carried his holy skull to Paphos, where it is kept to this day at the monastery of Chrysorrogiatissa in an elegant mother-of-pearl case; and it wonders, without deciding, whether a church of his name beyond Constantinople, mentioned as a monastery under Justinian I, was his.
