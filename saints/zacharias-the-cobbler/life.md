# Zacharias the Cobbler

The calendar's line for him is bare, and what stands behind it is a story, told of somebody else and naming him only at the end. A man called John, well known in worldly affairs, had left the sweetness of life for a humble and monastic way, and had the habit of keeping vigil all night in the churches of the Lord. One night he went to the great church of Holy Wisdom in Constantinople, and finding the doors locked sat down on a seat nearby, tired, reading his office in a faltering voice.

Then he saw a brightness of light coming from outside, and a reverent man following that light. When the man reached the closed gates of Hagia Sophia he bent his knees before them and prayed a long while, then raised his hands and made the sign of the cross over the doors — and at once they opened of themselves, and he went in with the light. He knelt on the floor beneath the painted icon of the Mother of God, went into the middle of the church and prayed with his hands raised, and as he went out through the porch the doors locked themselves again by the same divine working.

John followed him, to learn where such a pearl of God was hidden. The man turned a little off the straight road, went down towards the landing-stage of the Holy Martyr Julian, and knocked at the door of a very small house. His wife lit a candle from the lamp and brought it to him; and he did not lie down on the bed or rest his body in any way, but began to work, for he was a cobbler.

John went in after him, fell at his feet and wet them with tears, begging him not to hide who he was and by what high way of life he worked such wonders. The man said he was a sinner and had no good work in him at all; and only when the old man had wept and adjured him by the name of God did he rise from his stool, make a prostration to him, and speak. He had lived, he said, in sins and in the pleasures of the body; then, taking the fear of the judgement into his mind, he had taken a wife as John could see — and had not defiled the purity of the body, for the two of them kept virginity together in agreement and hid it, saying that she was barren.

Hearing the story and awed by that pure and blessed life, John praised the ever-remembered man — for Zacharias was his name — blessed him and went out of his house in joy, thanking God for the great wonders he had seen. And the blessed Zacharias, who was truly without pride, wanting to flee the deceit of worldly vanity, left his house and fled, and made himself entirely unknown to everyone. That is the last the account says of him: no year, no parentage, and no end.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 17 November, and names the man who told his story: [John](/saints/john-companion-of-zacharias). The two venerable men, saint.gr says, are mentioned in a certain narrative, in which John saw Zacharias the cobbler — a σκυτοτόμος, a maker of sandals and stitcher of leather, something between a shoemaker and a worker of leather goods — coming out of the church of Hagia Sophia accompanied by divine light. He followed him to his house and learned that although he was married he lived in virginity and chastity, that of what he earned by his craft he gave half to the poor, and that he had lived a life dear to God; the page puts the story in the seventh century.

Nikodemos the Hagiorite's synaxarion, which the page quotes, gives Zacharias's own words: I work at the craft of shoes, and whatever wage I get from it I divide into two equal parts; the first and chief part I dedicate to Christ, giving it to the poor who are Christ's brothers, and the other I spend on our own needs. Living so, he says, I picture every day the dread Judge who is to come, and remember the examination the tax-collecting demons have to make of me. John heard it, was amazed, praised him, and went out rejoicing; and Zacharias, truly without pride and wishing to escape the bait of human glory, left that house of his and fled, and became completely unknown to everyone.
