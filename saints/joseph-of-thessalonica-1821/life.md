# Joseph of Dimitsana

saint.gr says that Joseph came from Dimitsana in the Peloponnese and was metropolitan of Thessalonica in the hard years of the Turkish rule, and especially in the years before the Revolution broke out; that at its beginning he was seized by the Turks and put to death; and that he is therefore reckoned a national martyr. The page adds, in its own words, that there is no official act of the Ecumenical Patriarchate proclaiming his sanctity — such a thing would naturally have drawn sharp reactions from the Turkish government — but that it was judged right to include him in the hagiology of Thessalonica, since he is established in the conscience of the faithful not only as a national martyr but as a hieromartyr.

His person, the page says, has to be reassembled out of scattered notices, since no full biography of him exists, and most of what there is comes from the two periods in which he sat as a synodal bishop at the Patriarchate of Constantinople, where his signature stands on many important documents. He was of the Antonopoulos family of Dimitsana, which gave much to the struggle of 1821; the memoirs of Kanellos Deligiannis name his brother Athanasios Antonopoulos, the foremost man of the town, as a builder of its powder mills, and call Joseph the metropolitan of Thessalonica who was killed by the sultan at Constantinople along with the late Patriarch Gregory, and those of Derkoi and Ephesus, and other bishops.

He took his first schooling in his birthplace, probably, and where he continued it is unknown — Smyrna, Constantinople and the Holy Mountain all drew young men from Dimitsana. The monk Christophoros the Prodromite notes of him that he had a sufficient education, both the outward learning and above all our own. Under Gregory V he appears as archdeacon of the metropolitan of Ephesus, then as Grand Protosyncellus of the Ecumenical Patriarchate, and on 20 August 1787 he was elected metropolitan of Drama. He was distinguished for his learning, his piety and his industry, contributed money to the printing of several books, and it was he who urged Nikodemos the Hagiorite to compile the Synaxarist and promised to pay for the edition; after Nikodemos's death in 1819 he kept the promise, wishing only that his gift should stay secret.

From 1819 to 1821 he sat again in the Patriarchal Synod, now as metropolitan of Thessalonica, and his signature stands on the encyclical of 1820 and on the letter of excommunication of the leaders of the liberation struggle in 1821 — acts which, the page says, historical research has shown are not to be taken as treachery but read together with the whole climate of the period and with the hard role the Patriarchate had taken on as protector of the Christian population.

After the rising broke out in the Danubian lands the patriarch was ordered by firman to send certain of the foremost bishops to the Porte. The arrests were made by stages, and after 10 April [Gregory of Derkoi](/saints/gregory-of-derkoi), Ioannikios of Tirnovo, [Dorotheos of Adrianople](/saints/dorotheos-of-adrianople) and Joseph of Thessalonica were imprisoned in the Bostancıbaşı's oven. On 27 May, when the sultan learned of the burning of the Turkish two-decker off Lesbos, he ordered the prisoners killed in reprisal.

On 3 June the victims were taken with their executioner to the European shore of the Bosphorus. Ioannikios of Tirnovo was hanged first, at Arnavutköy, then the man of Adrianople at Mega Revma, third Joseph at Neochori, and last the man of Derkoi at Therapeia. After Joseph's death his property was confiscated, and the school of his native town lost the support it had had from him.
