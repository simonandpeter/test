# John of the Ladder

Which country and which city brought forth and raised this brave struggler, before his desert labours, the monk Daniel who wrote his life says plainly that he cannot tell. What place holds him now, and with what sweetness of undying food it feeds him, is certain: he is in that country of which the Apostle Paul cried, «our citizenship is in heaven», where he receives the rewards worthy of his sweat, and for his pains the honour that has no pain in it.

At sixteen years of age in the body, and a thousand years of age in the sharpness of his understanding, he offered himself to God as a blameless and acceptable sacrifice: he raised his body to Mount Sinai and his soul to heaven. Seeing with his mind the God who cannot be seen, and made a stranger to the world, he loved from the first the meekness that is adorned with humility, cutting off all boldness and pride of speech and all slander.

His elder was Martyrius, who tonsured him. Once Martyrius came with his disciple to the great Athanasius of Sinai, who looked at John and asked him where he had this disciple from and who had tonsured him, and then said in wonder: «Father Martyrius, you have tonsured the abbot of Mount Sinai.» Another time Martyrius took him to the great elder John the Sabbaite, in the desert of Goudda; and the elder rose, took water, and washed not Martyrius's feet but John's, and kissed his hand. When Stephen, his own disciple, asked why he had washed the disciple's feet and not the teacher's, the elder answered: «Believe me, son, I do not know who that young monk is; I received the abbot of Mount Sinai, and it was his feet I washed.»

When his father in God had been sent ahead to the King on high, John went out to a place of stillness in Sinai, about five stadia from the church, called Tholas; and there he spent some forty years from the beginning of his monastic life without slackening, always burning with the desire of the divine love. By that hermit's life, and by the rarity with which he saw a human face, he quenched the furnace of the flesh and turned it to ashes; the passion of anger had already been killed in him by the weapon of obedience. Long before he slept he would pray and compose books, and so he composed the book called the Ladder, for which he was afterwards called the Ladder-writer; for writing books was how he drove off his sloth.

A monk named Moses, envying his manner of life, begged to be received as his disciple, and, pressed by honoured elders who spoke for him, John took him. One day he sent Moses to bring earth from a certain place for the garden; and at noon, in the burning heat — for it was the month of August — Moses grew tired, went into the shade under a great rock and fell asleep there. Sitting in his cell at prayer, and in a light sleep, John saw a man of holy dignity who reproached him: «So, John, you sleep without a care? Moses is in danger!» — and the Lord, not willing to grieve His servants, kept Moses from a sudden death and John from grief.

Afterwards, when all had marvelled at him in every rectitude, they set him as a new Moses, against his will, over the common life, putting that shining lamp on the first lampstand; and receiving unwillingly the abbacy of Mount Sinai he drew nearer in spirit to the mountain of God. After he had taken the abbacy — that is, after forty years of monastic life, as Synchronus writes — six hundred strangers once came to the monastery, and sitting at the meal they saw a young man clothed in a Jewish shroud who went everywhere and gave orders with authority to the stewards and the cellarers, the cooks and the servers. When they looked for him and could not find him, John said: «Leave off looking for him: Moses the prophet and lawgiver has served in his own place.»

One year there was no rain in those parts and a great drought, and the people of the cities round about gathered and came to him, begging him to ask rain of God by his prayers; and when he had prayed a great rain came down at once, and watered the dry ground and made it bear fruit. At the end of his temporal life he was in no way unlike Moses, save in this: that John entered with his soul into the Jerusalem above, while of Moses's body it is not known where it was laid. It was John, abbot of Raithu, who had asked him, and at whose asking he came down from Mount Sinai, like a new seer of God, and showed them his own tablets written by God — the words that are called the Ladder.

His brother, Abba George, wept that he was being sent on ahead to God and left behind; and John answered him: «Do not weep and do not grieve, for if I find boldness before God I shall not wait for you to finish this year here, but shall ask the Lord and take you to myself.» And so it was: after the blessed end of John, Abba George went to God in the tenth month. A note at the end of the life adds that Daniel the monk covers the saint's birth and city, and that some say he was the son of Xenophon and his brother George the Arsealite, called Arcadius from birth, who changed his name in the monastic order as John did not — and it sends the reader to the twenty-sixth day of January.
