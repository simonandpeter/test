# Maria (Skobtsova)

saint.gr keeps her among the Russian saints who ended their lives in France, a synaxis the Ecumenical Patriarchate appointed for 20 July. She was born in 1891 at Riga in Latvia and her name before her marriage was Elizaveta Pilenko. Her parents were faithful and pious people who passed on to her from childhood the essentials of the faith; but at fourteen her father died, and the child Elizaveta felt the injustice of that death, and with it the absence of a God whom she now reckoned not to exist.

In 1906 she and her widowed mother moved to Saint Petersburg, where she fell in with the circles of the intellectuals, in particular with the poet Alexander Blok, and, like so many of her contemporaries, became a member of left-wing political organisations — to fight the injustice in the world, as she put it later. In 1910 she married Dmitri Kuzmin-Karavyev, a Bolshevik, in a marriage made less of love than of sympathy. Though she still reckoned herself an atheist, her old interest in Christ revived, more as a heroic figure than otherwise, and alongside her poetry — two collections were already published — she decided to take up the study of theology.

She enrolled in the theological academy of the Alexander Nevsky monastery; her marriage broke up in 1913, and in the October of that year her first child was born. Studying, and searching out her own faith, she concluded that Christian asceticism does not consist in the torment of the body but in concern for the needs of other people and for the bettering of their conditions. The Bolshevik power then hunted her; a friendship with Lenin's wife saved her from execution once, and when, elected mayor of the little Black Sea town of Anapa in the civil war, she was taken by the Whites, Daniel Skobtsov, her judge, saved her again. She came to love him, and they married.

With her family she emigrated, and reached Paris in 1923 after many adventures. In 1926 she lost her daughter to meningitis — the death of someone you love, she wrote, throws wide the gates of eternity, and the whole of natural existence loses its firmness and its coherence. She gave herself more and more to study and to social work among the destitute Russian emigrants of the French towns and villages, and she liked to help the afflicted because she saw in them the true face of Christ. Metropolitan Eulogius, knowing that work, proposed that she become a nun; her husband consented to a church divorce in 1932, and a few weeks later she was tonsured in the chapel of the Theological Institute of Saint Sergius in Paris and given the name Maria.

Monasticism within the world, as she called it, was her mission from then on. She made a small house that became a refuge for all the harried of Paris and at the same time a meeting place of considerable people; by 1937 as many as a hundred and twenty meals a day were served to the poor there, and all Paris came to know the beggar-nun who smoked. In 1935 she founded Orthodox Action, whose donors in France and abroad paid for hostels, refuges and schools, for help to the unemployed and the old, and for books. She had not withdrawn from the world bodily, the page says, because the world needed her; she had withdrawn from its spirit.

Paris fell to the Nazis on 14 June 1940, and it was the turn of the Jews to receive her love: she supplied forged certificates of baptism to many who faced certain destruction, being sure that Christ himself would have done the same. After she had sheltered dozens of Jews in her house and helped many children through the occupation, the Nazis arrested her and sent her to the camp of Ravensbrück, where she lived two years, and that, the page says, thanks to her ascetic life. Death found her in March 1945, on Great Friday. Why she died is disputed: some lay it to the hardships of the camp, others say she was among those chosen to be executed, and there are testimonies that she took the place of another, Jewish prisoner. Her son [Yuri](/saints/yuri-skobtsov) died in a camp as she did.
