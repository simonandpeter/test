# Stephen Dounale

saint.gr's calendar for 17 December keeps the venerable Stephen the Confessor, who was called Dounale before, and sets his homeland a long way west: near Gadeira in Spain, it says, on an island that was called Nivertis, or Berroe according to others. He brought up his children in piety and made them useful and well-doing members of society; and afterwards he gave himself over wholly to God, having left every care of this life behind.

He went as a pilgrim to Rome and gave much money to the Christian foundations there, and did the same again when he went on to Constantinople in the year 919 — the one year the page gives him. From there he went to the Holy Places, where he shared out the last of his money and became a monk with the name Stephen, from Dounale.

He was seized by the Saracens and hard tortured, but managed to escape into Egypt with two priests; and there too he preached the Gospel with zeal. He was denounced to the Amir, imprisoned and put to frightful tortures, and gravely wounded he died glorifying God. The page gives no year for that end, names neither the Amir nor the two priests, lists no relic and prints no hymn for him.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same menologion day, under the name he bore before his tonsure — „Преподобни Данил (Донале)“ — and fills in most of what the Greek page left out. He was a great lord and the governor of the island of Niverta, near Cadiz in Spain. Having come to know all the vanity of this world, he left both his glory and his wealth behind, and came to Rome, where he was made a monk.

From Rome he went on to Constantinople, where he conversed with the emperors, Constantine and Romanus Porphyrogennetos; and from there he set out for Jerusalem. At Jerusalem he received the great schema from the patriarch Christodulus, who gave him the name Stephen. Harried by the Saracens, who pressed him to shave off his beard, he withdrew into Egypt, where he suffered many torments, and death, for the name of Jesus. He passed over into the Kingdom of Christ at the end of the tenth century, the book says — a date which that same book's emperors, and the 919 the Greek page gives for his coming to Constantinople, both sit oddly against.
