# Damian of Rome

Damian and his brother [Cosmas](/saints/cosmas-of-rome) were born of one father and one mother in old Rome and brought up in the Christian faith. They learned the craft of medicine, and whatever suffering people or beasts they laid their hands upon were at once made perfectly whole, for the grace of the one God helped them in everything.

They took no payment for their healing and were called the physicians without fee; the only payment they asked was that those they healed should believe in Christ. They sold the property they had inherited from their forefathers and shared it out among the poor and the needy, fed the hungry and clothed the naked, and going through the towns and villages about Rome they healed the sick and turned many from idol-worship to Christ.

They lived apart in a village within the borders of Rome, where their parents' property lay. Slandered to the emperor Carinus by certain of their own servants, they refused to hide when the faithful begged them to, and went out to the soldiers of their own will. At Rome the emperor was healed by them and gave thanks to God before all the people, honoured the two physicians and let them go in peace; and they returned to their village and to their old round of the towns and villages.

The envious one, unable to destroy them by his first device, found another. A famous physician of those parts, the man from whom they had first learned their craft, could not bear their good name. He called them to him deceitfully and led them far up a mountain as though to gather herbs, setting each to gather apart; then he fell upon the one and killed him with stones, and killed the other in the same way, and hid the two bodies by a watercourse. So the two unmercenaries received the martyr's crown. The doxologia page names the emperor Carinus and gives no year; saint.gr's heading does give one, 287, and it is recorded here.

The Greek calendar keeps the two unmercenaries on the same 1 July, and saint.gr tells their story in a shorter Greek form. They were brothers and physicians by trade under the Roman emperor Carinus, healing all who had need and taking no money, asking only that the healed should believe in Christ. Well-wishers denounced them to the emperor as working their cures by magic; rather than let others be taken in their place they came before him of their own accord.

When Carinus was interrogating them his face was twisted round towards his own back. They healed it at once by prayer to Christ, everyone standing there believed, and the emperor sent them home to their kinsfolk with great honours. Long afterwards their own teacher in medicine, envious of their fame, took them up a mountain as though to gather herbs and killed them there with stones. Their page's third line sets the day beside the stones: «Πρώτῃ Ἰουλίοιο λίθοισιν Ἀνάργυροι ἤθλουν» — on the first of July the Unmercenaries contended by stones.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two brothers on the Julian 1 July, the same menologion day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep them on, in one entry under one heading, and it gives a year three years off the Greek page’s: they suffered honourably for the faith of Christ, it says, in the year 284, where saint.gr’s heading says 287. The folder’s date is widened to hold both books rather than deciding between them. It has the brothers Romans by birth, baptised as children and brought up in the Christian spirit, healing men and beasts of every sickness and torment by the laying on of hands, asking no reward but that the sick should believe in Christ the Lord, and giving away in alms to the poor and the distressed the great estate they had inherited.

Two things in that entry are new and one of them is a disagreement. The new thing is what the brothers said to the emperor. They did not merely refuse his order to deny Christ and sacrifice to the idols; they counselled him to turn from dead idols himself and own the one true God, telling him that their God is not made but is himself the Maker of all, while his gods are men’s inventions and the work of craftsmen’s hands — and that if there were no craftsmen to make them gods, they would have nobody to bow down to.

The disagreement is how they came before him at all. The other two books have them come of their own will, refusing to hide so that nobody should be taken in their place; this book has the persecutors of Christianity bring them fettered in chains, and after a long interrogation and the wonder worked on the emperor himself, whose grievous illness it does not name, he confessed his own faith in Christ and let the holy brothers go in peace. Both readings are written here and neither is chosen. The end agrees with the other books: a physician who had once been their teacher envied their glory, took them into the mountain under pretext of gathering healing herbs, and stoned them to death.

The Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery keeps the two of them on 1 июля ст. ст. — «Бессребреников Космы и Дамиана , в Риме пострадавших (284)» — on the same menologion day as the three calendars this folder already carries, with the earlier of its two years and with Rome named in the line itself. The day page prints a troparion and a kontakion for the pair, both of which address [Cosmas of Rome](/saints/cosmas-of-rome) and Damian together by name.
