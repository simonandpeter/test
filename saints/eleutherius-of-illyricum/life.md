# Eleutherius of Illyricum

doxologia.ro says that while Aelius Hadrian was reigning at Rome and serving the idols diligently, Eleutherius became known like a shining star. His homeland was the great city of Rome, his parents were well born, distinguished and rich, and his mother was counted worthy of a greater honour still for her faith in Christ, having been taught by the Apostle Paul, who baptised her; her name was [Anthia](/saints/anthia-mother-of-eleutherius). She gave her son the name Eleutherius and brought him up well in the right faith.

When he came of age they gave him to God through the hands of Anicetus, archbishop of Rome, who saw the boy's good habits and put him among the clergy: he made him deacon in the fifteenth year of his age, ordained him priest in the eighteenth, and in the twentieth chose him bishop of Illyricum. Nobody should wonder at so young a bishop, the page says: the archbishop had seen great virtues in him, and he was so wise and so good a speaker that those who fell into conversation with him were drawn to the faith. Many Greeks turned and were baptised, for the sweetness and wisdom of his words moved his hearers to know the truth.

The devil stirred up the emperor Hadrian against the truth, and Hadrian sent a commander named Felix with a harsh order to bring Eleutherius by force to the imperial court. Felix reached Illyricum at the hour when the good shepherd was feeding his rational sheep in the church of the Lord, surrounded the church with soldiers and went in alone, looking about him savagely; but seeing the bishop's bearing and hearing his sweet tongue and most wise teaching, he wondered, and stood and listened carefully to what was said of Christ and of his power, and his sharpness turned to gentleness. Out of a wolf he became a sheep, the life says, and out of a persecutor a disciple.

He fell at the saint's feet and never left him again, and did not think of going back to the man who had sent him; and Eleutherius, wishing to endure to blood for Christ, hurried to go with him to the emperor, so that Felix led him not as a commander leads a prisoner but as a lamb follows the shepherd. When they came to water, Felix was baptised by Eleutherius as the eunuch of Candace was by the Apostle Philip. At Rome, Eleutherius was brought to judgement by the emperor's order and stood without fear.

The emperor looked at him, young and handsome and bright-eyed, and asked why he had left his fathers' faith for a new God who had died, and died a most cruel death. Eleutherius would not answer folly at first, following Christ, who was silent before Pilate; but pressed, he answered that he could not serve unfeeling gods and lifeless idols, and wept for those who worshipped them, who had been honoured by the true God with reason and had made themselves less reasoning than wood and stone. The honours promised him and the torments threatened, he said, he reckoned as children's toys and arrows, for he had renounced the world and been crucified like his teacher Paul, and counted death for Christ a delight.

Hadrian had a brazen bed brought with coals heaped beneath it and the martyr stretched naked upon it, the fire to be fed until he died; and the crowd reproached the emperor for his tyranny, asking why so honoured and wise and well-born a man should perish like a criminal. God lightened his pains, so that he lay as though sprinkled with dew and set upon roses, and when they took him off for dead he stood up alive and unharmed, singing that he would exalt his God and King. A red-hot iron gridiron with boiling oil poured over it went out and cooled under him; a pan filled with wax, pitch and tallow did the same, and a cool wind blew over him.

The eparch of the city, Coremon, a man skilled in many kinds of torture, offered to settle the matter, and had a brazen furnace made with sharp irons inside it. He knew the faith of Christ, having been taught by Felix, but for the sake of passing glory had not left the idols. As the martyr prayed — thanking God, and praying for his tormentors as Stephen prayed for those who stoned him, that God would touch their hearts and make his name known to them — the eparch listened carefully, and the faith he had once received caught fire in him; and he went to the emperor and asked why Eleutherius, who was without fault, should be given to such torments.

Hadrian reminded him of the riches and the office he had been given and accused him of taking gold from the martyr's mother; Coremon answered that the emperor's honour could stay with him for his destruction and his gold be burnt by the fire that waited for him, and that the God whom Eleutherius honoured had made him stronger than fire. He was thrown into the furnace he had prepared, asked Eleutherius to arm him with the same weapon of Christ with which he had armed the commander Felix, was armed with the sign of the Cross, and came out an hour later unhurt; and Hadrian had him beheaded.

Eleutherius was thrown into the same furnace, and the fire went out, the irons cooled and the sharp points broke, and the bystanders cried that great was the God of the Christians. He was starved in prison for many days and fed with angels' food; wild horses were brought to tear him apart, and an angel loosed him out of his tormentors' hands and set him on a mountain near the city, in a waste place where the beasts lived, and there he praised God and lived among lions and bears that fawned on him like sheep.

Hunters found him and told Hadrian, who sent soldiers; the beasts would have torn them if the saint had not held them back and sent them into the desert, and he went off with the soldiers as gladly as to a feast, talking on the road of the Kingdom of God and of the fire prepared for idolaters, so that he made Christians of them and baptised them, about five hundred in all. At Rome the emperor condemned him to the beasts: a savage lioness went at him and then grew quiet, laid her muzzle at his feet and licked his soles, and a male lion, sent for because a lioness was thought too weak, was gentler still.

The tyrant, at a loss, had him beheaded with the sword, and so he gave up his soul into God's hands. His mother Anthia, who had watched his contest with joy, embraced his dead body and kissed it, giving thanks that the blood of her son had been poured out for Christ, and fell dead beside him, killed with the sword by the unbelievers; and the faithful of Illyricum and of Avlona, that is of the martyr's own diocese, took their bodies, anointed them with spices and buried them with honour. doxologia.ro gives no year for any of it, and this folder gives none.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr tells the same life in a different century. It has him born in the second century in Greece — at Rome, according to others — of rich parents, when the emperors were Commodus and Septimius Severus; orphaned of his father and brought up according to the commands of the Gospel by his most pious and charitable mother [Anthia](/saints/anthia-mother-of-eleutherius), who became a Christian, it says, by hearing the preaching of disciples of the Apostle Paul. Anthia's burning wish was to visit Rome, whose soil had been dyed with the blood of the Apostles Peter and Paul, and at last she went, and took her young son with her.

[Anicetus](/saints/anicetus-of-rome), the bishop of Rome, seeing Eleutherius and valuing his great intelligence, his warm faith and his pure character, took him under his protection. At fifteen Anicetus ordained him deacon, three years later priest, and at twenty, by the common vote of clergy and people, he became bishop of Illyricum — today's Albania, the page says — with his seat at Aulona. To the question how he could be ordained so young, saint.gr gives Nicodemus the Hagiorite's own footnote: that nobody should wonder at it, because the saint lived before the canons of the Sixth Council and of the local council of Neocaesarea, which set twenty-five for a deacon, thirty for a presbyter and over thirty for a bishop.

His ordination, the page says one of his biographers wrote, was by God's dispensation, for the sake of the great virtues and the wisdom with which he drew idolaters to Christ; and the sweetness of his word, confirmed by his many wonders, brought those in error to embrace the Christian truth. His fame reached as far as Britain, and its king Lucius wrote to him that he and his people wished to become Christians; Eleutherius answered at once by sending two men trained in the faith, who catechised and baptised Lucius and his people.

When Septimius Severus learnt of his work he ordered his arrest. After many tortures the idolaters brought Eleutherius into the arena at Rome, and because the wild beasts would not touch him he was beheaded, and his mother with him; and so, the page says, he passed into the freedom of the glory of the children of God. His synaxis is kept at his martyrion near the Xerolophos. He is held the helper of pregnant women, and gives them «καλή λευτεριά», a good deliverance, which one of the day's stichera says of him too. The same day's calendar keeps [Coremmon](/saints/coremmon-the-eparch) the eparch of Rome and [the two executioners](/saints/the-two-executioners), who believed from his wonders and were beheaded, and a second and different [Eleutherius](/saints/eleutherius-the-cubicularius), the cubicularius of Constantinople.
