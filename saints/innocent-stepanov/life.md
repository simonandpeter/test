# Innocent (Stepanov)

Innocent — Jacob Firsovich Stepanov in the world, a nobleman of the Stary
Oskol district of Kursk province — was born in 1825 and came to the Glinsk
hermitage in 1845, where he was under the guidance of the famous Glinsk
elders. His brilliant gifts, quiet temper and zeal for piety soon drew the
notice of the monastery’s authorities, and he was made its clerk and
librarian, and as clerk and attorney was the right hand of three superiors
— Eustratius, Auxentius and Joasaph — learning thoroughly the many and
various duties of the abbot of a great common-life house; he had a
remarkable memory, and what he had once read with attention he could
quote by the page ten years later. In 1862 the abbot’s staff was rightly
given to him, and his first care was the building of the monastery’s
churches and houses — the cathedral, the two-storey stone infirmary and
many others rose in his time. In the mornings he prayed long, that in all
the day’s labours he might not for a moment lose devotion to God’s will,
the love God commands or the wisdom the Spirit gives, and might take news
and decisions as befits a Christian, a monk and a pastor: when you have
prayed and peace has settled in your soul, he taught, the Lord’s grace
guards you from breaking that peace; a man stirred by the enemy’s
temptation comes meaning to voice his displeasure or some remark not
according to God, but seeing you in a spirit of peace and meekness he
turns from a wolf into a quiet sheep and says what he meant in another
tone, or holds his peace. Prayer was a real necessity to him; he would
sooner forget food than prayer. Great was his care for the great family of
the Glinsk brotherhood, which had not only to be fed but kept in peace,
love and the pleasing of God, and if possible presented worthy of God’s
mercy at the judgement; and very many came to him for counsel, help and
comfort — he heard them, supported them, answered, stood in prayer for the
suffering and gave alms, until sometimes he was exhausted to the point
where his tongue could no longer speak, and stopped, and seeing the crowd
still thirsting for his blessing began again after a few minutes, grieving
that he could not satisfy them all. In the evenings, having sent his
visitors away, he sometimes kept back one of the brethren to talk of
prayer — one who by his inner state and zeal could take up the unceasing
inner watch against alien thoughts — and his grace-filled speech flowed
unstoppably past midnight; when there was no such talk he prayed and kept
vigil over the sleeping brethren, and the gatekeeper who came at midnight
for a blessing to wake the waker always found him dressed and awake; then
he went to the forest and in the night’s quiet kept vigil for himself,
strengthening himself by prayer for the day’s labour — his soul thirsted
for solitude and could find it nowhere but in the forest, and only at
night.

Beside the word of spiritual wisdom he used widely the gift of foresight
God had given him, the manifestation of the Spirit for profit: a
merchant’s wife told how at first sight he told her her whole life in the
smallest detail, better than she could herself, reminding her of things
long forgotten; hardly a young man wishing to marry or a bride with a
suitor decided on the marriage without the Glinsk superior’s blessing, and
those he blessed lived happily and those he did not repented of not
heeding him — a lady he advised to wait a year before her daughter’s
marriage did not, and the daughter died in terrible torment in her first
pregnancy. Not for nothing was he called tender-hearted, child-loving,
compassionate, merciful: he gave alms without counting, openly and in
secret, and no one went away without help, sometimes with large sums he
told no one of lest the brethren judge him too generous, and when there was
nothing to give he gave his own under-cassock, boots or shirt; out of the
same compassion he helped the sick, healing chiefly by anointing with oil
from the lamp before the wonder-working Glinsk icon, as almost every monk
of his time knew. His cell-attendant Iliodor told of two women who came,
one with a girl in her arms who had seen for a year and for two years had
not opened her eyes, moaned constantly and nodded her head like a
paralytic, and asked his prayers: I can do nothing, he said, but I will
just anoint her eyes with oil from the Queen of heaven’s lamp, and you go
to the skete and pray to the Mother of God, then come back to me — and
blessed the girl and anointed her eyes crosswise, and that minute she
stopped nodding her head, and after dinner they came back and the girl was
wholly well, looking about her gaily and smiling; to the mother’s thanks
he said, that is the work of the mercy of the Mother of God. To those with
bad legs he unwound the leg-cloths himself — they came in bast shoes —
rubbed the legs with ointment, helped them shoe themselves again and said,
now go with God; he dressed dreadful festering wounds without a trace of
squeamishness, on people not every layman would go near. Healing others,
he took no medicine himself, and with his weak constitution was almost
always ill, bearing it cheerfully and with thanks to the Lord, seeing in
the body’s sickness the soul’s health; and for the last eight or ten years
he took on a special labour, never to open the shutters of his bedroom,
living there by lamplight even in the day. Father Innocent hid his
labours, one Glinsk monk testified; he was a great elder, though he was
taken for an ordinary one. On 21 July 1888, after the wonder-working icon
was carried out to Glukhov, he fell into his last illness and suffered
terribly without showing it to anyone; a month before his death he received
a benefactress of the monastery, who stopped in the doorway, threw up her
hands and said, father, I have come to you! — her face strangely changed —
and told the guestmaster afterwards that as she came in she had seen a
golden crown round the abbot’s head and his face like an angel’s, and had
trembled with fear; taking leave she began to tell him of her vision, and
he cut her short with a smile: pray, and you may receive the same. On the
evening of Saturday 17 September 1888, as they sang at the vigil, Lord,
now lettest Thou Thy servant depart, he died quietly and blessedly in his
sixty-fourth year, anointed, confessed and communicated; for four days his
body did not change, stiffen, decay or smell, and the people streamed to
his bier, each hurrying to pay him the last honour. The life is from the
Glinsk Paterikon of the schema-archimandrite John (Maslov); the Council of
Bishops of 2017 entered him in the calendar of the Russian Church, which
keeps him on 9 September of the old style, 22 September, in the synaxis of
the Glinsk fathers.
