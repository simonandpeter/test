# Calliopius

A woman of Perga in Pamphylia named Theoclia, right-believing and God-fearing with her whole household, gave much alms and lived honourably. She was married to an honourable man of the rank of senator and patrician, and for many years bore no children, being barren; then she conceived, having asked God in prayer to loose her barrenness. While she carried the child her husband died and left her great wealth; and when the son was born she named him Calliopius, brought him up in letters, and took pains to teach him the fear of God and all the wisdom of the divine Scriptures. In those days most people were bowing and sacrificing to the unclean idols, and Calliopius gave his time to prayer and fasting.

When word was brought to the judge that the young man was a Christian, his mother gave him much gold, clothing and servants and sent him away by ship to another country to escape the heathen court. He sailed to Pempropolis, a city of Cilicia, where the heathen governor Maximus was making sacrifices and holding dances and games in honour of his idols. Seeing it, the young man wondered, and asked some of them what the dancing was for; and they said that it was the feast of their gods, and told him to come and share their banquet.

He answered that he was a Christian and kept what belongs to his Christ, and that a man who glorifies Christ cannot enter into the unclean sacrifices of the heathen gods. The word came to Maximus, who was filled with anger and had the young man seized and set before his judgement seat. Asked his name, he said: I am a Christian, and I am called Calliopius. The governor asked how, with the great banquet of the gods' feast in hand, he alone stood in his delusion and would not keep the feast with them; and Calliopius answered that it was they who walked in delusion and in darkness, having left the true God.

The governor, learning from such answers that the martyr's mind was unchangeable and his greatness of soul unmoved, ordered him crucified like Christ. The day was Great Thursday, on which the remembrance of the Supper of the holy Mysteries of Christ is kept. Theoclia, seeing her son led to the place of crucifixion, gave the soldiers five gold pieces and begged them not to crucify Calliopius as Christ was crucified, but head downward — and she did it out of humility, counting it unworthy that her son should be made like Christ his Lord in his crucifixion.

So Calliopius was crucified head downward for Christ on the day of the saving Supper; and on Great Friday, the day of the voluntary Passion of Christ, at the third hour, he gave up his spirit into the hands of God. A divine voice came to him then from heaven and said: come up, martyr of Christ, fellow-heir with the saints. When they had taken his dead body down from the cross, his blessed mother took her son's neck in her arms, glorifying Christ, and gave up her own spirit in that moment. The brethren came and took both bodies and buried them together in an honourable place.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and tells the same story with a few things the Romanian life has not got. saint.gr names his home as Perge in Pamphylia and sets him in the years of Diocletian; it says he was left fatherless very young and that his virtuous mother Theokleia raised him and fed him on the undying words of the Christian faith, so that when the persecutions came the young man was not afraid but went about putting heart into the faint-hearted and comforting them. It gives the governor who came into that country the name Maximus.

When Maximus began to torture and to kill many Christians, Calliopius came before him of his own accord, with the boldness and the plain speech the page says marked him, to warn him that he would answer to God for the crimes he was committing against faithful men. The governor, beside himself, had him seized, tortured and shut in prison; and when he was brought out again some days later and confessed his faith in a great voice in spite of the hardships and the torments, the governor ordered his death. He was crucified, the page says, and by coincidence it fell on Great Friday, and so he received the unfading crown of martyrdom; and his mother Theokleia, by the tradition, embraced her son's body and breathed her last on the spot.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same day and tells the story again with some things of its own. The only son of a senator of Perga in Pamphylia, wept for from God; his devout mother Theoclia taught him the honouring of God and a pure life in his early youth; and he was still a youth when the terrible persecution of the Christians arose under the emperor Maximian. To save him from death his mother put him on a ship, gave him money enough and sent him away to the city of Pompeiopolis — the name this page gives where the Romanian life has Pempropolis in Cilicia. But it was arranged otherwise by the providence of God: landing there, Calliopius fell into the middle of a stormy festival of the heathen, and when he would not take part in that foolish feast for all the pressing of the maddened crowd, he was dragged before the commander Maximus and confessed to him that he was a Christian.

The commander gave orders, and they beat him with leaden staves and roasted him on fire, and at last threw him into prison wounded all over. Hearing of her son's torture, Theoclia gave away her whole property to the poor and the needy and hurried to the prison with a little money; and going in, she bowed down before her son and bound up his wounds. Then the commander gave his final sentence, that Calliopius be crucified. In the mother's heart joy mingled with pain, and when they led her son out to the place of execution she slipped five gold pieces to the executioners, that they should crucify him not as the Lord was crucified but head downward — and she did it out of humility before the Lord. So on Great Thursday Calliopius was crucified upside down, and his mother stood under the cross giving thanks to God; and when they brought him down dead the next day, she fell upon him and breathed her last herself. So both of them went out before the throne of the King of glory, and the Prologue dates their honourable suffering to the year 304 — the only year any source read for this folder gives it. Theoclia is named here as the entry names her and is identified with nobody.
