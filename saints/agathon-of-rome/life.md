# Agathon of Rome

Agathon — our venerable father and a worker of miracles, as doxologia's life opens — was of Italy, the son of Christian parents, devout and of good honour, who took pains to teach him the whole of the Scripture that is inspired of God and profitable. He profited by it so much, and was so humbled by it, that when his parents died he gathered up the whole of their wealth, called the poor together once, and shared it out among them to the last of it; and then he went off to a monastery and became a monk.

Clothed in the angelic habit, he served God day and night and made prayers for the world; and he laboured so hard at virtue — and virtue does not stay hidden — that he came to be pope of Rome. Having adorned that dignity well, he departed in peace to the Lord.

That is the whole of the life the page prints. It gives no year and no century, names no council and no city but Rome, and says nothing of how he governed; and the page carries no troparion.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and adds a Sicilian birth and a council. saint.gr says Agathon came from Palermo in Sicily and was watered from an early age with the streams of piety, and that he was the seventy-ninth pope of Rome. He became pope in 678 and took an active part in the Sixth Ecumenical Council, sending three representatives to it — the presbyters Theodore and Sergius and the deacon John. That council met at Constantinople in 680, the page says, to condemn the heresy of the Monophysites, and its decisions rested partly on the warmth with which pope Agathon met the question.

He anathematised, the page goes on, everyone who had shown a monophysite mind in the past, among them his own predecessor — whom it calls pope «Ονούφριο τον Α'», of 625 to 638 — and it draws its own conclusion from that: «Έτσι στον Αγάθωνα χρεωστάμε ένα από τα αποτελεσματικότερα βέλη κατά της αξίωσης περί αλάθητου των Πάπων», so to Agathon we owe one of the most effective arrows against the claim of papal infallibility. He fell asleep in holiness in 682. The page prints a couplet on his steering the rudders of Rome and backing his stern towards the harbours of the mind, and no hymn.
