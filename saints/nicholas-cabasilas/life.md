# Nicholas Cabasilas

Nicholas was born at Thessalonica about 1322. Through his father he came of the Chamaetos family, but he took later the surname of his mother, Cabasilas, which was old and well known. From his youth he was taught in the spiritual life by Dorotheos Vlatis, one of the best known spiritual fathers of the city and a close disciple of [Gregory Palamas](/saints/gregory-palamas), and he kept company with the devout laymen of Thessalonica who practised the Prayer of Jesus under the guidance of Isidore Bucheiras, afterwards patriarch.

His first letters and philosophy he had from his uncle Nilus Cabasilas, a theologian known for his treatises against the Latins and for two years bishop of Thessalonica; because the uncle's name in the world was also Nicholas, the two have sometimes been confused and the saint taken for an archbishop, which doxologia's life says plainly he was not. He went on to the school of philosophy at Constantinople, took there a high literary culture, and admired classical antiquity enough to stand with the humanists, without departing from the teaching of the Church.

While he was in the capital the dispute between Gregory Palamas and Barlaam over whether a man may be deified by the uncreated energies of grace woke him to the last end of the Christian life; but for the time he was more taken up with the social and political troubles of his age. After the death of Andronicus III in 1341 the empire was torn by civil war between the party of John V Palaiologos and that of John Cantacuzene, and at Thessalonica the Zealots rose against the imperial power and against the nobles.

Nicholas, then in the city, took upon himself to negotiate between the insurgents and Cantacuzene. In 1345 he was sent to Beroea as ambassador to Manuel, Cantacuzene's son and representative, and obtained favourable terms of surrender for the rebels; but on his return Andrew Palaiologos opposed the agreement, stirred up the Zealots and the outer quarters of the city, and the fortress where the notables had taken refuge was stormed. In the massacres that followed Nicholas escaped by the narrowest margin, hiding in a well.

He stayed at Thessalonica until 1347, unmolested despite his sympathy for Cantacuzene, and, turning over in his mind the causes of the civil war, wrote several treatises against usury and against inequality. When Cantacuzene came to the throne as John VI he brought Nicholas to Constantinople and made him his counsellor in every important affair of state. The emperor called him and his childhood friend Demetrios Kydones men risen to the crest of profane wisdom, philosophers in action who had chosen a chaste life, and for a time thought of retiring with them to the monastery of the Mangana.

Through all this Nicholas wrote steadily while taking an active part in public life. In September 1347 he was in the company that escorted Gregory Palamas, newly elected archbishop of Thessalonica, to his see; the people refused their shepherd, and the two withdrew to Mount Athos and lived a year in stillness and prayer. In 1349 he went back to Thessalonica, where after the Zealot revolt was put down and the parties reconciled Gregory was enthroned at last.

At the council of 1351 which condemned Akindynos and proclaimed hesychasm the doctrine of the Church, Cabasilas took the Palamite side openly, and afterwards favoured the project of a council of union with the Latin Church, though without doctrinal compromise; his friend Kydones went the other way, against the hesychasts and towards submission to the Latin theses. When a new civil war broke out in 1353 and the patriarch was deposed, Nicholas was named as a possible successor, but Philotheos was chosen instead.

The year after, he greeted in a brilliant oration the crowning of Matthew Cantacuzene as co-emperor; but John Palaiologos soon seized power with Genoese mercenaries, Cantacuzene abdicated and became a monk under the name Joasaph, Philotheos was deposed and exiled, and Callistus was recalled to the patriarchal throne. From then on Nicholas withdrew from public affairs to meditate on the mystery of Christ lived in the Church. Whether he remained a layman hesychast to the end or became a monk is not known, though some passages of his works suggest it.

He spent that long retirement mostly at Constantinople, apart from two years at Thessalonica, frequenting the monasteries of the Mangana, of the Xanthopouloi and of Stoudios, and won the reputation of a man come to the highest degree of virtue; great men, the emperor Manuel Palaiologos among them, asked his counsel and held him for their spiritual father. He avoided the troubles of the world and kept quiet in order to compose his two great treatises, the Commentary on the Divine Liturgy and The Life in Christ, two masterpieces of Christian literature. He fell asleep in peace, leaving no account of his last days, between 1391 and 1397.

Taking another road than that of Gregory Palamas, a humanist by training and a hesychast by vocation, he showed that deification and union with Christ are the end of the spiritual life of every Christian and not of monks alone. Doxologia's life calls him the doctor of a sacramental hesychasm, and says that since his commemoration was lately entered in the calendar he holds his deserved place among the Fathers of the Orthodox Church.
