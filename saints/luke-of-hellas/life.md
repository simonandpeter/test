# Luke of Hellas

Luke was born in the parts of Hellas, of parents who had come from the island of Aegina in the Aegean sea; unable to bear the frequent raids of the Agarenes, his father had left his homeland and settled in a village of Hellas in the parts of Phocis, which is now called Salon and is also named Castoria. His parents were named Stephen and Euphrosyne. While he was still a child nothing childish showed in him: he seemed a grown man in what he did, loving silence, quietness and honourable ways.

What is more to be wondered at is that at that age he began to live as an ascetic and to keep abstinence. He tasted no meat, and ate no milk, cheese or eggs, and not even apples and the other fruits that small children love; he lived on barley bread, water and garden greens, and on Wednesdays and Fridays fasted until sunset. And in all this abstinence he had no teacher or guide, but was guided by the divine grace alone and grew day by day in the struggle, fleeing surfeit and rich food and loving fasting and labour. His parents, seeing habits so unusual in children, wondered greatly, and thinking it a child's lightness of mind they tested him: they boiled meat and fish in one pot and set it before him at table, and his father gave the fish to him.

When he was seventeen he set out to become a monk, and the road did not open easily. He was taken for a runaway slave: men asked him whose servant he was and where he was going, and when he said, "I am the servant of Christ, and I am going to the Holy Fathers for their prayers," they did not believe him but thought he was hiding his condition, and beat him and shut him in prison until he should say whose slave he was. After a time some who knew him bore witness for him and he was set free, and going home he bore hard and shaming words from his own household. But he endured the blows and sighed to God day and night that He would bring his purpose to a good end.

He got his desire in this way. Two monks from old Rome came to that village, and seeing them Luke remembered his purpose and his heart was kindled. He asked them where they were going, and they said to Jerusalem, and he begged them to take him with them and make him a partaker of the monastic order. They held back, because he was too young — barely seventeen — and unused to the road, and because they feared his parents would come after them; but he told them he was poor and a stranger and of no account to anyone, and persuaded them, and they left the village secretly with him. At Athens they went into the glorious church of the most pure Virgin Mother of God and rested in the monastery there, and the monks went on to Jerusalem, leaving Luke behind with the abbot: "Here, young man, is the place fitted for your purpose." The abbot asked him again and again where he was from and of what family, and could learn nothing, for the young man hid his country and his house, wishing to be known to no one; and seeing him of good habit, gentle, humble and obedient, he clothed him in the mantle. His mother grieved bitterly for him, calling on God as the witness of her widowhood and her poverty, having lost her husband by death and now the son who had been her comfort.

He came in time to a mountain of his own, and men came to him for profit and troubled his quiet. He sent his disciple Germanus to Corinth, to a skilled and God-inspired man named Theophylact, to ask whether it were better to stay on the mountain of Ioannitsa and bear the trouble of those who came, or to go elsewhere, to a place unknown; and Theophylact sent him the counsel once given from heaven to the great Saint Arsenius: "Flee men and thou shalt be saved." Luke received it with joy, left with his disciple and settled in a desert place near the sea called Kalamion, where he lived by the labour of his hands, digging the ground, sowing seed, grinding it in his hand-mill and making bread. Once some sailors passing by came into his cell, found no one there, took the millstone and carried it to their ship; and when he came back and found it gone he ran to them and asked for it, since he had nothing to grind his wheat with, and they swore they had not taken it. "If you have not taken it, go in peace," he said, "and as for him who took it, let God repay him as He wills" — and he went away, and at once the man who had taken it fell down dead.

He went sometimes to the monastery near the city of Thebes to see the abbot Antony, for it was his custom to go to God-inspired men and talk with them of the profit of the soul. While he was there the son of one of the chief citizens of Thebes fell sick to death, and the man ran and fell at his feet, begging him with tears to come to his house; but Luke refused in his humility, saying that there is one physician of souls and bodies, who can deliver even from death, and that a sinful man can do no such thing. That evening the abbot said to him that they had done wrong in not visiting the sick and comforting the grieved; and Luke answered that to heal the sick is the power of God, and to comfort the grieved belongs only to those who have a word and wisdom in their mouths, and that he was far from the first and wholly without the second, being unlearned — but that if the abbot would go first and be his guide, he would follow. So both of them rose and came late in the evening and found the child more dead than alive; and at the father's and the abbot's entreaty Luke lifted up his hands and prayed, and then went back to the monastery, and at dawn hurried away to his mountain, fleeing the praise of men. When it was day the abbot Antony sent his servant into the city to learn how the child was, and the servant came straight back with a marvel: the boy who that night was at his end had met him riding on a horse, going from the house to the bath to wash.

He fell asleep in the sleep of this temporary death on the seventh day of the month of February, giving up his spirit into the hands of the Lord, and his holy soul went to God into the life that has no death. The next day the monks and the lay people round about gathered in a great assembly, and weeping for the loss of such a light of the world they buried him with honour in the place he had appointed, that is, in the cell in which he had struggled.

Six months later a certain monk, Cosmas the eunuch, from the parts of Paphlagonia, on his way to Italy, was told in a dream by a divine command to go to the place where the venerable Luke was laid and to live beside his grave. Cosmas came, raised the coffin with the incorrupt relics out of the ground, set it above the grave and fenced it about handsomely with boards, and turned the cell into a church, where wonders were worked from the holy relics, for they flowed with sweet-smelling myrrh: the lame were healed, the blind given light, the lepers cleansed and the devils driven out.
