# Theodore the Hadji of Mytilene

Theodore the Hadji was born at Pyrgi Thermis on Lesbos and lived in the
eighteenth century, under the Turks. In the settlement of Kato Pyrgi, by the
provincial road behind an old stone fountain, the fallen walls of a
tower-house still stand, whose stones were carried off under the German
occupation to build houses at Kara-tepe in Mytilene; in that old tower-house
he was born. A Christian by descent, when he came to manhood he married a
pious woman and had two children — the poet Odysseas Elytis told his
biographer that his own family descended from the saint, and set him at the
root of its tree — and a tradition of Pamphila, the next village, has him
living for a time in a tower-house at Vounaraki, perhaps the house he hid in
before the Turks took him, the house of the metropolitan of Drama who came
from Pamphila, which is why a large chapel of his stands at Drama, and why
so many men round Pamphila are called Theodore. He worked as a shoemaker,
with a workshop in the central market of Mytilene at Bas-Fanari, on the
corner of what are now Aigaiou and Ermou streets. Once, in a fit of anger,
he became a Muslim; then he came to himself, felt his sin, and went to the
Holy Mountain, where he lived a good while, received the immaculate
Mysteries and prepared himself for martyrdom. He came back to Mytilene,
presented himself to the judge, confessed Christ boldly and declared the
Muslim religion false; the judge at once sentenced him to the gallows and
handed him to another official, Nazir Omer Agha, who tried by flattery and
promises to change his mind, and against an unconquerable resolve gave him
over to terrible tortures. Led to the place of execution, he first kissed
the rope of the gallows, prayed to God, and so received the crown of
victory, on 30 January 1785 — the record of 1967 says 1784 — at the place in
Mytilene called Parmak Kapı. His body was thrown into the sea and by God’s
providence cast ashore south of the town; the Christians gathered it and, to
keep it from the profane eyes of the Turks, buried it without any mark of a
grave under the floor of the nearby chapel of John the Forerunner at the
place called Mothonas. There it lay a hundred and eighty-three years, until
4 September 1967, when, in the course of the chapel’s restoration and in the
presence of the metropolitan, the deputy prosecutor, the commander of the
gendarmerie of Lesbos, the town engineer and the parish priest of Vareia, a
search made on the testimony of Nicodemus the Hagiorite — whose New
Martyrology, Venice 1799, page 248, says the saint was buried in the church
of the Forerunner outside at Mothonas — and of the writers who follow him
found the bones under the floor to the right of the door, and the committee,
reasoning that Christians never buried their ordinary dead inside a church,
judged them the new martyr’s. They are kept at the church of the Life-giving
Spring at Vareia; on 3 September 1985 the priest of Pyrgi Thermis received a
portion of them and carried it home to the chapel of the saint built there
in 1980. The Greek synaxarion keeps his martyrdom on 30 January and the
finding of his relics on 4 September, which is the day this entry stands
under.
