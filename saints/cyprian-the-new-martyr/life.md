# Cyprian of Kletzos

Cyprian was born in the village of Kletzos in the district of Litza and Agrafa in northern Greece, and showed a calling to the priesthood early. After he had been ordained, Father Cyprian went to the Holy Mountain, where he grew in the spiritual life and became a worthy monk. But before long he was seized by the desire to be a martyr.

He went to the city of Thessalonica and presented himself before the kadi, where he preached Christ boldly and urged all who were present to give up Islam and follow the divine Nazarene. Those who witnessed the scene thought that Cyprian had lost his mind, and the servants there fell on him and threw him out.

His desire to give his life for Christ did not lessen, and it carried him to Constantinople, where he went boldly into the palace and met the grand vizier Kara Mustafa, to whom he preached the Christian faith. The vizier, taken by surprise, answered by inviting Cyprian to accept the religion of Islam, and got nowhere; so Cyprian was passed on to the mufti, to whom he preached the same saving news.

Losing his patience altogether, the grand vizier condemned him to death, which Cyprian received with joy, and he was beheaded in the quarter of the Phanar in Constantinople, the place to which the Ecumenical Patriarchate had moved in the year 1601. So the hieromonk Cyprian of Kletzos in Agrafa gave his life for the love of Jesus Christ in the city of Constantinople on Sunday, 5 July, in the year 1679. The page prints no troparion for him; it cites Nomikos Michael Vaporis, *Mărturisitori ai lui Hristos* (Sophia, Bucharest, 2002), pages 156–157.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 5 July. saint.gr says he was born of Christian parents in the village of Kleisto — also written Kleitsos or Kletzos — in the Agrafa, was brought up as a Christian should be, became a monk and was counted worthy of the priesthood, and went to the Holy Mountain, where he stayed in the cell of Saint George near the monastery of Koutloumousiou and became an example among the fathers. His heart burning for martyrdom, he went to Thessalonica and preached Christ before the judge with astonishing boldness, urging those in the court and the judge himself to deny Mohammed and follow Christ; they took him for a madman and kicked him out of the courtroom. He went on to Constantinople, walked into the palace of his own accord and preached Christ before the Vizier, who tried flattery and, failing, handed him to the Mufti; there too he confessed his love of Christ and was glad to hear the sentence. On 5 July 1679, a Saturday by this page's reckoning, they beheaded him at the Phanar, and his martyrdom was written by Ioannis Karyophyllis.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 5 July, the same menologion day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep him on, and it ends with the same day and the same year the Romanian notice gives: so this man of God suffered for Christ on the fifth of July 1679, and fulfilled his burning desire. It calls his village Klitsos and puts it in Epirus, where the Romanian page has Kletzos in Agrafa — the same village and not the same region, and both stand here with neither chosen. After the death of his good parents he went to the Holy Mountain, was tonsured there and gave himself to ascesis in a cell near the monastery of Koutloumousiou, laying labour upon labour and ascesis upon ascesis until he was renowned and honoured throughout the Mountain.

But he was not satisfied with himself. He was tormented by the thought that he could not be saved otherwise than through martyrdom for Christ. So he left the Holy Mountain and came to Thessalonica, went out before the pasha of the city and called on him to throw off the false faith of Mohammed and receive the true faith of Christ; and the pasha had him beaten and driven out of doors. Unsatisfied with so small a suffering for Christ, he went to Constantinople and wrote a paper to the grand vizier, setting out in it the falsity of Mohammed and the truth of Christ the Lord.

The vizier in his anger sent him to the sheikh-ul-islam, and he, when he had heard everything Cyprian had to say, gave orders that his head should be struck off. Cyprian was glad beyond measure, and went to the place of execution as to a wedding.
