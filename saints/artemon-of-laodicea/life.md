# Artemon of Laodicea

In the reign of Diocletian there was a great persecution of the Christians, and officers were sent through every city and country of the Roman dominion to sacrifice to the gods and to force the Christians to worship the idols, torturing and killing those who would not bend. Into the parts of Cappadocia came a count named Patricius to carry out the emperor's order. Before he arrived, Sisinius, bishop of Laodicea, hearing that the count was near, took his presbyter Artemon and some of the Christians, went by night into the temple of Artemis, broke all the idols there and burned them with fire.

Returning to the Christian church, which stood five stadia from the city, they kept their usual prayers, and the bishop strengthened the faithful: my children, we hear that the Antichrist is coming into the city meaning to kill the Christians; let nothing separate you from the love of Christ, neither fire nor sword nor beasts nor any kind of death, however terrible. When Patricius entered the city he first sacrificed in the temple of Apollo and held an inquiry, setting the imperial threats before the people, and for five days they were busy with questioning and with hunting Christians down.

Going then into the temple of Artemis to make his sacrifice and finding no idols, he asked where they were, and some of the pagans told him that the bishop Sisinius with his priest Artemon and other Christians had broken Artemis and the rest. He mounted at once and rode with armed soldiers towards the Christian church, meaning to shed the blood of the faithful and to cut Sisinius and Artemon in pieces; but when he came within about a stadium of it a great shivering took him and he fell, and such a fever burned in him that they carried him on a litter to a house nearby.

Artemon was afterwards taken and held in prison at Caesarea. A deacon named Philias came to him there and told him that it was a wild beast, speaking with a human voice, that had brought the bishop news of him, and that the bishop had sent to see whether it was true, for he grieved for him. Artemon answered: tell my master the holy bishop that I am taken to torture for Christ and sit in prison, and let him pray for me that I may overcome the cunning devices of this Christ-hating executioner and be counted worthy to share with my Lord Jesus Christ. They kissed one another and parted, the deacon to his own place and Artemon to his chains; and the bishop prayed with tears, asking the God who delivered Daniel from the lions and the Three Children from Nebuchadnezzar's furnace to deliver His servant.

Brought out for questioning, the count called him an old man and asked his name. «Mă numesc Artemon, rob al lui Hristos Dumnezeul meu» — I am called Artemon, the servant of Christ my God. Are you the one who broke the carved image of the great goddess? I am. Patricius mocked him, saying he had taken him for a servant of Artemis because his name was hers, and that she must have spared him for the likeness of the name; Artemon answered that he would overcome the count's tyranny too, by praying to his God, and that a dead and dumb idol had been easy enough to overthrow.

The account is a long one, and the end of it is this. The bishop of Caesarea, learning all that had happened — that many had been baptised by Artemon and that he was teaching the people the word of truth — came to him with the faithful, kissed him, prayed, and marked with a sign the place where the martyr had suffered, and afterwards built a church on it. Of the martyrs Artemon had baptised he ordained some priests and some deacons, and set Vitalis as bishop over the parts of Palestine. Artemon kissed the bishop and the people and went out to the part of Asia appointed him; caught up by an angel on the road, he was carried to the place he had been sent to, and there worked great wonders by the power of God and brought many to Him. Afterwards the unbelievers seized him and cut off his head.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and gives him the fullest page of it. saint.gr sets him in the reign of Diocletian (285–305) as a presbyter of the Church of Laodicea in Syria, already well on in years — sixteen years a reader, twenty-eight a deacon and thirty-three a priest — when the aged bishop Sissinius held the city. Before his martyrdom the two of them went into the idol temple of Laodicea and destroyed the idols there; and when a Count named Patricius came to Laodicea some time later and learned of the saints' daring, he rushed to the church where they were serving to punish them, and was struck at once with divine wrath and fell ill. Though he asked and received his healing from Sissinius, he stayed unrepentant, and having arrested Artemon put him to frightful tortures to force him to sacrifice to the false gods. Failing at last, he ordered a cauldron of pitch and resin heated to burn the saint in it — and then a divine power caught up the Count and threw him into the cauldron instead, where he found a pitiable death.

After that, the page says, the saint made a spring of water rise by his prayer on the spot where he stood, and baptised in it those who had believed in Christ because of his wonderful deliverance; and having worked many wonders he went into Asia Minor, to a place called Balbine, and fell asleep there in the Lord. That is the account of the best-known synaxaria. But an old full Life kept in an eleventh-century Cypriot manuscript carries what the page calls the most ancient traditions about him, and it continues otherwise: in Asia Minor he was ordained bishop, and to escape the human glory his wonders brought him he went to cape Anemourion, where he found no ship but a shining cloud, and a divine power caught him up and carried him strangely across to cape Kormakitis in Cyprus, opposite. There was then a place on the island named Aulon, near the present village of Aulona in the Morphou country, full of unclean spirits; Artemon went there on purpose, drove the demons out by his prayer and made a spring of water rise for the sanctification of souls and bodies. A little later he rested in peace at Aulon, and his body was buried by disciples who had come meanwhile from Laodicea and who wrote down «τὰς περιόδους αὐτοῦ», his journeys. His holy water became a baptistery afterwards, whose water was visible only once a year, after the Liturgy of Great Saturday, when the infants were baptised and great wonders were done, and his church there became a pan-Orthodox pilgrimage famous as far as Constantinople.

Those traditions were barely known until a few years ago, the page says, because the saint's church and shrine were destroyed and because of the island's various historical vicissitudes; and they have lately been verified to a large degree by two pieces of evidence taken together — the finding, on the site of ancient Aulon near the present villages of Aulona and Akaki of Morphou, of the remains of a basilica with a mosaic floor and the base of a small baptistery, dated to the end of the fourth or the beginning of the fifth century; and a single known reference, in a fragment of an early fourteenth-century Cypriot liturgical typikon from the monastery of Stavrovouni, to the relic of Artemon still being «ἐν τῷ Αὐλῶνι τῆς Σολίας, ἤγουν τῆς Κύπρου» and to his being kept on the island on 13 April. The page waits for the findings of the systematic excavation already begun. A piece of his relic survives in an old wooden reliquary shaped like an artophorion at the monastery of Kykkos, now in its museum, among relics of other saints of Cyprus; and he was honoured through the centuries in many parts of the island for his wonders — at Ornithi in the Mesaoria, where a medieval church of his stands whose first phase may be Byzantine, at Aphanteia, where a great religious fair was held until 1974, at Askeia, and at Athienou, Angastina and Anarita, where there are pilgrimage icons of his from the end of the eighteenth and the beginning of the nineteenth century.
