# Elijah (Izmailov)

Elijah was born in 1882 in the village of Khormaly in the Tsivilsk
district of Kazan province — today the Ibresi district of Chuvashia — to
the peasant Ishmael, one of five children; he studied at the parish
school, sang in the church choir from childhood and, grown up and
experienced, came to lead it. Called up in 1916 he came home at the war’s
end; taken with the idea of freedom he became chairman of the volost land
committee in 1918 and served in the volost executive committee until
1920. In 1925, without marrying, he received the priesthood from Herman
(Kokel), bishop of Alatyr, then in exile at Kozya Sloboda near Kazan,
where the ordination took place; the then priest of Khormaly, Krasnov,
had joined the Renovationists, and Bishop Herman, arriving in Chuvashia,
with the Orthodox drove Krasnov out and set the Orthodox priest Elijah
Izmailov over the parish — and the same year Elijah was stripped of his
vote, as a minister of religion. In February 1930 the authorities closed
the church at Khormaly, but the church council, of which he was
secretary, wrote to the All-Russian Central Executive Committee that the
closure was wrong when eighty per cent of the parish were for opening it —
we believing citizens, placing all our hope and full trust in the
representatives of the highest power, most humbly ask whether the
committee might find it possible to permit the opening of the church —
and the church was opened again and he went on serving. On 25 March 1932
he was arrested and put in the prison at Tsivilsk, charged in the case of
the group of the True Orthodox Church led in Chuvashia by the
archimandrite Agathangel (Yetrukhin), whose members did not recognise
Metropolitan Sergius (Stragorodsky) as head of the Russian Church. At the
services, he told his questioners, he had always commemorated
Metropolitan Peter of Krutitsy and all the “true Orthodox” bishops, and at
the insistence of some parishioners did not commemorate Sergius; asked
whether he was a member of a group of sectarians — I am not a member of a
group of sectarians, but I am a member of a group of believers; I believe
in one God; my political conviction is this — whatever power there is in
Russia is a matter of complete indifference to me. He bore himself
bravely at every interrogation and admitted no guilt, and with the others
in the case was sentenced by the special board of the OGPU for the Nizhny
Novgorod region under article 58-10 to three years’ exile in Kazakhstan,
which he served at Zhilaya Kosa in the Guryev region of western
Kazakhstan, returning to his village on 24 March 1935. The church was
closed then, and with the church council he pressed the authorities
insistently to open it, going on with the services at home; in September
1936 Bishop Vladimir (Yudenich) of Cheboksary offered him a vacant
priest’s place at Soldybaevo in the Kozlovka district, which he declined,
believing the church at Khormaly would be opened — building materials had
even been laid in to repair it. Caught in the first wave of the mass
repressions of 1937, he was arrested on 4 August and put in the prison at
Alatyr, and at one that afternoon his first and only interrogation began:
did he understand the resolution read to him charging him, and why had he
not signed it? — the resolution is clear to me, but I refuse to sign it,
since I admit no guilt; it lasted five and a half hours. One of the false
witnesses, a collective farmer, could give no concrete facts of
anti-Soviet agitation but knew of his anti-Soviet activity: the former
priest Izmailov daily organised the church activists for the opening of
the church, and though the church did not work, he did — at the house
where he lived he baptised and buried, and went constantly round the
yards and the neighbouring villages, though he was not the priest of
Khormaly; he organised women — the wife of a Komsomol member had baptised
her child in his house without her husband’s knowledge — and there had
been the organised outburst of women at the general meeting of citizens
on the evening of 31 May, where a speaker from Cheboksary had put the
question of the existence of Christ, and they had openly demanded the
opening of the church. Another said that he had been exiled in 1932 for
anti-Soviet and counter-revolutionary subversion and back only in early
1936, had not stopped but carried on openly, holding services and rites
not in the church but in private houses, going out to neighbouring
villages when called and using his wandering position for anti-Soviet
agitation, setting the individual farmers and collective farmers against
every measure by organising them to come out for the opening of the
church — at a citizens’ meeting that summer on an anti-religious theme he
and his activists had organised the women and others to come out as one
for the opening of the church, and they had shouted in chorus and not let
the speaker finish — and spreading through those close to him every kind
of counter-revolutionary slander about the collective farms and the
Soviet power. He admitted no guilt. On 7 September he was served the
indictment under article 58-10, on 8 September the NKVD troika of the
Chuvash republic sentenced him to be shot, and in the night of 17
September 1937 — 4 September of the old style, the day the Russian
calendar keeps him — he was shot in the cellar of one of the NKVD
buildings at Alatyr and buried in a common grave in the NKVD yard; that
night thirty-four people were shot, among them the priest Joseph
Kudryavtsev and the Renovationist “archbishop” Timothy (Zaikov).
