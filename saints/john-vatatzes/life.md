# John Vatatzes

saint.gr's calendar for 4 Νοεμβρίου names «Άγιος Ιωάννης ο Βατατζής ο ελεήμονας βασιλιάς» and dates him 1193 to 1254, and its page begins not with his reign but with his almsgiving: the pious emperor John Vatatzes, it says, had made mercy the first thing in his life, and it was on mercy and love that he met the thorny questions of his time and solved the problem of poverty. The page is open about the legend around him too, and says that the prophecies and tales of the marble emperor are bound up with wonders told of this saint king.

He was born in 1193 at Didymoteicho, of a distinguished Byzantine family, and was John III Doukas Vatatzes, who took the surname Eleemon, the Merciful, for his great almsgiving. In 1204, after the fall of Constantinople to the Frankish crusaders, the empire was divided into four rival kingdoms — the Latin empire of Constantinople, the empire of Nicaea in Asia Minor, where the lawful emperor and the Ecumenical Patriarch took refuge, the Despotate of Epirus in western Greece, and the Bulgarian kingdom of John II Asen.

In 1222 John Doukas Vatatzes succeeded his uncle Theodore Laskaris on the throne of Nicaea, and by his political skill managed to reorganise the Byzantine forces on the model of the old empire. As emperor, the page says, he showed the exceptional qualities of a political leader and the virtues of a Christian ruler. By a series of brilliant military victories and clever handling he secured control of Asia Minor within a few years and recovered the greater part of the lands that had been in Latin hands; he took Adrianople; and when he died in 1254 the empire of Nicaea in fact held the territories of the Byzantine empire as they had been before the Frankish conquest.

Nothing was left but the taking of the City itself, which was achieved by his successor Michael VIII Palaiologos in 1261 — and which the historians, the page says, credit to the emperor John Vatatzes. Carried along by a burning zeal for the good of the Church, John III conducted many negotiations with Pope Gregory IX; but in vain, because the Pope held to his own views.

The page prints several hymns for him and two epigrams, of which one turns on the two thrones: being an emperor below in time past, crowned one, you have now been shown an emperor above. It adds that what settled his own problem, the problem of spiritual death, is revealed by his incorrupt body.
