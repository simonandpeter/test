# Quadratus of Nicomedia

The Ohrid Prologue says this Quadratus was a nobleman and a rich man, and at the same time a convinced and baptised Christian. In the time of the persecution, when Valerian shut many Christians up in prison, Quadratus bribed the jailers and so got in to them, bringing the prisoners provisions of various kinds and strengthening them in the faith.

When they were brought before the judge and asked by him for their names, their country and their estate, they kept silence. Then Quadratus appeared from behind them and cried out with the whole of his voice that they were Christians by name, servants of Jesus Christ the Lord by rank and by thanksgiving, and that their city and their fatherland was heaven. After that declaration he too was put on trial, and after long and heavy torments he was beheaded with the rest.

That is the whole of the entry. It gives no year and no century — only the persecution of Valerian — names no judge, does not say how many the prisoners were or what became of their bodies, and prints no hymn.

The Russian church keeps him on this same day, and the Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery is the first source read for this folder to tell his sufferings at all. He came of a notable family, it says, and had great wealth, and spared nothing of it in helping his fellow Christians who were shut up for the faith. When Decius's envoy, the proconsul Perennius, came to Nicomedia, Quadratus presented himself before him of his own accord, to strengthen the courage of the prisoners by his own unsparing resolve. Perennius first tried promises of reward and honour; then, seeing them wasted, he threw him into prison and had him laid on his back on small nails with a great stone pressed down on him.

Going on to Nicaea, the proconsul had all the imprisoned Christians led after him. At Nicaea Quadratus asked to be taken into the pagan temple, and as soon as his hands and feet were untied he rushed at the idols and began to overturn and break them. Under the torments that followed he stayed firm and held up the other martyrs, whose wounds were being burned with lighted candles; and a bright cloud suddenly overshadowed them while the pagans were left in complete darkness, and in the silence the quiet singing of angels praising God was heard, so that many of those standing by confessed themselves Christians on the spot. Perennius set the wonder down to sorcery.

From Nicaea the martyrs followed the proconsul to Apamea, then to Caesarea, Apollonia and the Hellespont, tormented at every stage for a denial. At Caesarea Quadratus was tied into a sack of venomous creeping things and thrown for a night into a deep pit, and came out whole; and the beating that followed moved two noblemen, [Satorin](/saints/satorin-of-caesarea) and [Rufinus](/saints/rufinus-of-caesarea), to pity, for which they were beheaded. At the last, asked once more to deny Christ, he answered that from infancy he had known one God only, Christ, and no other; was laid on a red-hot iron grating over a fire, lay on it as on a soft bed and walked out of the flame unhurt; and was beheaded in the proconsul's helpless rage.
