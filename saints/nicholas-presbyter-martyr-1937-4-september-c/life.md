# Nicholas (Khvoshchev)

Nicholas Alexandrovich Khvoshchev was born on 3 December 1883 in the
village of Kalapino in the Lukoyanov district of Nizhny Novgorod province,
into the large family of the priest Alexander Khvoshchev. He studied at
the Nizhny Novgorod seminary but left after the fourth year; on 25 August
1905 he was made psalmist of the Epiphany cathedral of the district town
of Knyaginino, where he met and married Alexandra Yakovlevna Smorchkova,
adopted daughter of a local townsman. In the autumn of 1907 he passed the
examination for ordination and was appointed to the priest’s place at the
Protection church of the village of Isupovo in the Sergach district, today
the Gagino district, and on 24 October 1907 was ordained deacon by
Nazarius (Kirillov), bishop of Nizhny Novgorod and Arzamas, and on 26
October priest by the vicar bishop Euthymius (Eliev) of Balakhna; before
the revolution he taught the Law of God in the parish and zemstvo schools
of his parish. When the mass arrests of priests and active parishioners
began in 1937 the Gagino district NKVD demanded character references on
the clergy from the local authorities, and the chairman of the village
soviet reported that Khvoshchev had served as priest at Isupovo more than
twenty years; that at the end of 1936 he had opposed the wiring of the
village for radio, and at the end of April 1937 had organised and led a
group of churchmen who at his bidding locked in the bell-tower the
Komsomol members fixing an aerial — the club stood next to the church —
so as to prevent the radio; and that he certainly had ties with the
churchmen of the neighbouring village of Pokrov, with whom he worked for
the collapse of the collective farm and organised money for those who
left it. On 8 September 1937 the NKVD arrested him for
counter-revolutionary activity and put him in the district militia
station, and questioned him on the 10th: you are charged with
counter-revolutionary activity aimed at undermining the measures of the
Soviet power; do you admit guilt? — I do not; I carried out no
counter-revolutionary actions against the authorities’ measures. How else
than counter-revolutionary can one regard your agitation among the
believers of Pokrov to wreck the local authority’s plan to wire the club,
as a result of which the workers trying to run the aerial to the church
met the resistance of a crowd set on them? — I carried on no agitation
inciting a crowd to resist the measure; the incident arose spontaneously
without my part in it. Who if not you demanded that the electrician stop
the wiring? — that is a lie; I did not. The inquiry knows that in your
resistance you went to the extreme of allowing the electrician working
on the bell-tower to be locked in — the electrician was locked in the
bell-tower without my part in it; the women resisting the aerial locked
him in. Do you admit using the church for meetings to prepare the
believers to resist the village activists’ decision to close the church?
— I personally knew nothing of such meetings. The next day the
investigator wanted to know why he had not taken part in the church
service in the neighbouring village of Pokrov: who from Pokrov invited
you to the service of 16 July, and why did you refuse? — the evening
before, the churchwarden came to my flat and on behalf of the Pokrov
church council asked me to take part in the service they had fixed for
the 16th, saying other priests would take part too; I refused outright,
first because I felt not quite well that day, and second because I well
understood the responsibility of organising collective services,
especially at harvest time. At the close of the inquiry, on the false
witnesses’ testimony, he was charged with close ties to the
counter-revolutionary group of churchmen of Pokrov, with provoking the
people against the wiring of the club undertaken by the village Komsomol,
and with leading the believers of Isupovo in resisting the activists’
plan to use the church as a school; on 17 September 1937 the NKVD troika
sentenced him to be shot, and on 1 November 1937 he was shot and buried in
an unknown common grave at the execution ground of the Gorky NKVD. Ten
clergy and laity of the Gagino district had been arrested on 8 September,
none admitting guilt, charged with organising a solemn service on 6 July
in which four priests and many believers took part — no political sermon
was preached, but the service itself was treated as an anti-state act
because it drew the peasants from the collective farm’s work — and on 17
September eight were sentenced to be shot: the priests John Romashkin of
Subbotino, exiled once already in 1928; Peter Lebedinsky of Sorochki,
exiled in 1935 for his sermons; Nicholas Khvoshchev; and Alexander
Nikolsky of Panovo-Osanovo; and the peasants of Pokrov Alexander Blokhin,
Peter Lonskov, Stephen Mityushin and Basil Yezhov, who had served five
years in the camps from 1930 for unpaid taxes. The Council of Bishops of
August 2000 numbered him among the new martyrs and confessors of Russia;
the Russian calendar keeps him on 4 September (Julian) with the priests and
laymen of the Gagino district, and in the synaxis of the saints of Nizhny
Novgorod.
