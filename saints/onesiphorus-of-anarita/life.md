# Onesiphorus of Anarita

saint.gr begins by saying what it does not know: where exactly this saint was born and flourished, it says, we unfortunately do not know. What is known is that he grew up in Constantinople. His parents were very pious and virtuous people, and also very rich, and had been honoured with many honours and offices by the rulers and emperors of their day; yet for all that standing, the page says, they remained faithful and humble to the end. From such parents Onesiphorus took the Christian learning and rearing to match, and the hymnographer's line the page quotes sums him up: «Ἐκ ρίζης ἀγαθῆς ἀγαθὸς ἐβλάστησε καρπός» — from a good root a good fruit sprang.

He grew up inside the palace, the page says, and was not carried away by the glitter of the offices offered him early. While still very young he was made Augustalios on account of his wisdom, his prudence and his learning — admiral of the fleet of the Empire, as we should say today. He did not give himself airs or change his way of living, but from the first moment set about increasing the Empire's fleet with proper ships, for many were the enemies who had designs on its power and as many those who envied its glory.

He was not in time. One morning the enemy ships were sailing for the City, and Onesiphorus, unready as he was, moved to cut them off. In the sea battle that followed, «κρίμασιν οἷς οἶδε Κύριος» — by judgements the Lord knows — those Greek ships that had sailed to meet the enemy were broken up, and only the flagship was saved, the one the saint was in.

The defeat shook the honourable young man, and in his contrition he abandoned his career and came with ten other companions to Cyprus, landing at Paphos. All human things are vanity, he said and said again to himself; wealth does not stay, glory does not come with us, the others added; and they resolved to scatter and live the angelic life, the monastic one. What had happened to them they took for an intervention of God and a sign that he had them in mind for somewhere else, so they did not despair nor bewail their luck as the men of this world commonly do, but glorified God, and after warm prayer parted, each going on to a place of his own.

Onesiphorus, after travelling about various places, came to the parts of the village of Anarita; the quiet and the abundant green of that beautiful country satisfied his soul. A cave outside the village became his dwelling and his hermitage, and in it he walked steadily the narrow and hard road of virtue, free of every earthly care and having one care only, how to please God. With unceasing prayer and fasting and with every virtue, like a careful farmer, he worked daily to root the thorns of the passions out of the field of his soul; the temptations he was tried with in that narrow damp cave were, the page says, unimaginably many, and he overturned them all by the power of prayer.

For this God honoured him with particular gifts, and gave him the gift of healing in abundance. He worked many wonders and cures every day — of the possessed, of lepers, of cancer sufferers, of diseases of the eyes and a host of other illnesses; and once in a terrible drought he opened the floodgates of heaven by his warm prayer, like the prophet Elijah, and brought down rains that refreshed men and beasts and watered the thirsty ground. The wretched found freedom of speech in him, the sick healing, and the grieving consolation. When in deep old age he gave up his holy soul to the Lord, he went in as a conqueror to the glory of God, and from there, the page says, he continues his benefactions.

His cave can no longer be made out: time has effaced it and the place has been buried over. But outside the village, at the south-east corner of the cemetery, one sees a ruined little church which the saint built with his own hands — a small single-aisled Byzantine chapel with a vaulted roof, long and narrow, 10.80 metres by 3.60 — in which, the page says regretfully, the liturgy ceased to be served a few years ago. Around it are the foundations of cells, witness that a monastery in honour of Saint Onesiphorus once stood there.
