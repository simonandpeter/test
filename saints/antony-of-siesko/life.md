# Antony of Siesko

saint.gr's calendar for 6 December names the venerable Antony, the New and wonderworker «ἐν Σιήσκῳ», and then says in one sentence that it has no details of the Russian venerable's life. That is the whole of the page: no century, no monastery beyond the place in the day's line, no manner of death, no relic, no hymn, and none of the synaxaristic couplets the site sets over most of its entries.

What the entry does say is carried in the line itself, and it is set down here as the line has it: that he is reckoned a Russian ascetic, that the calendar calls him the New to distinguish him from the elder Antonys it keeps, that it calls him a wonderworker, and that it places him at Siesko. Nothing further is claimed.

The Russian calendar's life page gives what the Greek one said it did not have. In the world he was Andrew, born into the household of a rich farmer in the village of Kekhta near the Northern Dvina; he was brought up well, read a great deal, and learned to paint icons. When he had lost his parents he went to Novgorod and served five years with a boyar there, then married, and his wife died within the year. He gave his goods away to the poor and went as a wanderer to a hermitage on the river Kena, where he was tonsured with the name Antony and soon ordained hieromonk.

For love of solitude he left that hermitage with two companions out of its brotherhood and settled on an island washed on one side by the Siya and ringed on the other by lakes. There, in the thick of the forest, he built a chapel in 1520; and when the clearing of the wood proved heavy work and his companions began to murmur at him, an unknown man began bringing them food and giving money for the building. The house grew famous and people came to it from the settlements round about, so he took one disciple and withdrew to a still more hidden place by Lake Palun, and lived three years there in a cell alone.

When the abbot of the Siya house gave up the governing of it the brethren begged him back. He gave way to them, took the abbacy again, and governed the monastery devoutly until his repose in 1556, at seventy-nine. The day page carries four hymns under his name, two troparia and two kontakia, and both of its troparia ask him to visit the house he gathered and not to leave it.
