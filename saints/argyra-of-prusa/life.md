# Argyra of Prusa

Argyra was born in Prusa of Bithynia — the city the life glosses as today's Bursa in Turkey — around the year 1692. Her parents, George and Susanna, raised her in the Orthodox faith, and from childhood, the life says, she made the love of God thoroughly her own. She married a young Greek; and after the crowning she was put under heavy pressure by a pasha, the eparch of Bursa in those days, who tried to force her to leave her husband and her faith and enter his harem.

On her parents' advice Argyra left her native city with her husband and went to Constantinople, to relatives and friends of the family. From the testimony of her contemporaries it is known that her persecutor did not give up: using his power and pressing her young relatives, he learned where she had gone and sent soldiers to bring her back by force among his women. The quarters of the city where the Orthodox Greeks lived were searched hard, until the pursuers found her.

Because she would not give up her faith in the Saviour Jesus Christ, and because the Greeks defended her, her oppressors used the arm of the law: they accused her falsely of revolt and of insult to Islam, and had her condemned to prison for life. Her parents tried in vain to obtain a just hearing and her release, and did not succeed.

For the next sixteen years the innocent young woman was questioned again and again by the judge, beaten and imprisoned, and in the prison she was constantly harassed by the Turkish women condemned there. Argyra bore it with greatness and patience, gave herself to fasting and prayer, and put heart into the other Christian women who were in the same gaol.

She was told she could be free only if she gave up the Christian faith and agreed to become the wife of the pasha of Bursa; so she sent word to her parents that freedom was of no use to her if she were forced to become a pagan, and that she preferred the prison and would remain Christ's. Her heart was so filled with joy and thankfulness at being imprisoned for Christ that she counted her hardships as comforts.

So it was that when a devout Christian named Manolis, a net-weaver, managed to have the charge withdrawn so that she might be released, Argyra would not accept the lifting of the sentence, reckoning the prison an imperial palace. Imprisoned and in chains for Christ, she ended her life and received the unfading crown of martyrdom in the year 1725.

Three years after her death her holy body was found untouched, giving out a sweetness past compare that filled the Christians with unspeakable joy; and Paisius, patriarch of Constantinople, himself moved her wonderful relics to the church of Saint Paraskevi, where they are honoured by the faithful to this day.

The Greek calendar keeps her on 5 April and the translation of her relics on 30 April, which is the day the Romanian calendar prints her on. saint.gr tells the same story in fewer turns and with different years: born at Prusa in 1688, beautiful to look at and fearing God, still newly married when a Turk came to love her and, unable to get her to himself, bore false witness that she had wanted to embrace the Muslim religion. The judge of Prusa imprisoned her at once. Her Christian husband worked to have the trial moved to Constantinople, but the Turk came there too and bore the same false witness again.

In her defence she confessed her faith in Christ with spiritual courage and boldness, and by the judge's order she was thrown into the prison of Hasköy, where after many years of hardship and torture she gave up her spirit to the Lord in the year 1721. The page adds where the relic is: the church of St Paraskeve in Constantinople.

The Serbian church keeps her on the Romanian calendar's day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović tells her story in six sentences which side with the Greek account wherever it parts from the Romanian. This new martyr was of Brusa, it says, born of devout parents. She had only just been married to a Christian when a Turk from the neighbourhood set his eyes on her and called her to live with him; and Argyra, who loved Christ, refused such filthy proposals.

At that he flew into a rage and accused her before the court of having meant to accept Islam and afterwards recanting — which is the charge the Greek page brings, and not the Romanian life's revolt and insult to Islam. From judge to judge and from prison to prison, this book says, holy Argyra passed fifteen full years suffering for Christ, for she loved Christ above everything in the world; and at the last she ended in prison at Constantinople in the year 1725.

**That year is doxologia's own, so nothing in this folder's dates was touched.** It stands now at two accounts for 1725 against the Greek page's 1721, and the recorded death is still 1725. Fifteen years is one short of the sixteen the Romanian life counts, and this book makes her persecutor a neighbour rather than the pasha and eparch of Bursa; neither disagreement was resolved here.
