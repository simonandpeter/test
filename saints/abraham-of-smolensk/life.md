# Abraham of Smolensk

Abraham of Smolensk, a preacher of repentance and of the coming Last
Judgement, was born in the middle of the twelfth century at Smolensk to rich
parents who had had twelve daughters before him and prayed to God for a son.
From childhood he grew in the fear of God, went often to church and had the
means to learn from books; his parents hoped their only son would marry and
carry on the noble line, but he sought another life, and when they died he
gave all he had to monasteries, churches and the poor and went about the
city in rags, praying God to show him the way of salvation. He was tonsured
at the monastery of the Mother of God five versts from Smolensk, at
Selishche, and passing through its obediences gave himself to copying and
collecting books and drawing spiritual riches from them — Prince Roman
Rostislavich of Smolensk had opened a school in the city that taught from
Greek and Latin books as well as Slavonic, and had a great library, which
Abraham used. More than thirty years he laboured there before the igumen
persuaded him in 1198 to be ordained priest; he served the Liturgy every day
and was confessor to the laity as well as the brethren, and soon became
widely known — which roused the envy of the brethren, then of the igumen,
and after five years drove him to the monastery of the Exaltation of the
Cross in Smolensk itself. With the offerings of the devout he adorned the
cathedral of that poor house with icons, curtains and candles, and painted
two icons himself on the themes that most possessed him, the Last Judgement
and the trials of the soul at the toll-houses; lean and pale from his
extreme labours, in his priestly vestments he looked like Basil the Great.
Strict with himself and with his spiritual children, he preached without
rest in church and to those who came to his cell, rich and poor alike,
until the city’s notables and clergy demanded that Bishop Ignatius put him
on trial for seducing women and luring away spiritual children — and,
worse, for heresy and the reading of forbidden books, for which they
proposed to drown or burn him. Before the prince and the bishop he answered
every false charge, and was nonetheless forbidden to serve and sent back to
his old monastery of the Mother of God. A terrible drought followed, God’s
punishment for an unjust judgement, and only when Ignatius asked his pardon
and let him serve and preach again did rain water the Smolensk land.
Ignatius then built a new monastery of the Placing of the Robe of the Mother
of God, gave Abraham its government, and, resigning his diocese for age,
settled there himself. Many wished to come under Abraham’s guidance, but he
took them so choosily and after such testing that he had only seventeen
brethren, whom, after the death of Ignatius, his spiritual friend, he urged
more than ever to remember death and pray day and night not to be
condemned at God’s judgement. He died before 1224, having lived fifty years
as a monk; a service to him and his disciple Ephraim was already composed
by the end of the thirteenth century, and the terrible Mongol invasion,
seen as God’s punishment for the nation’s sins, far from stifling his
memory reminded people of his call to repentance and to the remembrance of
the Last Judgement. The Prologue, for the Serbian calendar, tells it more
shortly — the monastery of the Holy Cross, the temptations borne with
patience and thanksgiving, the rain brought down by his prayer, and repose
about 1220 after fifty years in the habit.
