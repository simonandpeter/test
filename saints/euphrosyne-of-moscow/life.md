# Euphrosyne of Moscow

In the world she was Евдокия, a name the Russian calendar glosses «Благоволение», good pleasure. She was the daughter of Димитрий Константинович, prince of Suzdal, and his wife Анна, and on 18 January 1366, with the blessing of the metropolitan Алексий, she was married at Kolomna to Димитрий Иванович, the great prince of Moscow. The marriage bound Moscow to Suzdal and mattered to the state; the chronicler says it filled Russian hearts with joy. The shorter of the calendar's two readings dates it from 1367 instead, and the folder keeps both.

It was made in a hard time. The forty years of relative quiet were ending, and besides the Horde and Lithuania the Russian principalities were still at each other's throats. Plague broke out in Moscow almost in the year of the wedding and people died by thousands; then came a great fire that ate the wooden city street by street. The weeping reached the prince's chambers, and the calendar says it was then that the young princess showed herself a mother to the burnt-out, the widows and the orphans. Moscow had hardly risen from its ashes when in 1368 the Lithuanian prince Ольгерд laid siege to the Kremlin, with the prince, the princess, the metropolitan and the boyars shut inside it.

Her whole married life runs through those wars. When her husband had to go to the Horde over the great principality, she prayed with the people for his safe return, and he came back with the patent. The household lived under the guidance of Алексий and of Сергий, who baptised her husband and two of their children, and her own confessor was Сергий's disciple Феодор, abbot of the Simonov monastery at Moscow. For the victory on the field of Kulikovo in 1380 she built inside the Kremlin a church of the Nativity of the Mother of God, painted by Феофан Грек and Симеон Чёрный. At the coming of Тохтамыш in 1382 she got out of the walls with her children and the metropolitan Киприан and was nearly taken on the road; the city fell after three days and was burnt. In 1383, with the khan in a rage, their eldest son Василий was sent to the Horde instead of his father and kept there two years as a hostage against a ransom of eight thousand roubles, which the ruined principality could not pay, until he escaped.

Her husband died on 19 May 1389, in his fortieth year, and the chronicler wrote down her lament for him, which the calendar calls one of the most inspired poems of old Russia. He left the throne to Василий and named her his co-ruler; she kept out of affairs of state. After his death she took up a strictly monastic life in the world, put on a hair shirt and wore heavy irons under her princely clothes, and gave feasts in the palace at which she ate nothing but fasting food. Slander went round Moscow touching the widow's honour and reached her sons; when Юрий asked her about it she called them all together and drew aside part of her clothing, and they saw a body so wasted that the flesh clung to the bones. They begged her forgiveness and wanted to avenge her, and she forbade them to think of revenge.

She founded in the Kremlin itself a new women's monastery of the Ascension, at the Florov gates, where she had seen her husband off and met him on his return from Kulikovo, raising the cells on the site of the palace Тохтамыш had burnt; and she built churches and monasteries at Pereslavl-Zalessky at the same time. At the invasion of Тамерлан in 1395 it was on her advice that the wonderworking Vladimir icon of the Mother of God was brought from Vladimir to Moscow, and she met it on the Kuchkovo field on 26 August with her sons, the metropolitan, the clergy and the city; and the calendar says that on that day and at that hour Тамерлан saw in a dream a shining Woman ringed with lightning-bearing soldiers, and turned his armies back from the Russian border.

In 1407, after a vision of the archangel Михаил foretelling her death, she resolved on the monastic life she had wanted all along, and had an image of the archangel painted for the Kremlin church she had built. The night before her tonsure she appeared in a dream to a blind beggar and promised to heal him; and as she walked to the monastery he called out to her — the lover of God, the great princess, the feeder of the poor, who had never refused them — and she went on as though she neither saw nor heard him, and let the sleeve of her shift fall across him as if by accident, and he wiped his eyes with it and saw. The account says thirty people were healed of various sicknesses on the day of her tonsure, which was 17 May 1407, in the wooden church of the Ascension, and that she took in it the name Евфросиния, which means joy. Three days later the stone church was begun, and she chose her burial place in it. She died on 7 July 1407 in her fifty-fourth year and was buried where she had chosen, and the calendar follows the church's long history after her: the great princesses and tsaritsas of Russia were buried there, thirty-five tombs stood in it by the twentieth century, the monastery was destroyed in 1929, and her relics, saved from destruction, cannot now be told apart from the other remains in the two fifteenth-century stone tombs they lie in.
