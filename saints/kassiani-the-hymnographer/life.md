# Kassiani the Hymnographer

Kassiani — Kassia, Ikasia or Eikasia — was born between 805 and 810 at
Constantinople and lived in the reign of Theophilus (829–842). Grown up, she
joined beauty to quickness of mind, and three Byzantine chroniclers — Symeon
the Metaphrast, George the Monk and Leo the Grammarian — say she stood in
the bride-show his stepmother Euphrosyne arranged for the young emperor, at
which he chose his wife by handing her a golden apple. Dazzled, Theophilus
came up to her and said: through a woman flowed the base things — meaning
Eve; and Kassia answered: but through a woman too spring the better things —
meaning the Virgin and the incarnation. His pride was hurt; he passed her
over and chose Theodora.

The next thing known of her is that in 843 she founded a convent to the west
of the city near the walls and was its first abbess. Many take that as the
sequel to the bride-show; a letter of Theodore the Studite gives her other
motives, and she kept close ties with the neighbouring Stoudios monastery,
whose work on the service books in the ninth and tenth centuries is why her
own has survived. The tradition adds a last meeting: Theophilus, still in
love, came to the convent to see her once more before he died; she was alone
in her cell writing the troparion now sung on the evening of Great Tuesday,
heard the imperial retinue, and hid in a cupboard rather than let an old
passion outrun her monastic zeal, leaving the unfinished hymn on the table.
He found the cell, searched it in vain, wept for the woman a moment’s pride
had cost him, read the manuscript, and added a line — “whose feet Eve in
Paradise, hearing them at evening, hid herself in fear” — and on his way out
saw where she was hiding and, respecting her wish, said nothing; she came out
when he had gone, read his line, and finished the hymn.

She travelled to Italy and to Crete and ended her life on Kasos, where her
marble sarcophagus and a ninth-century mosaic survive in a chapel of her name
with a slab dated 890 built into the wall; her bones are said to have been
carried to Ikaria. No synaxarion records her memory. The people of Kasos,
from the kinship of her name and their island’s, fixed it on 7 September,
and George Sassos of Kasos composed a service for her, printed at Alexandria
in 1889 and dedicated to the patriarch Sophronius, who passed it to the
metropolitan of the Thebaid to print — which is how, as the islanders wanted,
the church of Alexandria came to set its seal on her. Some forty-five works
are attributed to her, at least twenty-three beyond doubt: the troparion
“Lord, the woman fallen into many sins” in the plagal fourth mode, the
heirmoi “By the wave of the sea” of the canon of Holy Saturday, the
tetraodion “Foolish old man”, many doxastika including the Christmas
“When Augustus reigned alone”, and stichera for the saints — she wrote both
words and music, the most eminent woman melodist in the history of Byzantine
chant, and Krumbacher found in her work initiative, deep learning,
self-assurance and boldness, and deep piety. The synaxarion is careful to
say what she was not: the sinful woman of her own troparion is the nameless
woman of Luke’s gospel, neither Kassiani nor Mary Magdalene. The Romanian
calendar keeps her the same day, with a troparion that praises her for
disdaining the love of the earthly emperor for love of the Emperor of all.
