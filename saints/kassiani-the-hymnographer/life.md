# Kassiani the Hymnographer

Kassiani — Kassia, Ikasia or Eikasia — was born between 805 and 810 at
Constantinople and lived in the reign of Theophilus (829–842). Grown up, she
joined beauty to quickness of mind, and three Byzantine chroniclers — Symeon
the Metaphrast, George the Monk and Leo the Grammarian — say she stood in
the bride-show his stepmother Euphrosyne arranged for the young emperor, at
which he chose his wife by handing her a golden apple. Dazzled, Theophilus
came up to her and said: through a woman flowed the base things — meaning
Eve; and Kassia answered: but through a woman too spring the better things —
meaning the Virgin and the incarnation. His pride was hurt; he passed her
over and chose Theodora.

The next thing known of her is that in 843 she founded a convent to the west
of the city near the walls and was its first abbess. Many take that as the
sequel to the bride-show; a letter of Theodore the Studite gives her other
motives, and she kept close ties with the neighbouring Stoudios monastery,
whose work on the service books in the ninth and tenth centuries is why her
own has survived. The tradition adds a last meeting: Theophilus, still in
love, came to the convent to see her once more before he died; she was alone
in her cell writing the troparion now sung on the evening of Great Tuesday,
heard the imperial retinue, and hid in a cupboard rather than let an old
passion outrun her monastic zeal, leaving the unfinished hymn on the table.
He found the cell, searched it in vain, wept for the woman a moment’s pride
had cost him, read the manuscript, and added a line — “whose feet Eve in
Paradise, hearing them at evening, hid herself in fear” — and on his way out
saw where she was hiding and, respecting her wish, said nothing; she came
out when he had gone, read his line, and finished the hymn.

She travelled to Italy and to Crete and ended her life on Kasos, where her
marble sarcophagus and a ninth-century mosaic survive in a chapel of her
name with a slab dated 890 built into the wall; her bones are said to have
been carried to Ikaria. No synaxarion records her memory. The people of
Kasos, from the kinship of her name and their island’s, fixed it on 7
September, and George Sassos of Kasos composed a service for her, printed at
Alexandria in 1889 and dedicated to the patriarch Sophronius, who passed it
to the metropolitan of the Thebaid to print — which is how, as the islanders
wanted, the church of Alexandria came to set its seal on her. Some
forty-five works are attributed to her, at least twenty-three beyond doubt:
the troparion “Lord, the woman fallen into many sins” in the plagal fourth
mode, the heirmoi “By the wave of the sea” of the canon of Holy Saturday,
the tetraodion “Foolish old man”, many doxastika including the Christmas
“When Augustus reigned alone”, and stichera for the saints — she wrote both
words and music, the most eminent woman melodist in the history of Byzantine
chant, and Krumbacher found in her work initiative, deep learning,
self-assurance and boldness, and deep piety. The synaxarion is careful to
say what she was not: the sinful woman of her own troparion is the nameless
woman of Luke’s gospel, neither Kassiani nor Mary Magdalene. The Romanian
calendar keeps her the same day, with a troparion that praises her for
disdaining the love of the earthly emperor for love of the Emperor of all.

The Russian church calendar, which does not keep her in its menologion but
prints her page on 7 September of the old style, 20 September, after the
Synaxarion of Macarius of Simonopetra, has her born between 800 and 810 of a
noble family, tells the bride-show with the golden apple in 821 or 830 —
evil came from a woman, said Theophilus, meaning Eve; yes, she answered, but
from a woman came the highest good for us, meaning the Mother of God, the
new Eve — and says that, stung by her wit and afraid of a wife who would
outdo him, he chose Theodora, who was afterwards to restore Orthodoxy.
Kassia founded a monastery on the hill of the Hebdomon, later called the
monastery of Kassia, and there, after the urgings of Theodore the Studite,
whose letters to her survive, was tonsured and chosen abbess, so that,
missing an earthly kingdom, she gained the heavenly one, and put the
treasures of her contemplative life into hymns that set her among the
greatest hymnographers of the Church. From her youth she held to the icons
and openly opposed the emperor’s iconoclasm; under the ban she was cruelly
scourged, and unshaken by it visited the imprisoned monks with gifts and
letters, and said of those who lacked the courage to confess Orthodoxy, I
hate silence when it is time to speak. She died in peace between 843 and
867. Thirty-three genuine hymns survive under the names Kassiane, Kassia the
nun or Ikasia — idiomela for many saints’ days in the menaia, the sticheron
of the Nativity vespers, When Augustus reigned alone on earth, the many
rules of men ceased, the heirmoi ascribed to her of the Great Saturday
canon, and above all the doxastikon of the aposticha of Great Wednesday
matins, the troparion of Kassiane, in which she binds the Gospel’s sinful
woman to the fall of Eve. The page adds that her memory is in no
hagiographical manuscript, that her veneration has spread widely in Greece
since the end of the nineteenth century, and that she is counted the
patroness of Kasos.
