# Urpasian

As soon as Maximian took the reins of the empire into his hands he began to rule the parts about Nicomedia harshly, making himself a burning defender of the idols. His friends in counsel and in drink once kindled his anger like a strong flame against the Christians; and gathering together all the counsellors and chief men of his empire he cried out to them that if any among them had fallen into the faith of the Christians and would not return to the well-wishing gods, he should undo his belt before them all and go out of the palace and the city.

Fear took hold of all who had believed in Christ, and it could then be seen plainly how the right faith in Christ stood in each of them: some of those present hid that they were Christians, and others denied it outright. But those who carried the love of God unstained in their souls despised the torments, mocked the tyrant, threw down their belts and went out.

Then Urpasian too, a brave man with a soul of diamond, who was one of the counsellors, threw off his cloak and his belt and said to the emperor in a strong voice that since that day he declared himself a soldier of the heavenly King, who is his Lord Jesus Christ, and that the emperor might take back his belt, his honour and his rank. Maximian's face changed at once, and for a long while he stayed speechless; then, rubbing his eyes and looking darkly at him, he roared like a wild beast, and had him hung up and his body broken with ox-sinews.

The saint was beaten without pity for hours together, his eyes turned to heaven and praying, and not saddened in the least. The tyrant had him taken down from the wood on which he had been tormented and thrown into a dark and well-guarded prison, to be left there alone until he should think with what death to destroy him. While he lay there the emperor devised a new instrument of torture, an iron cage; and he had the saint brought out, shut into it, and the cage hung up.

The cage covered his whole body, and the tyrant ordered torches lit and the cage burned without mercy with the saint inside it. Hanging there and praying, he was so burned that all the flesh melted off him and ran like wax into the ground, and his bones became like the dust of a threshing floor. The air filled with a sweet scent, and his soul went up to the Lord like a bright morning star, as some of the faithful who were present said they had seen. The emperor, still in his madness, had both the earth into which the melted flesh had run and the dust of his bones carefully gathered and scattered in the sea before him.

All this happened in the city of Nicomedia. doxologia names Maximian but gives no year, and prints no hymn for him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr says more of him than the Romanian line does. He belonged to the order of the senators and lived at the beginning of the fourth century. When Diocletian published his edict against the Christians he first called the senators together and declared that any one of them who was a Christian would be pardoned if he said so at once and renounced Christ. Urpasian heard the emperor out and, instead of any other answer, took off the insignia of his own rank with his own hands and handed them over to him.

Diocletian was angered and ordered him tortured. He was scourged with ox sinews and thrown half dead into prison, and afterwards various of his friends among the senators came and tried to persuade him to sacrifice to the idols and keep his office; he stayed by his decision, and his death was then decreed. They opened his sides with iron instruments and set lighted lamps in the wounds, and the burns together with the smoke brought his martyr's end. The page prints two distichs for him, one of which calls the cage a chariot of fire and the torches his four-horse team of ascent, and gives no year.

The Russian church keeps him on this same day, and the Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery tells the martyrdom shortly and names the emperor: Maximian Galerius, whose reign it gives as 305 to 311, persecuted the Christians serving in his army and at his court, and some of the faint-hearted wavered and began to worship the pagan gods while those strong in spirit stood firm to the end. Urpasian, a dignitary, threw his cloak and his belt down at the ruler's feet and said that from that day he was a soldier of the heavenly King, the Lord Jesus Christ, and that the insignia given him might be taken back.

Maximian had him tied to a tree and beaten with ox sinews, and then tied to the upper grating of an iron cage with a fire lit beneath it; he bore the torments praying without pause and was burned alive, and his ashes were thrown into the sea, because even dead he was dreadful to tormentors who had not managed to break his spirit. The page sets all this at Nicomedia, as the Romanian life does. Its day page prints no hymn under his name, only the four to the Forty of Sebaste whom the day heads; and the calendar's own line dates him about 295, ten years before the reign its life page assigns him, a contradiction this folder records and does not resolve.
