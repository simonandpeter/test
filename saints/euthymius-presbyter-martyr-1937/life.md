# Euthymius (Goryachev)

Euthymius Nikitich Goryachev was born in the village of Nikolskoye-Barnuki
in the Gorodishche district of Penza province on 19 January 1884 to a poor
peasant family. He was four when his father died, and soon his mother died
too; the only boy, he was left in his sisters’ care, and his uncle Alexis
Goryachev and his wife Alexandra, his godmother, who had four sons of
their own, gladly took him into their house — a God-fearing family, and
Alexandra, even among her Orthodox neighbours, marked by deep religion and
judgement, so that the local priest would send parishioners in family
difficulties to her. The priest, who had no children, asked for the boy
when he was seven — Alexandra, give me your foster-child and godson; you
have four sons of your own, and I will bring him up — and she, having come
to love the quiet, obedient orphan, agreed for his good. The priest put
him through the village school and then a two-class school that became a
seminary that year, dressing him like the peasant children, in bast shoes,
so that he should learn early to fend for himself; a diligent student, he
was soon earning by lessons in richer houses, and after the seminary
taught seven years at the school of Arkhangelskoye, near the railway
station of Chaadaevka, where the educated young met in the evenings and
the cheerful, sociable young man, a good dancer, became the soul of the
company. There he nearly married: the wealthy and devout Popov family
wished to give him their only daughter, she was willing and he liked her,
the priest Alexander Fyodorov and his aunt urged it, but her wealth and
the great dowry shamed him — he did not want people to say he had married
for money — and on eighteen roubles a month the match fell through. Moved
to the better-paid school at Voskresenskoye-Lopukhovka near Penza, he was
pressed by his uncle and aunt to marry Alexandra, the daughter of their
friend Constantine Kiryanov — a man who had risen from terrible want by
his own wits and honesty in a merchant’s service, and who once told
Euthymius that his wealth was the surer, an eternal crust of bread, and
that on the scales he alone would outweigh Kiryanov with all his money.
The girl did not love him and never would; he saw it, and thought of
fleeing whatever the scandal, but her father, having set his will on the
match, beat her with the reins until she cried, give me to whom you like,
only do not beat me. She was some months short of sixteen; a petition
went to the bishop, and on 9 February 1909 they were married. The quarrels
began at once, and she showed her contempt before company, sitting beside
anyone but her husband; he could only bear it, and did. She was faithful,
loved her children without reserve when they came, but her faith was
shallow — he never saw her weep at prayer. Meanwhile the educated young
were drifting into circles and socialism, and worse, into a shallowing of
soul that could no longer feel the rites of the Church; his old guardian
saw it and asked, as his last great wish, that Euthymius choose the
priesthood. He agreed, took the pastoral courses in Moscow in 1911, and in
March 1912 was ordained priest for the Siberian village of
Novo-Novoselovo. He had great gifts as a preacher — peasants sent a cart a
hundred versts to fetch him — and having grown up poor he took nothing
from the poor for the rites and gave away his last to a family that had
lost its breadwinner, so that his own family soon knew real want and he
earned his bread by peasant labour beside his parishioners. On 11 March
1917 he was moved to the St Nicholas church of Bolshoy Uluy near Achinsk
in the Krasnoyarsk country, where almost the whole of his priestly life
passed and where his people loved him for his unselfishness and his
sermons from the heart — at his sermons on repentance before confession
they knelt and many wept aloud. On 5 November 1917 the parishes of the
deanery chose him dean, and the years of the Russian troubles began. In
1919 the partisan Shchetinkin’s detachment held the district; two priests
of his deanery, Vladimir Fokin and Michael Kargopolov, were killed; he
went on living as usual, and was twice summoned before Shchetinkin over
marriages he had refused to perform — once for want of a proper baptismal
record, once because the Church’s rule forbids weddings in Shrovetide —
and each time the partisan chief laughed and let him have his way. Then a
Cossack punitive column came, quartered its colonel in his house and then
next door; that night the reprisals began in the house across the road,
where he got one man freed by vouching for him and was put out of the
room, and in the morning he found stripped corpses in the ravine with a
pig at them. The colonel told him he would wipe Uluy from the face of the
earth, burn it and shoot every tenth soul, women and children counted;
then begin, said the priest, with my house, and shoot me first, my wife
tenth, my first child twentieth, and so to the last — and wept and
crawled on his knees until the colonel, who had meant to except his
family, gave in and promised the village no reprisals, and kept his word.
Once, when a detachment shelled the village and the houses caught fire so
that his hair singed, he went into the church with his eldest daughter
Antonina clinging to his cassock, opened the royal doors and prayed at the
altar while the child knelt and begged God to spare them all: that
child’s prayer, he said afterwards, saved the village. Soviet power
reached Siberia in 1922 and Renovationism with it; for resisting the
Living Church he was removed as dean in November 1922, suspended and
retired in 1923, marked for expulsion from the Achinsk district, and in
January 1924 “deprived of his rank” — and served on regardless, until in
August 1924 he was arrested and held a month in the Achinsk prison and
two in the Krasnoyarsk GPU. Every church round about was in Renovationist
hands; but in the spring of 1925 the Orthodox bishop Amphilochius
(Skvortsov) came to Krasnoyarsk, blessed him to serve wherever he could,
and the people of Bolshoy Uluy drove the Renovationist out, so that on
Great Thursday 1925 he served again in his old church, and by June all
fourteen churches of the deanery had come back to Orthodoxy; the deanery
re-elected him dean in 1926, and in June the bishop, needing confessors
in the city cathedrals, made him rector of the Trinity cathedral of
Achinsk. In November 1927 he went to Moscow with his churchwarden to test
the claims of the Gregorian “Higher Temporary Church Council”, saw
Metropolitan Agathangel at Yaroslavl, Metropolitan Sergius twice and
Bishop Philip three times, and sat through four Gregorian sessions at the
Donskoy monastery; he judged the council uncanonical, a device to divide
the Church with the godless power’s consent, and when the warden argued
that the old Church too had had unbelieving officers and gentry he
answered that the old power had not persecuted the Church as the Soviet
did, and he would always stand with any power that let men believe
freely — and the cathedral community, and the whole deanery, refused the
council. In 1929 the persecution sharpened; naked couples paraded Achinsk
with red sashes “down with shame and conscience”, the people lay down in
the churchyard to keep the bells from being thrown down, his house was
taken, and he, his wife and seven children lived in a bath-house on a
straw mattress. On 30 November 1929 he was arrested; his wife was in such
despair that she thought of stopping the stove and ending them all, but
the parishioners brought food, and his daughter Antonina stood under the
prison windows begging the sentry to kill her instead of her father until
his voice came from the cell telling her to stand back and asking whether
they had eaten that day. He was accused of anti-Soviet agitation, of
telling his flock that Soviet power made men renounce God and the Church,
and of having betrayed partisans to Kolchak’s punitive column; he answered
every charge at length — whoever says I had any dealings with the Kolchak
reaction I call a liar, and if I must perish I shall perish with the
thought that I never knowingly did anyone harm — and to the informer put
in his cell to talk him out of the priesthood he said: never; I serve by
conviction; a time may come when they carry us under straw and dung to
serve in cellars and pits, and I shall not stop; Christianity will remain,
as it remained in the catacombs and triumphed — and a child drinks in
Christianity with its mother’s milk. On 23 February 1930 the OGPU’s
special board gave him three years in a Siberian concentration camp, from
which he came back barely alive; his wife wrote to Antonina, “one more
mouth to feed has arrived”, and the daughter, herself starving in the
famine of 1933, begged twenty kilos of flour from her manager, an old Red
partisan who gave it unasked, and knelt in the bath-house porch asking her
father’s forgiveness — it is I alone who am to blame for everything, he
said, kissing her head; none of you is to blame. The Achinsk cathedral was
closed; he served at Skripachnikovo from January 1933 until it too was
closed in April, and rather than eat his children’s bread walked out in
the deepest frost with two sacks of joiner’s tools on his back, looking
back the whole five versts for someone to call him home, and would have
frozen ten versts short of Achinsk had a passing peasant not put the
“former cathedral priest” on his trace-horse and driven him in at a
gallop. In May 1934 the people of Beya in the far south begged him from
Archbishop Dionysius of Achinsk and Minusinsk, carted his family there
themselves, and he served and was made dean of the district; he preached
seldom now, knowing how the informers twisted his words, but explained the
sacraments to the grown and the commandments to the children, and warned
the parish that the taxes would ruin the church. On 30 July 1935 the
executive committee closed the church “until major repairs”, he was turned
out of the church lodge with one darned vestment and some books, and
fined a hundred roubles for failing to register at a passport office that
was shut; telegrams to Moscow went unanswered, and he set up a house
church in a rented room, serving Christmas and a night Liturgy for
Theophany for twenty or thirty people. We have to serve like thieves, like
exiles, he told them, and you are to blame yourselves by your weak faith —
you take fright at a little finger, and if someone stamps his foot you are
ready to bury yourselves; we must bear everything with patience, as the
apostles did and as the Old Believers did under the tsars, and may have to
serve in the taiga and underground. A heart attack in 1936 laid him low,
and he let his daughter photograph him for the only time since his youth.
He served Great Lent and Easter openly, asking leave for Easter only so
that the authorities could not say he had not asked; at two in the
morning of Easter night the NKVD broke in, listed the thirty worshippers
and arrested him, seizing his registers of births, marriages and deaths —
which showed that the village soviet had failed to register fifteen births
and nine deaths in 1934 and eleven deaths in 1935, so that he was charged
with keeping a civil register to the harm of the Soviet state. If I must
be accused, he wrote from prison, I submit with joy; do not release me
when the inquiry ends, for once free I shall feel bound again to baptise,
to bury and to perform the other rites. In August 1936 the special board
gave him three years in the Karaganda camps; on the way he was taken off
the convoy to drive sheep to Lake Balkhash, then held near Dolinka, and in
the summer of 1937, in the camp hospital, blind since his spectacles were
crushed, robbed of his boots and linen and already listed as under
investigation rather than as a prisoner, he wrote his last letter home.
He was sentenced to be shot and shot on 15 September 1937 — 2 September of
the old style, the day the Russian calendar keeps him. The life, by the
igumen Damascene (Orlovsky), tells also of his friend the priest Nikita
Storozhev, who died in a camp near Kemerovo after his arrest in 1935. The
Council of Bishops of 2000 numbered him among the new martyrs and
confessors of Russia, and the Russian calendar also keeps him in the
synaxis of the saints of Krasnoyarsk.
