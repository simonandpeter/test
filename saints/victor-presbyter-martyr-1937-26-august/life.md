# Victor (Ellansky)

Victor Vasilievich Ellansky was born on 16 September 1874 — 1875 by other
accounts — in the village of Ploskoye in the Oster district of Chernigov
province, the son of the priest Basil Ellansky. At eleven or twelve he
entered the Chernigov church school, where at first, the records note, he
showed weak marks though always excellent for conduct, a beginner’s
difficulty that did not keep him from going straight on to the Chernigov
seminary, which he finished in 1894, the year his wife Elena Ivanovna, born
Mankovskaya, a priest’s daughter too, finished the Chernigov diocesan
girls’ school; they had eight children. On 5 February 1895 Bishop Anthony
(Sokolov) of Chernigov and Nezhin ordained him priest, and he served at the
Dormition church of Tuligolovo in the Glukhov district, teaching the Law of
God in its parish school; after a short spell in 1899–1900 in the Turkestan
diocese, at the Trinity church of the stanitsa of Lepsinskaya, he returned
to Chernigov province, to the George church of Shumarovo in the Mglin
district and within a year to the Alexander Nevsky church of Lopatni in
the Gorodnya district, where he sat on the deanery council, was
investigator in spiritual affairs and in 1904 received the nabedrennik; on
the eve of 1905 he came home to the Oster district, to the Dormition
church of Pogreby, and three years later had the skufia for zealous
pastoral work. In 1910 he entered the Kiev Academy, finishing in 1914 a
candidate of theology; he was briefly inspector and teacher of the Law of
God at the Smolensk diocesan girls’ school, and at the end of 1914 passed
to the ministry of education as teacher of the Law of God at the Vinnitsa
teachers’ institute. The year of the Bolshevik revolution was the year he
began to serve in Kiev, where he was one of the closest helpers of the
hieromartyr Metropolitan Vladimir (Bogoyavlensky).

His martyr’s road began in 1924 with an arrest for counter-revolutionary
activity: the OGPU collegium gave him three years’ exile, served at Kozlov
in Tambov province. In 1927 he returned to serve in Kiev, was raised, it
seems that year, to archpriest, and until a new arrest in March 1929 was
rector of the Alexander Nevsky church in Lipki; after more than half a
year in the Kiev house of preliminary detention he was sentenced in
October to three years’ exile, this time in the Irkutsk region, in the
village of Privalikhino on the Angara, with a ban on living afterwards in
twelve regions of the country. His letter of December 1931 to E. P.
Peshkova, head of the Aid to Political Prisoners, begging her great help
to return to his family, tells of that exile: this summer was especially
hard; I was left without a kopeck and had to keep myself with my own
hands; on 21 July all my money was taken, and then my outer clothes; I had
to find my own food, in summer gathering berries and mushrooms and
fishing, in autumn going out to work, and in that work, often beyond my
years, I wore out completely and in my working clothes came to look like a
ragged tramp; I have no warm underclothes, no warm boots, no warm coat,
and meanwhile I have had to take on for the whole winter the clearing of
the ice-holes for the cattle, the washing and the village’s water, a daily
and often grievous labour — and the weight of it is made worse by the
unrelenting reproaches of conscience that you are the cause of your
family’s misery. Elena had written to Peshkova at the end of 1929: I am
often ill, and my family is large, eight children unprovided for and
small; two sons earn a little and can give me no more than twenty roubles
a month; the rest live on the chance help of benefactors; the children
have neither shoes nor warm clothes and must go to school; winter is
coming and I am in despair; I spend the whole day looking for bread. After
the exile, barred from twelve regions, he settled in January 1934 at
Michurinsk in Tambov province, received the blessing of Archbishop Vassian
(Pyatnitsky) of Tambov to serve in the churches of the diocese, and showed
himself a zealous pastor, serving often in the town and the villages
around; but in March 1935 he was arrested a third time and put in the
Michurinsk prison, questioned the same day and charged with anti-Soviet
agitation — telling believers the Soviet power would not last and
foretelling its speedy end. He did not admit it: all authority is given by
God, he explained; as a deeply believing man I acknowledge the Soviet
power as a power given by God. In December 1935 — 18 December 1936 by the
shorter life, which cannot stand beside what follows — the visiting
session of the special collegium of the Voronezh regional court sentenced
him to five years’ imprisonment, later changed to camp, and on 15 April
1936 he reached the Bidaik section of the Karaganda camp, where he worked
as a book-keeper at the Alikhan camp point. In October of that year, at
sixty-three, he wrote to Peshkova again: I feel completely exhausted,
painfully tired, almost a full invalid; winter is coming and I have neither
warm clothes nor boots; my coat is rags and my boots were stolen; all my
clothing is worn through, and the trousers and shirt issued me are no
shelter from the cold — and, not hoping to live out his term in such
conditions, asked her once more to plead for his return to the family he
had not seen for eight years. In 1937 he was arrested in the camp, charged
that among the prisoners he carried on defeatist counter-revolutionary
agitation, saying the Soviet power would soon be destroyed by God; that
beside the agitation he held services, gathering former priests round him;
and that, religious to the point of fanaticism, at Pentecost he sang the
troparion of the Trinity and other things. He did not admit guilt. By
sentence of the troika of the NKVD for the Karaganda region of 31 August
he was shot on 8 September 1937 — 26 August of the old style, the day the
Russian calendar keeps him. The Karaganda prosecutor rehabilitated him in
1990, and in August 2000 the jubilee Council of Bishops glorified him; the
short life is by Igumen Damascene (Orlovsky), the full by M. N.
Tomashevskaya.
