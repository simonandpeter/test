# Valens the Deacon

Valens was deacon of the church of Aelia — the name the Romans had given Jerusalem — and the second of the twelve martyrs who suffered for Christ at Caesarea in Palestine in the reign of Diocletian. The life calls him a man honourable in understanding: old, but sharp of mind, for he knew the divine Scripture by heart. With him were [Pamphilus](/saints/pamphilus-of-caesarea), presbyter of Caesarea and first of the twelve, and [Paul](/saints/paul-of-jamnia), who came from the town of Jamnia.

The three were put to various tortures by the governor Urban and then thrown into prison, and there they stayed two years, until Firmilian took the governorship after him. In that time five young Egyptians, brothers in body and in spirit, who had escorted a hundred and thirty banished confessors as far as the mines of Cilicia, were seized at the gate of Caesarea on their way home for saying plainly that they were Christians.

On the next day, which was 16 February, the five were brought out of prison with Pamphilus, Valens and Paul and set before Firmilian. He had the Egyptians beheaded when he could make nothing of their prophets' names or of the Jerusalem above they claimed as their country. Then he asked after the three older confessors, and learning that Urban had tortured them enough two years earlier he only asked whether they would obey the emperor. They would not, and he sentenced them to the sword.

Doxologia gives Valens no year beyond Diocletian's reign. The page it offers as his troparion is a recording of a sung invocation of the martyrs Valens and Pamphilus, and prints no hymn text.

The Greek calendar keeps the company on the same day and names twelve where the Romanian named four. Its line is «Άγιος Πάμφιλος και οι συν αυτώ Μάρτυρες», Pamphilus and the martyrs with him, and the page behind it lists them: Pamphilus, [Daniel](/saints/daniel-the-egyptian), [Elias](/saints/elias-the-egyptian), [Isaiah](/saints/isaiah-the-egyptian), [Theodulus](/saints/theodulus-companion-of-pamphilus), [Jeremiah](/saints/jeremiah-the-egyptian), [Julian](/saints/julian-companion-of-pamphilus), Valens, Paul, [Porphyrius](/saints/porphyrius-servant-of-pamphilus), [Samuel](/saints/samuel-the-egyptian) and Seleucus. It puts the martyrdom under the emperor Diocletian, whose reign it gives as 284 to 305, says that they came from various places but that the love and faith of Christ joined them, and that working at Caesarea in Palestine they confessed Christ before the eparch Firmilianus, who spent every effort on persuading them to deny their faith and could not move them from the piety of their fathers; so he ordered them put to death after being tortured first.

The page then divides the deaths. Elias, Pamphilus, Valens, Paul, Seleucus, Jeremiah, Isaiah, Samuel and Daniel were beheaded with the sword; Porphyrius, Pamphilus's servant, was seized while he was searching for his master's body and was burned alive together with the martyr Julian; and Theodulus they crucified upon a tree. So, it says, the saints were martyred and were added to the choir of the contenders of Christ. Of its five couplets, one is for Pamphilus alone — loving you, Word, above all, Pamphilus counted even the cutting of his head a thing to love — one for Valens, Paul and Seleucus, one for Porphyrius and Julian, one for Theodulus, and one for the five it marks as Egyptians, Elias, Jeremiah, Isaiah, Samuel and Daniel: the callings of prophets and the deaths of martyrs are the boast of five beheaded martyrs. A last line counts the day: on the sixteenth the sword's edge cut Pamphilus.

The Greek page gives him no rank; the deacon's office this corpus records for him is the Romanian calendar's, not saint.gr's, and saint.gr's roster calls him simply Ουάλης.

The Serbian church keeps him on the same day, in one entry for the whole company: the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović heads it „Светих дванаест мученика.“ — the holy twelve martyrs who suffered in the time of the emperor Diocletian — and then names all twelve in its body. He is the second of them, a deacon, old in years and grey with wisdom; an excellent knower of Holy Scripture, the Prologue says, who knew it almost by heart.
