# George the Iberian

George was a great Georgian monk, writer and ascetic, who carried on the work of his cousin the venerable [Euthymius the Iberian](/saints/euthymius-the-iberian). His family belonged to the class of officials at court, and for that reason he received a choice Greek education at Constantinople. He struggled as a novice in various cenobitic houses of his own country, and became a monk in a monastery near Antioch on the Black Mountain, living close to his wonderful guide, George the Recluse.

At his elder's urging he went to Mount Athos and continued his struggle in the monasteries of the Great Lavra and of Iviron. In the second he struggled for nearly fifteen years, and there he became abbot; with the help of the emperor Constantine Monomachos he renewed the monastery, and so became a founder of it himself, of the nave of the church above all.

He gave up the dignity of abbot and went back to the monastery on the Black Mountain. The empress of Georgia employed him in various important missions, and for the five years that he remained in Georgia he was content to help in the raising of the morals of the people and of the higher society. In his old age he wished to end his days on the Holy Mountain; and as he was returning to those friendly places, death found him at Constantinople. His honoured relics were carried to the monastery of Iviron, where he was buried at the monastery's litia.

He wrote the life of his kinsmen and the founders of the monastery, the venerable [John](/saints/john-the-iberian) and Euthymius. He translated from the Greek, or revised, many books of the New Testament and works of canon law, of dogmatics, of hagiography and of liturgics. He and Euthymius are reckoned among the greatest figures of Georgian literature. Although he fell asleep on 24 May, the Georgians keep his memory on 30 June, setting him together with the Holy Apostles; and he is honoured on 13 May.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same civil day and gives him a single sentence: he was elected abbot of the monastery of the Iberians on the Holy Mountain after the resignation of the venerable [Euthymius](/saints/euthymius-the-iberian), and he fell asleep in peace. saint.gr prints no year for either event, and its own page for Euthymius, read the same day, says instead that Euthymius died at Constantinople in 1028 on business of the Mountain; the two statements are set down here as the source has them and are not reconciled. Above the notice stands its couplet: there you dwell where your kindred race is, boast of the venerable, O right-minded George.

The Ohrid Prologue, read for the Julian 13 May, keeps the four of them under one heading as the founders of the famous Iberian — that is, the Georgian — monastery on the Holy Mountain, and it keeps him on the thirteenth of May, which is the day his Romanian page settled on out of the three it names. [John](/saints/john-the-iberian) struggled first in the lavra of Athanasius and afterwards founded his own monastery, Iver, and fell asleep in 998. [Euthymius](/saints/euthymius-the-iberian) and George, it says, translated the Holy Scriptures into the Georgian tongue; Euthymius fell asleep in 1028 and George in 1066, which is the year this folder already had from the title of its Romanian page. [Gabriel](/saints/gabriel-the-iberian) was counted worthy to receive the wonderworking icon of the Mother of God which came to the monastery over the sea.

The same book keeps him twice, and its second day is a whole entry rather than a mention. The Ohrid Prologue for the Julian 30 June sets him among the apostles and begins where no other book of this folder begins, with a year: he was born in Iveria, or Georgia, in 1014, a kinsman of the Georgian kings. He was well schooled in the classics as a child, but his heart drew him to the spiritual life, and he struggled under the famous spiritual father George on the Black Mountain before fleeing to the Holy Mountain and taking up the struggle again in the monastery of Iviron, where he became abbot.

With the help of the emperor Constantine Monomachos he renewed Iviron and roofed the church with lead, and that lead roof, the entry says, is standing to this day. He translated the Holy Scriptures into the Georgian tongue, and the Prologue and the service books with them. The emperor Bagrat called him into Georgia to teach the people; he was received in his homeland as a king is received, and went everywhere instructing clergy and people. In his old age he wished to end his days on the Holy Mountain and set out for it, but death caught him at Constantinople, and his relics were carried to Iviron.

This entry puts that death in 1067, where the Romanian page title and this same book’s own 13 May entry both put it in 1066; the interval now holds both years and neither is preferred. And the entry ends by explaining the two days in one sentence: although he died on the twenty-fourth of May, the Iverians keep his memory on the thirtieth of June, reckoning him equal to the apostles.

The Russian calendar keeps him on 27 июня ст. ст. where the other three keep him on 13 May, and the index page it gives carries two readings, both read here. He was of Iveria, which is Georgia, born in Trialeti in the south of it in 1009 — or in 1014, both readings add — to Maria and Jacob, lords of a noble house; his father went as the Georgian king’s ambassador to the Persian shah. At seven his parents brought him to the women’s monastery of Tadzri where his elder sister was being brought up, and in three years there he was twice saved from death by the providence of God, once in the river Kcia and once in a fire that broke out in the house.

He was sent to Constantinople in 1022 and studied there twelve years, getting, the calendar says, an excellent and many-sided education. Returning to Georgia he took the monastic habit, though he had lived as a monk from his youth, and after a time went to venerate the holy places of Palestine. He spent three years in the houses of the Black Mountain near Antioch and then on the Wonderful Mountain, where he received the great habit from an enclosed elder of his own name.

From Antioch, at the invitation of the Georgian king Bagrat IV, he went into Georgia and spent five years there, teaching the people in word and deed, helping to set the life of the Church in order and bringing in the work of his own translating. He gathered many orphans and poor children and taught them the Scriptures; so in the land of Iveria, the calendar says, he completed the work of the apostles who had first preached there.

Foreseeing his end he set out for the Holy Mountain, and on the way, not having reached Athos, on the eve of the feast of the chief apostles, he departed in peace to the Lord in 1066; the elder was buried on the Holy Mountain in the Iveron monastery on 24 May 1067. The day’s own line gives the year as 1065, a third reading, and no field was changed for it.
