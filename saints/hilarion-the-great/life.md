# Hilarion the Great

Hilarion was born in a village called Thavatha in Palestine, near the city of Gaza, of Greek parents — a rose from thorns, the Romanian life says. Sent to Alexandria to be schooled, he learned the wisdom the Greeks seek and the spiritual wisdom besides; he believed in Christ, was baptised, and went often to church. Hearing of [Anthony the Great](/saints/anthony-the-great), whose way of life was famous everywhere, he went into the desert to see him.

He stayed with Anthony some while, watching his prayers and his handwork, his fasting and his poverty. But crowds came to Anthony for healing and for a blessing, and Hilarion, wanting a solitude without noise, took his blessing and went home. He found his parents dead, divided what they left between his kinsfolk and the poor, kept nothing, and settled alone in the desert about seven stadia from Maiuma of Gaza, between the sea and the lake, where there were robbers.

He became known in all Palestine, and people came to him in their troubles. A barren woman of Eleutheropolis was the first; a woman blind ten years, who had spent everything on physicians, he healed as his Lord had done. Many left the world to live near him, and so, the life says, he was the first guide of monks in Gaza and in Palestine, as Anthony was in Egypt. At sixty-three he had a multitude of brethren about him.

Because a lamp cannot be hidden, he went away again and again: into the Libyan desert with his disciple Zeno, by ship from Paraetonium to Sicily, from there to Epidaurus in Dalmatia, where he stilled the sea, and at last to Cyprus with his disciples. He would take nothing from anyone, and lived on a bundle of firewood a day, sold for a piece of bread.

At eighty, knowing he was going, he wrote his testament with his own hand and left his disciple Hesychius the Gospel he had copied himself, his tunic and his cowl. As he died he said: "Go out, my soul, what dost thou fear? Eighty years thou hast served Christ, and dost thou fear death?" He was buried in the garden where he lived, as he had asked. Hesychius came from Palestine, mourned at the grave, waited ten months, opened it secretly, found the body as though newly dead, and carried it home to the monastery at Maiuma. So, the life ends, the Cypriots said they had his spirit and the Palestinians his body, and wonders were worked in both places.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 21 October, and saint.gr adds the end of the story. After he had travelled through many places and countries, it says, he gave up his soul to God at eighty years of age, having spent the last five years of his life in the bishopric of Paphos. His relics did not stay long in Cyprus: when the Christians of Palestine learned of his death they sent his disciple Hesychius, who contrived to open the grave, took the holy relics without anyone noticing and carried them to Palestine, where they were laid with particular honours in the monastery of Maïouma. The page also keeps one of his desert conversations. Robbers once asked him what he would do if thieves came upon him, and he answered, «Τί έχει να φοβηθεί ο γυμνός;» — what has a naked man to fear? And when they said, but if they killed you? he answered that it would be so much the better, since the death of the body closes the night of this present life and brings one into the dawn of the life to come; and the page says those answers brought the robbers to repentance.

The Russian calendar keeps him on the same day and dates his death forty years earlier: its line reads «Прп. Илариона Великого (371-372)», where the Greek calendar's own arithmetic — born 333, dead at eighty — gives 413. Both readings are set down in this folder and neither is chosen. The day page carries three hymns for him, two troparia and a kontakion, and the second troparion likens his gift of wonders to Anthony the Great's and calls him a settler of the desert cities and a support of monks.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 21 October and opens on an image of its own: as a rose grows up among thorns, so this great saint was born of pagan parents, in the village of Tavatha near Gaza in Palestine. His parents sent him to school in Alexandria, where the gifted youth quickly took in both worldly learning and spiritual wisdom. Having come to know Christ the Lord and been baptised, he longed to give himself up wholly to the Lord’s service.

With that desire in his heart he visited [Anthony the Great](/saints/anthony-the-great) in the desert and became his disciple. Then he returned to his own country and struggled near Maiuma, by Gaza. Demons frightened him with all kinds of apparitions, but he always conquered them and drove them off with prayer to God and the sign of the cross. A multitude of lovers of the spiritual life gathered about him, and Hilarion became for Palestine what Anthony had been for Egypt.

A divine teacher, an unflagging ascetic, a wonderful wonderworker, he was honoured not only by Christians but by pagans. But fearing the praise of men — and saying through tears, woe is me, I have received my reward on earth — he fled from place to place only to hide himself from people and be alone with his own soul and with God. So he settled and lived for a time in Egypt, in Sicily, in Dalmatia, and at last in Cyprus, where he ended his much-labouring life, around 372, in his eightieth year — neither of which figures this folder records, the one being approximate and the other an age. His wonderworking relics were carried back to Palestine by his disciple Hesychius, who laid them in the monastery Hilarion himself had founded.
