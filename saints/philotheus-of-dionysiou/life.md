# Philotheus of Dionysiou

Philotheus was born in the Macedonian city of Chrysopolis, of parents from Elatia in Asia who had fled the Turks. His father died there, leaving a widow and two sons with no kin in a strange place, mocked by their neighbours as foreigners. When the Turkish levy of children came, both boys were taken and shut up in prison — though the law did not allow both sons of one house to be taken.

The Mother of God delivered them. She appeared to them at night in the likeness of their mother, told them to rise and follow her, opened the prison doors at a word, and brought them by matins to a monastery near Neapolis, where she bade them be tonsured and obey the abbot until she should come again. The abbot understood, gave them to an elder to be taught, and made them sacristans. Their mother, knowing nothing of them, was tonsured in the women's monastery nearby under the name Eudocia, and recognised her sons by their voices at a feast years later.

A nun conceived a passion for Philotheus and would not leave him in peace; he answered her as another Joseph, and at last told the abbot, who drove her out. Not bearing the honour the brotherhood then paid him, he asked leave to go, and when it was refused he went anyway, and came after some days to the Holy Mountain and to the monastery of Dionysiou, where the abbot received him into the common life.

After long years there he wished for solitude, and feigned an illness that left him deaf and dumb so that the abbot would let him go. Six miles into the wilderness he built a little hut and lived on bread and water, and often ate only on Saturday and Sunday. The devil came to him in the shape of a shipwrecked man and pushed him into a ravine, and he was not hurt; but his pretended deafness was found out, and he moved to another place, where he took three disciples in the image of the Trinity. When three boats of Turks came to his shore, he made the sign of the Cross and prayed, and a storm rose that scattered them.

He was given also the gift of foresight: at a great feast at Vatopedi he saw a raven going before the candles at the great entrance and knew that one of the priests was unworthy to serve, and kept him from the Liturgy, after which the raven was seen no more. He died at the age of eighty-four, having charged his disciples not to bury him but to throw his body into the wilderness for the beasts and birds. They obeyed, and dragged the relics nine stadia into the forest; but a monk coming back from fishing before daybreak saw a great light there, and found the skull above the bones.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 21 October, and saint.gr tells how he came to the Mountain. He was born in 1526 at Chrysoupolis in Macedonia, near Kavala, to parents who had fled there from Elateia in Asia Minor for fear of the Agarenes; his father died after fathering two sons, and the Turks seized both boys, so that their mother Eudokia, to lighten a grief she could not carry, shut herself up in a women's monastery. At the festival of a men's monastery she met her two sons again, and heard the story of their rescue from the abbot's own lips. Theophilos — that was his first name — showed great eagerness in that house and was tonsured a monk as Philotheos; and in 1551, at twenty-five, he went to the Holy Mountain and to the coenobium of Dionysiou, where his ascetic labours became an example to many of the brothers. Seeking more quiet he pretended to have fallen ill and lost his hearing, and withdrew to a cave outside the monastery; when the pretence was found out he had to move again so as not to be honoured, and in the new place he gained three disciples and the gift of foresight. He died in peace at eighty-four, having told his disciples not to bury his body but to leave it to the beasts of the forest; God lit it with a light, by which a monk was led to it, and his skull was given to the disciples and is kept to this day at the monastery of Petra in Thessaliotis in a silver case.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 21 October and gives him a childhood the other books read for this folder do not. He was born at Chrysopolis in Macedonia. The Turks took him away from his widowed mother and threw him into prison together with a brother of his; and the most holy Theotokos saved them out of that prison miraculously and carried them to a monastery at Neapolis in Asia Minor. Their mother later found her sons again as monks, and was tonsured herself.

Philotheus crossed to the Holy Mountain, where he struggled first in the monastery of Dionysiou and afterwards in the desert: a wonderful ascetic, the book says, and a great conqueror of the power of the demons. He fell asleep in peace in the eighty-fourth year of his life — an age, not a year, and it happens to agree exactly with the span this folder already records.

And he gave orders that he should not be buried, but that his body should be thrown into the forest for the birds and the beasts. Later a fisherman saw a great light in that forest at night and, being cold, went towards it to warm himself, thinking it was a fire. The light was not from a fire. It was from the wonderworking relics of Saint Philotheus.
