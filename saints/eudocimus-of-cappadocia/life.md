# Eudocimus of Cappadocia

The Romanian life of Eudocimus opens by saying why it is worth telling: not because he loved virtue ardently, as many have done in the desert, but because he did it while living in the cares of the world, carried like a ship in the middle of the sea and beset by the devil's devices, and yet kept himself untroubled by every passion of anger and his soul clean of the passions of desire down to the finest thought. He was of Cappadocia, born and brought up by believing parents: his father Basil, a man of rank, wealth and office, holding the dignity of patrician, and his mother Eudocia.

Set to his letters as a boy, he needed neither the fear of his teachers nor their blows to drive him to study, but pressed on of his own nature, day and night, above all at the reading of the divine Scriptures; and his walking abroad was to go to the churches and hear the services and the words of God. When boys of his own age were urged to hunting and feasting and amusements, his one pleasure was to pray and to read what profits the soul — and not as those who pray with their minds elsewhere, but with his whole mind fixed on the words he was thinking on.

He loved chastity above the other virtues, and settled it with himself that he would not look on a woman's face as long as he lived; so that, except his mother, he never in his life looked another woman or girl in the face, nor spoke with one. To chastity he joined almsgiving with a wholly cheerful face, and gave away even what he needed for his own living, not money only but whatever anyone had need of, so that he was called father of orphans, governor of widows, clothing of the naked, filling of the hungry and comfort of the grieved.

For his virtues he was given, against his will, an imperial dignity, and was appointed commander over the army of Cappadocia in the province called Charsianon. He did not use the office for his own honour and profit, as the life says most men do, but took all his care to govern small and great with reverence and justice. So he lived until he came to the blessed end of this present life, in Cappadocia, at thirty-three years of age — young in years, the life says, but very old in judgement and understanding.

Knowing his end, he was not troubled, for he had thought of nothing all his life but death; the one thing that grieved him was that his mother was far off. When the last hour came he spoke to those who visited him about the remembrance of death, and made them swear by God to bury him in the very clothes he was wearing and to do none of the things that are usually done for the dead. Then he made a sign, and when they had all gone out he prayed, and some who heard him reported it afterwards: Lord my God, as I did not want my way of life to be seen while I lived, so I pray that my end may be without any gift, and that no one may think that I have pleased You. Then he said, Lord, into Your hands I give my spirit, and his soul went out; and those present, in reverence for his command, left him not only his clothes but the bed of split wood he had slept on, and put it into the grave with him.

But the sun could not be hidden in a cloud. A few days after his death a man named Elias, who had a terrible demon, passed that way, and as soon as he touched the grave the demon threw him to the ground like a dead man and went out of him. The report of it spread, and crowds brought their sick and were healed: a child paralysed in both hands, another in hands and feet, women with unclean spirits, a woman who mixed earth from the grave with her tears and anointed her sore and was made well. The oil of the lamp at the grave healed, and earth from it was carried to the sick who were far off.

Hearing of the wonders, his parents were consoled in their grief, and his mother set out on the long road to the grave. There she embraced it, wept, and spoke to him half in lament and half in praise, calling herself no longer the mother of the fruit of her womb but a woman born of her son in the spirit, and asking him to make her a friend of his heavenly Father. Then she had the stone raised and the coffin brought out: eighteen months after the burial the relics were whole. A hieromonk named Joseph who lifted them to set them upright found them stand as though alive, and in fear fell at the saint's feet and asked leave, as of a living man, to take his clothes for devotion; and they came away as easily as if the saint helped him.

Afterwards there was a disagreement between the saint's mother, who wanted to take the relics to Constantinople saying that this star had risen from her womb, and the people of the place, who answered that it had risen from her but shone upon them. She went home quietly, as a prudent woman. Many days later that same hieromonk Joseph found his chance, stole the treasure and fled; but he was betrayed on the road by the myrrh that flowed from the relics and by the wonders worked along the way, a possessed woman delivered and a girl with a fearful affliction healed. So the precious gift came to the saint's parents, and his mother made a silver reliquary for the relics and set it in the beautiful church of the Most Holy Theotokos which she had built before, and there too, the life says, countless wonders were worked every day.
