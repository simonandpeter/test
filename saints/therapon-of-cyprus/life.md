# Therapon of Cyprus

Doxologia's life of Therapon begins by saying that almost nothing about him can be told. Where he was from, what family he came of, what parents bore him, in what age he lived, against whom he fought and how he won, and in what way he received the crown of martyrdom — none of it can be said, because the memory of him was lost with time. The Romanian calendar names him for 14 mai as a hieromartyr and bishop in Cyprus, and the page is honest that the little it gives rests on an old oral tradition and not on a written life.

What the page does hold to is this. That he chose the monastic life is shown, it says, by his icons, which depict him in that habit and schema. That he was bishop of the island of Cyprus, that he was brought to Christ through blood, and that he finished the struggle of the contest — these were taken from an old story handed down by tradition without being written, and as it was taught by those who came before, the page says, so we believe it.

His honoured relics were brought to Constantinople. The Agarenes were taking counsel to fall upon the island of Cyprus, and the saint himself gave the order by a revelation that he should be moved; and there, where he now lies, he works wonders continually for those who run to him with faith.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same civil day, and what its page is most careful about is what it does not know. Where Therapon came from it does not know with certainty: some make him out of the East, «ἐκ τῆς Ἑῴας», as Andrew of Crete notes, and others out of the land of the Alamanni, that is a region of present-day Germany. The exact year of his birth it says it is entirely ignorant of; from what the various synaxaria give — his action against the heresy of theopaschism and against iconoclasm — it reasons that he lived in the seventh century or the beginning of the eighth, but not later than 740, the year Andrew of Crete, the Jerusalemite, fell asleep, who towards the end of his own life spoke a fine encomium in his honour. Hagiography, the page adds, records him as belonging to the order of monks, those who truly renounce their own will and carry the Lord's cross gladly.

Tradition has it that he lived a while at Jerusalem and then became bishop of Kition in Cyprus, where he went on working wonders and shepherding the people until his life ended in martyrdom: Arab pirates cut him down on the holy table while he was celebrating. His honoured relic was carried to Constantinople, first to the church of the Mother of God at Blachernae and then to a church bearing his name, and inside the walls of the city there is still a small shrine which streams myrrh and works wonders for those who come to it in sincere faith. Portions of his relics are kept at the coenobitic monastery of Zographou on the Holy Mountain, at the church of Saint Therapon in Zografou of Athens — whose patron he is — and at his church in Mytilene. The service in his honour was written by Nikodemos the Hagiorite and corrected in 1785 by the archdeacon Agapios of Paros.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 25 May, not on the 14 May the other two calendars keep, and it is much the fullest account of the journey of his relics. A monk and an ascetic on the island of Cyprus, it says, he was counted worthy of the rank of bishop, and then in a time of persecution of the Christians was counted worthy of a greater crown still, the crown of martyrdom; and his body rested in a church on Cyprus.

When, in the time of the emperor Nicephorus, in the year 806, the Agarenes fell upon the island of Cyprus, the saint appeared to the warden of that church, told him that the unbelievers would attack Cyprus, and ordered him to carry his relics to Constantinople. The warden did it at once. When the ship with the reliquary set out over the sea a great storm rose up, but around the ship the sea was still; and out of the reliquary a sweet fragrance poured over the whole ship and all about it.

Then the warden opened the reliquary, and all saw it filled with myrrh flowing from the relics of the saint. Many sick people were anointed with that myrrh and were made well. At Constantinople a church was built over these wonderworking relics, which went on giving healings to all who came to them with faith; and by the grace of God the gravest sick were healed at the relics of Saint Therapon — of palsy, of cancer, of haemorrhage, of madness, of blindness, of withering, and of various other torments.
