# Marius of Persia, husband of Martha

Marius and his wife [Martha](/saints/martha-wife-of-marius) were Persians, born at the beginning of the third century of noble and wealthy families; doxologia sets them beside the three magi as the offering that the old land between the Tigris and the Euphrates brought to Christ, and counts them among the earliest martyrs of Persia, though they suffered outside it. They were baptised, married, and had two sons, [Audifax](/saints/audifax-son-of-marius) and [Abachum](/saints/abachum-son-of-marius), whom they raised in the faith.

The family loved the martyrs and honoured their relics, and it was that love, the page says, which moved them to leave their earthly homeland and go west to Rome, to venerate the tombs of the apostles Peter and Paul and of the other martyrs. They came during a short lull in the persecutions, and after kissing the holy relics they said, Lord, it is good for us to be here, and resolved to stay in the city. Soon a fresh persecution came upon the Roman church, and upon the four Persians with it.

They cared for Quirinus, who lay wounded in prison, and helped the priest John to bury the bodies of two hundred and sixty Christians and of Blastus the tribune, shot with arrows and burnt: they drew the bodies out of the fire, wrapped them in clean linen and buried them in the Cucumer catacomb on the Via Salaria. Then, though soldiers were looking for them, they went by night and found the body of Quirinus, killed and thrown into the river, and buried it honourably in the catacomb of Pontian.

For several months they stayed hidden. In that time the priest Valentine brought the official Asterius and his whole household to faith in Christ, and Marius with his wife and sons went to Asterius's house to strengthen him and remained there thirty-two days; and at the emperor's order all of them were taken and imprisoned. Asterius and his household, forty-six Christians, men and women, were sent to Ostia and put to death there, some by the sword and some by stoning, and Valentine, left at Rome, was beaten and then beheaded.

The four were tried by Flavius, prefect of Rome, and the governor Muscian, who tried flattery and then threats to make them sacrifice. Marius and the two boys refused, and were beaten, torn with iron claws and had their hands cut off, saying nothing but, Glory to You, Lord Jesus Christ. In the end all four were condemned. The three men were beheaded on the road called Via Cornelia; Martha, who had never ceased urging them to endure, was drowned in a pool near the Nymphaeum.

From the names in the story — Bishop Callistus of Rome, who held the see from 217 to 222, and the emperor Claudius II the Goth, who reigned from 268 to 270 — doxologia reckons that the four came to Rome about the year 220 and died about 270. Their bodies were taken by a faithful woman named Felicitas and buried on her farm called Buxus, today Boccea, where a church was later built; the relics are now in several churches in Rome and in Germany, and fragments are honoured at the seat of the Romanian Orthodox Diocese of Northern Europe in Stockholm.

Of Marius the page says that he was beheaded on the Via Cornelia with his two sons, after his hands had been struck off, and that he gave thanks to Christ under every torment.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 6 July, the same menologion day the Romanian calendar keeps him on, and it states a year where the Romanian page reasoned one out: all of them suffered in the time of the emperor Claudius Flavius at Rome, it says, in the year 269, where doxologia inferred about 270 from the overlap of two reigns. The folder’s date now holds both. Marin and Marta were rich people out of Persia, this book says, who sold everything they had there and came with their sons to Rome, to venerate the relics of the holy apostles and of the other martyrs.

And when the emperor asked them why they had come from such a distance, leaving their own household gods, to look for dead men at Rome, they answered: we are servants of Christ, and we came to venerate the holy apostles, whose immortal souls live with God, that they might be our intercessors before Christ our God. Neither of the other books has the question or the answer.

The heading of this entry names the two of them and then runs on in the body with their sons and a presbyter Valentine, a Quirinus, an Asterius and many others, none of whom this reading writes a Serbian row for. One act in that list is the two parents’ own, and it belongs here: Quirinus was thrown into the Tiber, and Marin and Marta drew his body out of the river and buried it honourably. The rest of the entry is the company’s and not theirs — Valentine healing a commander’s daughter who had been blind two years and baptising him and his whole house — and all of them, this book ends, received torments and death for Christ the Lord in various ways.
