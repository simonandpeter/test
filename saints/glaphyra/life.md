# Glaphyra

Glaphyra was an Italian by family, honourable and believing, and a Christian; and she served in the palace of Constantia, the sister of the emperor Constantine and wife of Licinius, who is called in the life a believing and Christian empress. The life describes her as a maiden very beautiful and of a full good sense. Licinius saw her, was inflamed with an unclean desire, and ordered Venegnus, the chief of his chamberlains, to carry her the word.

Venegnus brought it as though it were a great gift — that the emperor loved her and would have her, and that she was to make herself ready. She was filled with the fear of God, was sickened at such a sin, drove the messenger out with dishonour and rebuked the lawlessness; then, fearing the hatred and jealousy of the empress, she went and told her the whole of it, begging her by God, whom she feared and whom her brother Constantine served, not to let her virginity be lost in a lawless union.

The empress loved her the more for her chastity, and took counsel with the fear of God how she might hide her. When the emperor asked after her, she had it given out through the palace that Glaphyra had lost her mind, that she lay sick and was near death; and the emperor left off thinking of her. Waiting her time, the empress set her free with much gold and silver, precious stones, costly ornaments and clothing, and gave her faithful men and women servants, as many as she would need.

She entrusted her to honourable and believing men of her own household and bade them take her into the parts of Armenia and tell no one anything of her, and stay there until the Lord should order what was good for her. So Glaphyra and the maidens with her were dressed in men's clothes and went out of Nicomedia; and going a long way they came near Armenia, and reached the city of Amasea, which was the metropolis of the country of Pontus.

Seeing the beauty of the city she said to her servants that if they found a Christian people there they would stay, and bade them ask after the Christians. They met a young man of the household of an honourable citizen named Quintius, who, seeing that these strangers were Christians and were looking for their own, ran to them himself, took them into his house and told them that their city had many Christians and a bishop like the apostles. The bishop, [Basileus](/saints/basileus-of-amasea), came to them, and Glaphyra told him the whole secret of her flight.

He and Quintius charged her that neither she nor her servants should go out of the house or speak to anyone, lest the governor learn of her and every Christian in Amasea be endangered; and Basileus said that her flight and exile would be to the glory of God. He was then building a church inside the city, for until then the Christians had had only a very small one outside it. Glaphyra gave him much silver for it and spent on that building everything the empress had given her, keeping nothing back.

She wrote to the empress, her mistress, to say where she was and with whom, and of the building of the church, and to ask for more gold to finish and adorn it; and the empress sent her much wealth and gifts for the church and the bishop, and wrote commending her servant to him. But Venegnus the chamberlain found that letter, read it, learned that Glaphyra was alive — whom they had thought dead — and where she was, and told Licinius.

The emperor in great anger wrote to the governor of Amasea to put the bishop and the servant in irons and send them quickly to him at Nicomedia. By the providence of God, before the letter reached the governor, Glaphyra had gone to the Lord. The governor seized only Basileus, bound him and sent him to the emperor, and reported of Glaphyra that she was dead. The life gives no year for her death, and dates her only by the reign of Licinius in the East.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, and saint.gr's three sentences differ from the Romanian in who moved her and whose money built the church. She was a maidservant of the empress Constantia, wife of Licinius, and it was the empress who removed her out of Licinius's amorous designs, having furnished her with a great deal of money. She went towards the East and wandered in many places, and came at last to Amasea, where she presented herself to [Basileus](/saints/basileus-of-amasea) the bishop of that city and handed over her money to him for the raising of a church; and she died peacefully at Amasea. Her couplet says that Glaphyra now sees God through the eyes of the soul, not obscurely but in a far-shining light — a play on her name, which in Greek means the polished or the subtle.

The Serbian church keeps her on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović puts her inside the entry it heads for [Basileus](/saints/basileus-of-amasea): the heading names the bishop alone, and the body is where this book tells her part. Licinius, given over to idolatry and without measure to all the passions, set out under that assault to defile „девицу Глафиру“, the maiden who stood in the service of the empress. She complained to her mistress, and the empress sent her secretly out of the imperial court at Nicomedia into the country of Pontus.

The maiden reached the city of Amasea and was warmly received there by the bishop Basileus and the rest of the Christians. She was very glad that God had saved her in her virginity and wrote of it to the empress; and the empress rejoiced and sent her money for the church at Amasea. But one of her letters to the empress fell into the hands of an imperial eunuch, who showed it to the emperor; and Licinius, learning where she was, sent at once that she and the bishop both be brought to Nicomedia. „У међувремену умре Глафира“ — in the meantime Glaphyra died, so the soldiers brought the bishop alone. **This book gives no year for her death either**, so the folder stands undated as the other two calendars left it.

The Russian calendar keeps her on this day too, and gives the year the other three withhold: she died in 322, before the emperor's order to send her and the bishop to him had reached Amasea. Its life is one page headed for the two of them together, and it turns three things differently. Licinius's wife is named «Констанция», and it is she herself who dressed Glaphyra in a man's clothes, gave her money, and sent her out of the city with servants of her own household she could trust, putting it about that the maidservant had gone out of her mind and lay at the point of death. The money was spent to the last coin on the church the bishop was then building, and it was for more of it that she wrote to the empress, who sent it. And it was that letter of hers, and no eunuch's interception of the answer, that came into the emperor's hands.
