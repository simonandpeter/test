# Joseph Gerontogiannis

saint.gr says John Vitsentzos, called Gerontogiannis, was born in 1799 in the half-ruined small monastery of the Honourable Forerunner of Kapsa, in eastern Crete, where his pious parents Emmanuel and Zampia had gone because of a Turkish raid; later, when things quieted, they settled in the village of Lithines. Of age, he married Kalliope, of the family of the Gerontakides, who was herself hiding on the south-eastern shore for fear of the fate of her only sister, a girl who had killed herself rather than be dishonoured by a Turk; they had four children, three daughters and a son.

Gerontogiannis was unruly, the page says, but particularly pious, and often a target of the Turkish authorities. One Sunday he loaded firewood on his beast to sell it in the villages and buy wine, taking his wife as far as Lithines and leaving the children alone at the farmstead. Coming back they found their small daughter Irene burnt out on the threshing floor, where the other children had carried her thinking the wind would put out the fire that had caught her dress. He took the child's death as a divine punishment for his sins and above all for breaking the Sunday rest, and the page says that event sealed his life and was the occasion of his transformation.

He left the farmstead and settled at Lithines, and those who knew him saw the change daily: the hard-hearted, quick-tempered and quarrelsome John became long-suffering, merciful, gentle and free of malice. In 1841, at forty-two, he fell into a deep sleep in which, the page says, an angel of the Lord caught him up as it did the Apostle Paul, and he saw the ranks of the righteous in heavenly glory and the punishments of the condemned. After forty-three hours he woke calm and joyful before a crowd of kinsmen and neighbours, stretched out his hand over a paralysed old woman among them, whispered a prayer and healed her; and from then on he preached and worked wonders.

The noise of it made him suspect to the Turkish authorities, who were told that the gatherings at his house were revolutionary under a religious pretext, though in truth, the page says, those around him were the sick and the crippled, who came for relief and comfort and healing. Three times he was called to answer before Mustafa Pasha, governor of Crete, and each journey had the opposite effect, since in every village he passed through a crowd gathered to greet him and take his blessing.

He kept, the page says, the inner state of stillness, and counselled, strengthened and healed all who were sick, out of a boundless love towards suffering men after the pattern of the Lord. It was widely known that he blessed sea water with the cross and it became sweet; that he would throw his cassock on the sea and use it as a raft to cross for stillness to Koufonisi, some miles from the monastery; and that he had the gift of foresight, so that he picked out the stolen goods pilgrims sometimes brought him as gifts and told those who brought them to take them back.

Long and more than human asceticism soon wore out his sickly body, and his voice thinned until those present could barely hear him; his last months he kept to his cell, bedridden. He foresaw his death, called the whole brotherhood to his cell to ask their forgiveness and give his last counsels, left the monk Ananias his successor, foretold what would happen to the monastery after him, and named the exact day and hour. On 6 August 1874, having communicated at the hands of his grandson Joseph, then a deacon, and the hieromonk Gennadios, he asked forgiveness again, made the sign of the cross, lay down on his right side and gave up his soul.

His body became a popular pilgrimage and lay unburied three days while crowds filled the monastery, and he was buried on 9 August 1874 in the church of the Honourable Forerunner, in a carved stone tomb made by that grandson, afterwards abbot of the monastery, who later raised the honourable skull and laid it above the tomb. Pilgrims venerated the skull and his icon and took earth from the grave as a blessing, and were healed. The translation of his relics was made on 7 May 1982, a hundred and eight years after his repose, after an all-night vigil, and its memory is kept on the Tuesday into the Wednesday of Renewal Week; the relics were laid with the honourable skull in a silver reliquary in a conspicuous place in the church.
