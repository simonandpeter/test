# Mercurius

Decius and Valerian, the pagan emperors of Rome, called together all their lords and took counsel how to spread the honour of their gods and to destroy the Christian faith; and with one mind they agreed and gave out an edict in the capital. It commanded every order of the people, free men and slaves, soldiers and civilians, to sacrifice to the gods; whoever dared to break it was to be thrown bound into prison and then killed with various torments, and one found to be of the Christian faith was to be tortured so above all — while those who obeyed would inherit great honour and gifts.

The whole city was troubled when that godless order was proclaimed and sent out to all the cities and their districts. In that time the barbarians rose against the empire, and the emperors made their armies ready to go out to war, ordering the troops of every city to gather at Rome; and among the soldiers was Mercurius, whose father Gordian had been a soldier in the same cohort in which he now served.

Decius went out to the war, Valerian stayed at Rome, and the fighting and slaughter between the barbarians and the Romans went on a long time. Then the angel of the Lord appeared to Mercurius in the shape of a great man dressed in white garments, holding a sword in his hand, and said: *Do not fear, Mercurius, and do not be afraid, for I am sent to help you and to make you a victor. Take this sword and go against the barbarians; and when you have beaten them, do not forget the Lord your God.*

He was frightened, and thought the one who had appeared was one of the lords of Rome. Taking the sword he charged the enemy and cut them down like grass, and going through the barbarian ranks as far as their king he killed him with the sword, and a great number of brave soldiers with him, so that his hand stuck to the hilt with blood; and so the barbarians were beaten and driven off. Decius, seeing his great courage, called him, honoured him with great gifts and set him commander over all the armies, thinking that the gods had given the victory.

One night, while Mercurius slept, the angel came to him in the same shape as before, struck him in the side and woke him; and he was frightened and struck dumb. The angel said: *Mercurius, do you no longer remember what I told you in the battle? See that you do not forget the Lord your God, for you must suffer for him and receive the crown of victory with all the saints.* Then he became invisible.

Coming to himself, Mercurius gave thanks to God and remembered the Christian faith he had heard of from his grandfather and his father. For Gordian had often said: *Blessed is he who soldiers for the King of heaven, for he will receive his reward from him in the heavenly kingdom; for that King made all things by his word alone, and will judge the living and the dead and reward each according to his works.* Remembering those words and weighing the angel's appearing, he was filled with joy, and began to weep and lament: *Woe to me, a sinner, that being the branch of a green tree I have dried up, having now no root of the knowledge of God.*

When the emperor's servants came to call him to council he said that he was ill, and Decius put the council off to another day. Then a lord told the emperor that his counsellor Mercurius, whom the goodness of the empire had raised, was renouncing the gods and would not bow to them. Decius sent for him with honour and asked whether he had not honoured him, setting him commander over all the lords; and the saint answered so boldly that the emperor was astonished, and looked at him in silence, marvelling at his words and at the beauty of his body, for he was tall of stature.

The next day Decius sat in judgement and had him set before him like a condemned man, and asked why he would not do their will and keep the command given to all men. When the saint said he had the shield and breastplate of faith, the emperor was filled with anger and ordered him hung up naked between four forked stakes. That done, he had him untied lest he die too quickly, and shut up in a house; the servants carried him, for he could not walk and was barely alive.

The next day the soldiers who went to fetch him found him whole, and brought him to the emperor, who saw him healthy and had him tortured again. He was beaten and burned with fire, and a sweet fragrance came out of his burnt body; and the martyr endured so bravely that he did not cry out, did not groan, did not so much as sigh, at which all who saw it marvelled.

Seeing that he would not bend, and having no more time to torture him because he was hastening back to Rome, the emperor gave sentence against him. The soldiers set him on a beast and bound him there, his body being very weak from the many torments, and took him into Cappadocia; and when he came to Caesarea the Lord appeared to him and said: *Mercurius, come to me and rest. You have finished the course and kept the faith, so receive the crown of your struggle.* At those words the soldiers cut off his head, in a good confession of our Saviour Jesus Christ, on the twenty-fifth day of the month of November.

Afterwards the Most Holy Theotokos appointed this holy soldier to a wonderful service of arms. At the prayer of [Basil the Great](/saints/basil-the-great) she sent him, spear in hand as a soldier, that the ill-believing emperor Julian the Apostate, a great persecutor and destroyer of the Orthodox, might not come back from the Persian war to the ruin of the Christian faith. Basil saw the icon of Saint Mercurius, which stood beside the icon of the Theotokos, become invisible for a little while and then appear again with the spear bloodied.

At that very time, in the far-off war, Julian was run through by an unknown soldier who vanished as soon as he had struck; and the wretched man took blood out of his wound and threw it up towards heaven, speaking blasphemies against Christ, and said: *You have conquered, Galilean.*

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 25 November, and gives him his family and his promotion. saint.gr makes him a brave soldier, not only of the army of the emperor Decius, in which he served, but of Christ: he came from Scythia and his father was called Gordianus. Distinguishing himself in some battle in which he killed the opposing general, he was honoured with the rank of commander-in-chief. But when he was called on to take part in the idolatrous sacrifices he refused, and when he was asked did not hesitate to confess that he was a Christian. The king tried at first with sweet words to change his mind; and seeing that he was failing, he ordered him tortured. So they filled him with wounds and burnt his flesh badly, and then beheaded him at Caesarea of Cappadocia, between the years 253 and 259.
