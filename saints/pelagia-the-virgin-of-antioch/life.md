# Pelagia the Virgin of Antioch

saint.gr's calendar for 8 Οκτωβρίου keeps two women of one name, and this is the second of them. Its page heads her «Αγία Πελαγία η Παρθένος (; - 284)» and says that she came of a glorious family of Antioch in Syria, in the years of the emperor Numerian, 282 to 284. When the ruler of Antioch learned that Pelagia was a Christian he sent soldiers to arrest her, and they surrounded her house and made ready to seize her.

Learning of it, she asked the soldiers to wait a little. Then she raised her hands and her eyes to heaven and prayed fervently that God would not let the soldiers take her, but that she might leave this life pure and a virgin. Then she opened the window and threw herself into the void, and was mortally injured, and so gave up her pure soul to God, kept from the defilement of the rough soldiers. saint.gr adds that Saint John Chrysostom wrote a celebrated encomium for her.

The other Pelagia the day keeps is the penitent of Antioch, who is the corpus's [Pelagia of Antioch](/saints/pelagia-of-antioch); the two are separate entries on the same day page, with separate lives, and this folder is the virgin. saint.gr prints no hymn for her, and gives her no place of burial. The page carries no asterisk and not the sentence «Δεν είναι διασταυρωμένη η ύπαρξη του Αγίου», so the site vouches for the entry as it stands.

The Russian calendar keeps her on its own 8 October, beside the other Pelagia of Antioch, and gives her a line of four words: «Св. Пелагии девы (303)» — holy Pelagia the virgin, 303. The year is not saint.gr's: the Greek page puts her under Numerian and dates her to 284, and this folder's interval now carries both and chooses neither. The day line marks her «Св.» where her own index entry heads her «мч.», martyr, which is one more of this site's small disagreements with itself.

Her name page carries no life. It sends the reader to an article on the site's main pages instead, and what that article gives is the moment of her death rather than her history: knowing that the rough soldiers would attempt the virginity she had consecrated to the Lord, she went to the place in the house where she was used to pray, raised her hands to heaven, washed her face with abundant tears, and begged God to deliver her from that shame, so as to stand before him undefiled in soul and in body.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her on the Julian 8 October as the day’s third entry, and is the book that tells the two Pelagias apart by their rank words: the first entry is venerable and this one is a martyr. It calls her a virgin of notable family from Antioch, and puts the arrest in the time of the emperor Numerian, as the Greek page does. The governor of Antioch sent soldiers to bring her to trial as a Christian of some notoriety; they surrounded the house and called her out to the door, and when she heard why they had come she made herself cheerful and asked them to wait a moment or two while she went up to change her clothes.

She went up on the roof of the house instead, raised her hands to heaven and prayed a long while that God would receive her soul and not suffer her virgin purity to be defiled. And God received her soul, and her body fell dead before the soldiers. **In this account she does not throw herself down at all**, where the Greek page has her open a window and cast herself into the void and be mortally injured; the two books differ, and both readings stand here as what each says.

Last, the book prints two sentences of the encomium that the Greek page only says [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom) wrote for her. Her death, he writes, was the instrument not of a natural fall but of the commandment of God; and so this virgin body, purer than any gold, lay upon the earth, with angels about it, archangels honouring it, and Christ himself beside it.
