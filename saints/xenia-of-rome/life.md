# Xenia of Rome

There was at old Rome a famous man, one of the greatest of the senators, an Orthodox Christian with one daughter only, whom he kept as the light of his eyes; her name was Eusebia. When she came of age to marry, a nobleman of equal rank asked her of her parents for his son, and they betrothed her and set the wedding day. But the girl wanted to be the bride of the incorruptible Bridegroom by a lifelong virginity, and hid that wish from her parents, knowing that they would never have allowed it. She had two faithful servants who had grown up with her from childhood, and she bound them by an oath and took them into her secret.

The three left home together. On the road they found an old man and fell at his feet, begging him not to overlook strangers in a far country, but to be to them what the apostle Paul had been to Thecla. He answered that he too was a stranger there, coming back from the holy places; that he was from Mylasa in Caria, and was no bishop, as she had guessed, but a presbyter and abbot of a small brotherhood in the monastery of the apostle Andrew, and that his name was Paul. He took the three virgins with him to Mylasa, and there, with the gold she had brought from home, Eusebia bought houses in a quiet place near the church, built a small church in the name of the great martyr Stephen, and soon set up a little convent of virgins.

Paul tonsured her and both her servants with his own hands, and nobody knew to the end of her life where she came from, why she had left her country, or what her true name was; she was called Xenia, which is to say the stranger. When people asked, Paul said only that he had taken them from the island of Cos and brought them there, so that everyone supposed them to be from Cos and the convent itself came to be called Cos after the island. When Cyril, the bishop of the city, died, Paul was chosen in his place, and as bishop he came to the convent and ordained Xenia deaconess as one truly worthy of it.

At her burial all the people saw a crown of stars with a cross in the sky above the bier, going where the bier went and standing still when it stood, until she was laid in a new tomb at the place called Sichinie, at the southern entrance of the city, where she had asked beforehand to be put; and then the crown was seen no more. The people divided among themselves the fine cloths that had lain on her body, and many healings were given at her grave. Both her servants died soon after and were buried at their mistress's feet; and it was the second of them, asked on her deathbed by all the nuns, who told them at last whose daughter she had been, and that her name from birth was Eusebia, changed to Xenia because she had made herself a stranger for the love of God.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and gives the century the Romanian page did not: saint.gr says she lived in the fifth century, that she was first called Eusebeia, and that she fled her parents' pressure to marry secretly to Alexandria, where she was renamed Xenia, the stranger, so that her traces would be lost. It carries her by way of the island of Kos to Mylasa in Caria, where with the two maidservants who had stayed faithful to her she built a church in the name of the protomartyr Stephen and hesychastic cells; and it adds that at her burial her holiness was owned when a cross formed of stars appeared in the sky. Her head, the page says, is at the church of the Forerunner in Kavala.
