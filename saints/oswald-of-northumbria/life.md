# Oswald of Northumbria

saint.gr says Oswald was the son of Ethelfrith, the idolatrous Anglo-Saxon king of Northumbria, who was killed fighting the invaders Edwin and Redwald. Edwin became king of Northumbria, was made a Christian with his subjects, and was honoured by his people as a saint; and through his reign Oswald and his brothers grew up exiled in Scotland, where they were baptised. After Edwin's death in 633 Oswald's two elder brothers took, Osric the kingdom of Deira and Eanfrid that of Bernicia — and both, once in power, forgot the heavenly King, went back to the idols and undid Edwin's missionary work. By Bede, whom this page follows throughout, their punishment came through the British king Cadwalla, who killed one in battle and the other by treachery.

Oswald raised a small army against Cadwalla, who had ruled Northumbria as a tyrant for a year. On the eve of the encounter he had an enormous wooden cross made and set up on the crest of a low hill overlooking the field, and before it he prayed on his knees and laid his hopes on God. With God's help he beat a far greater army and killed Cadwalla in 634, and the place of that cross was called from then on Heavenfield, the Field of Heaven. For centuries Christians would venerate there and take as a blessing small pieces of the wood, or of the moss that grew on it, and water hallowed with that wood was given to the sick; many wonders were done so. One was to a monk of nearby Hexham named Bothelm, who had broken his arm falling on ice: a brother brought him some of that moss, he tucked it inside his tunic and forgot it, and waking in the night to a coldness at his side he reached out with the injured arm and found it sound.

In 635 Oswald asked the elders of Iona in Scotland for a bishop to preach Christ in his country and to set right what his brothers' apostasy had shaken. The Scots sent the monk Corman first, who was austere by nature and failed with the Anglo-Saxons; after that a synod chose the holy monk Aidan, who was consecrated bishop. Aidan was an Irishman, and his method bore fruit at once — thousands of idolaters were baptised. Oswald gave him the island of Lindisfarne for his see, and the work of that blessed pair raised churches, endowed lands for monasteries, cultivated arts and letters and pushed back barbarous customs. Because Aidan had no English at first, the king, who had grown up in Scotland, went with him on his rounds and translated his preaching.

Oswald himself, the page says, was humble, compassionate and full of alms, though a ruler. One Easter, as he sat at table with the bishop over a worked silver dish of choice food, he was told that poor men were waiting at the palace gate; he handed the dish to an officer with orders to share out the food and then to cut the dish itself into small pieces and give those away as alms. Moved by that, Aidan took the king's right hand and cried, may this hand be always strong and glorified. By his prudence and his holiness Oswald joined the kingdoms of Deira and Bernicia; and on 5 August 642, at thirty-eight, he fell in battle against Penda, the idolatrous king of the Mercians, in defence of his faith and his country.

His end was a martyr's. While he was still praying for the souls of his bodyguard, who had all been killed, the pagans beheaded him, and by Penda's order his body was ritually cut in pieces in honour of their god Woden, and his head and both arms raised on wooden stakes on the field. A year later his brother and successor Oswy recovered the region and found the relics: the skull and the left hand had suffered the ordinary decay, but the right hand, the one Aidan had blessed, was found incorrupt. The new king laid the incorrupt right hand and what was left of the other in a silver reliquary in the church of Saint Peter at Bamburgh, and treasured the skull in the church of the monastery of Lindisfarne; the remaining relics went to the abbey of Bardney, where the Mercian monks first refused them out of racial hatred and left the wagon outside all night, until a column of light from heaven, visible across the whole province of Lindsey, stood over them till morning and the monks received them with hymns. The page tells further wonders from Bede — a horse healed on the spot where he fell, a paralysed girl who walked home from it, earth from the field that would not burn, a boy cured beside the shrine, and a sick Scottish scholar in Ireland healed by Willibrord with a splinter of the stake his head had been set on — and it ends by saying that at least sixty-seven churches have been raised in his honour.
