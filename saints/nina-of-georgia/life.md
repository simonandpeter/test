# Nina of Georgia

Nina was born at the end of the third century in Cappadocia, where many Georgians lived, and she was a near kinswoman of the Great Martyr George — his cousin, an old manuscript says — so that her reverence for him was great. Her father Zabulon was a soldier of repute and much piety who had left Cappadocia to offer his service to the emperor Maximian; her mother Susanna, also born in Cappadocia, was the sister of a bishop of Jerusalem whom some identify with Juvenal.

Susanna and her brother had been orphaned young and left without support, and had gone to Jerusalem together, where he was made steward of the Holy Sepulchre and she entered the service of the devout Sara-Nophora of Bethlehem, a deaconess there. Zabulon meanwhile came to Rome and rose to command the army. When the Franks rose against the Romans, crossed the Alps and camped along the Po, he was sent against them and beat them and took many prisoners. Before they were to be put to death they asked him to baptise them in the name of the God who had given him such strength; he baptised them, persuaded the emperor to set them free, and went back with them to preach in their own country, where for ten days the priests baptised the people on the banks of the Gadamar.

Coming afterwards to venerate the holy places he met the bishop Juvenal, and at the deaconess's advice the bishop gave him his sister Susanna to wife, and the two went home to Cappadocia. Nina was their child. At twelve she went with her parents to Jerusalem, and there her father, burning with love of God, asked leave to become a hermit; and having his wife's consent and the bishop's blessing he took leave of his daughter with tears, telling her to follow Mary Magdalene and Mary the sister of Lazarus, and that if she loved Christ as they did his grace would not leave her. Then he went into the desert of the Jordan and was seen no more, and where he laboured and died is not known. Her mother was made a deaconess of the church of the Resurrection, to care for the poor and sick women, and Nina was given to the old Nophora to be brought up.

Reading the Scriptures daily, her heart caught fire; and reading of the crucifixion she wondered where the Lord's tunic was. Nophora told her the tradition: that it lay in the city of Mtskheta in Iberia, north-east of Jerusalem, brought there by that city's rabbi Elioz, who had taken it from the soldier who won it by lot beside the cross, and that the people of that country, who call themselves Kartveli and border on the Armenians, were still in the dark of idolatry. Afterwards the Theotokos appeared to her and put into her hand a cross made of vine stems, and told her that it would be her shield and her guard against every enemy seen and unseen, and that by its power she would bring the land of the Iberians to the faith of her Son. She woke with the cross in her hands, kissed it with tears, cut a lock of her hair and bound it about it, and went to her uncle Juvenal, who knew the will of God in it plainly.

She set out alone, and in the year 315, after many hardships, reached the banks of the Mtkvari near Hertvisi. Coming up to Mtskheta she met the procession of King [Mirian](/saints/mirian-of-georgia) and Queen [Nana](/saints/nana-of-georgia) going to worship the lifeless idol Armazi. She healed the queen, who confessed before the king that Christ is the true God and made Nina the friend of her soul; the king held out, and from tolerating the Christians turned to planning their destruction and her torture, until he was struck blind on the mountain of Thoti, called on the God of Nina, received his sight, and came back and fell at her feet. He sent envoys to the emperor Constantine asking for a bishop and priests, and they came back bringing Eustathius, archbishop of Antioch, and two priests with him.

She would not stay for the honours of the king and the people, but went up to the mountain of Kazbegi by the sources of the Aragvi, and found a small cave, and by her prayer with tears brought water out of its rock: the spring still called the weeping one, because drops fall there to this day like tears, and the milk-giving one, because it gives milk to nursing mothers who have none. The people of Mtskheta saw for several nights a shining cross crowned with stars above the church then being built, and four of the brightest stars going out from it; and at Nina's word a great tree was cut down and four crosses made, one for the sheer rock, one for the mountain of Thoti, one for Queen Salome to raise at Ujarma, and one for the village of Bodbe in Kakheti. Then, taking a presbyter named Jacob and a deacon with her, she preached in the mountains north of Mtskheta, and in Tsaleti, Ertso and Tianeti, and in Kakabeti, and came at last to Bodbe, where she made herself a hut on the mountainside and prayed day and night before the Honourable Cross.

There God showed her that her end was near, and she wrote to Mirian that she was going out of this world as a stranger and a traveller, and asked him to send the bishop John to prepare her. The king came in haste with his court and all the clergy and found her still alive; the crowd stood weeping about her bed, and the sick who touched it were healed. Her disciples begged her to tell them of her birth and her life, and Salome, queen of Ujarma, wrote her words down, and those notes became the chief source of her biography. She asked to be buried in the poor hut where she lay, so that the newly founded church of Kakheti should not be left orphaned; then she spoke of eternal life, received communion from the bishop's hand, and gave up her spirit in peace on 14 January, most probably in the year 338.

The king and the bishop and all the people wished to move her relics to the cathedral at Mtskheta, but for all their trying they could not stir her coffin from the place she had chosen, and so they buried her there in her own hut at Bodbe. In 342 Mirian laid the foundation of a church over her grave, dedicated to her kinsman the Great Martyr George, and his son Bakar finished and consecrated it; a convent grew there afterwards. Her body was kept incorrupt and hidden, as she had asked, and the Church of Iberia, with the blessing of the Church of Antioch, numbered her among the saints as equal to the apostles and enlightener of Iberia, to be honoured every year on 14 January, the day of her falling asleep.

The Romanian calendar keeps her a second time on 19 May, where doxologia.ro prints the same line word for word and links to the same life. The folder holds the January day, the day of her repose, because one church has one feast row here; the May commemoration is recorded as a note on it.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, and its header dates her 296 to 335. saint.gr says she was born in Cappadocia, where many Georgians lived, and is reckoned a kinswoman of the Great Martyr [George](/saints/george-the-trophy-bearer); that her father Zabulon was a devout and famous soldier who had left his native Cappadocia, before he married, to serve the emperor Maximian; and that her mother Susanna was the sister of Juvenal, bishop of Jerusalem. Her father, burning with love for God, became — with his wife's consent — the ascetic the page goes on to describe, and the account of Nina's own mission to Georgia is longer than what is set down here. The page adds, under its heading for relics, that portions of her holy relics are at the church of Saint Marina in Thessalonica and at the monastery of Kykkos in Cyprus.
