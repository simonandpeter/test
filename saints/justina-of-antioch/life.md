# Justina of Antioch

Justina, the Greek synaxarion says, was a Christian virgin called Justa at Antioch in the years of Decius, whom a pagan named Aglaidas loved and could not win. The magician [Cyprian](/saints/cyprian-of-antioch), to whom he turned, found all his arts useless against her steady refusal, burned his books and was baptised; and when he became bishop he took her with him, ordained her deaconess and renamed her Justina. At Nicomedia Claudius beheaded her with Cyprian, and devout Christians carried their relics to Rome and buried them on the city’s most eminent hill.

The Romanian life, which is Cyprian’s life told again, makes her the daughter of Edesius, a pagan priest, and of Cleodonia, at Antioch. Sitting at her window she heard the deacon Praylius, passing by, preach the incarnation, death and resurrection of Christ, and went secretly to church until she believed; she brought her mother and then her old father to faith, and after Edesius saw Christ among a host of angels in a dream, the bishop Optatus baptised the three of them, and later made Edesius a priest, who died a year and a half afterwards.

She lived in prayer, virginity, fasting and great abstinence. When the young scholar Aglaidas, told that she had Christ for her Bridegroom, tried to carry her off, the neighbours rescued her; when Cyprian’s demons came at her by night with burning desire, she drove them off with prayer and the sign of the cross, and with a hair shirt and bread and water; and she saw through the third, who came as a woman arguing that marriage is honourable, answering that great and unspeakable is the reward of those who live in purity.

Her prayer lifted the sickness Cyprian had brought on her house, her kin and the city, and when he was baptised she gave thanks, gave alms to the poor and made offerings to the church; as bishop he made her a deaconess and abbess of a convent of virgins. Denounced with him to the governor Eutolmius, she was struck on the mouth and eyes at Damascus, came out of the boiling cauldron with him unharmed, and was sent with him to Claudius at Nicomedia; at the place of execution Cyprian asked that she die first, and she bowed her head gladly under the sword. Rufina, a kinswoman of Claudius, buried her at Rome with Cyprian and Theoctistus.

The Greek calendar keeps her with Cyprian on 2 October with an apolytikion and a kontakion that name them both; the Romanian keeps her the same day with a virgin martyr’s troparion and the kontakion for the two together.

The Serbian church's Ohrid Prologue keeps the two of them second on this day under one heading, and it tells the household the other two calendars leave out. Cyprian it brings from Carthage to Antioch, where Justina was living with her parents, Aedesius and Cleodonia; Aedesius was a priest of the idols and his whole house heathen. Justina, going about the Christian churches, came to know the true faith and turned both her father and her mother to Christ the Lord, and all three of them were baptised by the bishop Optatus. Cyprian for his part was a sorcerer, who had dealings with unclean spirits and told fortunes.

A dissolute heathen youth named Aglaidas, carried away by Justina's beauty, meant to have her at any cost; and when the holy virgin refused him outright he sought help from Cyprian. Cyprian set the evil spirits on her one after another, to kindle an unclean passion in her for Aglaidas, and got nowhere at all, because Justina drove the spirits of malice from her with the sign of the cross and with prayer to God. Then Cyprian knew the power of the cross, and was himself baptised, and in time became a presbyter and a bishop.

Embittered heathens accused him and Justina, and both were handed over to the court at Damascus, and afterwards tortured and beheaded at Nicomedia «крајем III столећа», at the end of the third century. That is the Prologue's date and it does not agree with the other two: the Greek entry sets them in the years of Decius (249–251) and the Romanian «Pe vremea împărăției lui Deciu», and both have Claudius condemn them at Nicomedia. The recorded floruit now spans both readings and prefers neither. The Prologue names Aedesius, Cleodonia, Optatus and Aglaidas, but as people in the story and not as commemorations of the day, so none of them gets a folder here.

The Russian church calendar keeps her on the Julian 2 October and gives her a great deal the Greek entry does not. She turned to Christ out of pagan error and brought her father and mother to the true faith after her; she consecrated herself to the Heavenly Bridegroom and lived in fasting and prayer, keeping her virginity. When the youth Aglaides asked her to marry him she refused, and it was he who went to Cyprian to have her bent by sorcery. Nothing held: the demons sent to kindle desire in her were driven off by the sign of the Cross, and the page says they fled at the very sight or even the name of the holy virgin. When Cyprian sent plague on her family and on the whole city, her prayer beat that too. After his conversion she withdrew to a monastery and was chosen its abbess, and she was seized with him under Diocletian, taken to Nicomedia and beheaded there in 304.
