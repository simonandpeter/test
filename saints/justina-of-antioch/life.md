# Justina of Antioch

Justina, the Greek synaxarion says, was a Christian virgin called Justa at Antioch in the years of Decius, whom a pagan named Aglaidas loved and could not win. The magician [Cyprian](/saints/cyprian-of-antioch), to whom he turned, found all his arts useless against her steady refusal, burned his books and was baptised; and when he became bishop he took her with him, ordained her deaconess and renamed her Justina. At Nicomedia Claudius beheaded her with Cyprian, and devout Christians carried their relics to Rome and buried them on the city’s most eminent hill.

The Romanian life, which is Cyprian’s life told again, makes her the daughter of Edesius, a pagan priest, and of Cleodonia, at Antioch. Sitting at her window she heard the deacon Praylius, passing by, preach the incarnation, death and resurrection of Christ, and went secretly to church until she believed; she brought her mother and then her old father to faith, and after Edesius saw Christ among a host of angels in a dream, the bishop Optatus baptised the three of them, and later made Edesius a priest, who died a year and a half afterwards.

She lived in prayer, virginity, fasting and great abstinence. When the young scholar Aglaidas, told that she had Christ for her Bridegroom, tried to carry her off, the neighbours rescued her; when Cyprian’s demons came at her by night with burning desire, she drove them off with prayer and the sign of the cross, and with a hair shirt and bread and water; and she saw through the third, who came as a woman arguing that marriage is honourable, answering that great and unspeakable is the reward of those who live in purity.

Her prayer lifted the sickness Cyprian had brought on her house, her kin and the city, and when he was baptised she gave thanks, gave alms to the poor and made offerings to the church; as bishop he made her a deaconess and abbess of a convent of virgins. Denounced with him to the governor Eutolmius, she was struck on the mouth and eyes at Damascus, came out of the boiling cauldron with him unharmed, and was sent with him to Claudius at Nicomedia; at the place of execution Cyprian asked that she die first, and she bowed her head gladly under the sword. Rufina, a kinswoman of Claudius, buried her at Rome with Cyprian and Theoctistus.

The Greek calendar keeps her with Cyprian on 2 October with an apolytikion and a kontakion that name them both; the Romanian keeps her the same day with a virgin martyr’s troparion and the kontakion for the two together.
