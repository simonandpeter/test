# Nicholas

Nicholas — Theodosius, the son of the psalmist Nicephorus and his wife Maria
— was born on 9 April 1877, Easter day, at Komissarovka in the
Verkhnedneprovsk district of Yekaterinoslav province, and named for the
martyr Theodosius. His father was strict, exacting in order and in the work
he set the children, and a great lover of church singing, of the people’s
own singing above all, which he passed to them; his mother, he remembered,
was love itself, who never shouted, but looked at a child who had done wrong
so sorrowfully that it was ashamed; his grandmother Pelagia gathered the
children onto the stove through the long winter evenings for endless stories
of the saints, and his grandfather was a priest. In 1904 his long wish was
granted: on the eve of St Nicholas’s day he was tonsured at the
Nilo-Stolobensky hermitage with the name Nicholas, ordained hierodeacon in
May 1905 and hieromonk on 9 October, and at the brethren’s insistence
entered the Moscow theological academy in 1907, finishing in four years. In
October 1919 he was consecrated at Chernigov bishop of Starodub, vicar of
the Chernigov diocese, and served afterwards under the protection of St
Theodosius of Chernigov, whom he honoured greatly; in 1923 he was made
bishop of Kashira, vicar of Tula, where the Renovationists held nearly every
parish and he fought them stubbornly with his little flock until his arrest
on 8 May 1925. After more than two years in prison he was given the see of
Oryol, and served there until 27 July 1932, when he was arrested again and
taken to Voronezh for the investigation; he told afterwards how he and the
interrogator parted with regret — the man said he was glad to have done him
some good by proving his testimony true, so that the article would be
changed and he would get five years instead of ten; for what? — for your
popularity; men like you must be isolated for a while so that people forget
you exist; you have too much authority among the people and your preaching
means too much to them; they follow you — and he could only say, Lord, glory
to Thee; I, a sinner, served Thee as I could; now no term will frighten me.
Of his wanderings through the camps he told most of Sarov, where the ruined
monastery had been made a camp and he was long held: crossing its threshold
his heart filled with a joy hard to contain — so the Lord has brought me to
Sarov, to St Seraphim, to whom I have prayed so often; I kissed every
grating and every little window; Seraphim’s cell was still whole, and all my
time there I counted myself in obedience to him, by whose prayers we were
comforted to serve the liturgy in prison and communicate. Made archbishop in
1941, he learned of the war at the proskomedia from a friend in the quiet of
the altar, and could say to a flock weeping for Christ’s comfort only what
Alexander Nevsky said, God is not in strength but in truth; it was the
Sunday of All Saints of Russia, and he thought that had a meaning — for our
sins we bore a heavy trial, and the saints of the Russian land did not leave
us.

On 27 June 1941 he was arrested and held six months in the Saratov prison,
then sent to Aktyubinsk in Kazakhstan and after three months to Chelkar as a
“free exile” — asked years later whether he had murmured, he said, God’s
will in everything; the trial ended in great spiritual joy; and think what
becomes of a man who spends his whole life in ease among his own; a life
sated with earthly goods petrifies the heart. He travelled in a prison car
and was pushed out onto the platform at Chelkar at night in his underclothes
and a torn wadded jacket, sixty-four years old and white-haired, with
nothing but the paper that bound him to report to the NKVD twice a month; in
the morning old women gave him a jacket, a cap and patched felt boots, and
one sheltered him in her shed with her cow and her pig; no one would give
work to a man who looked older than his years, and he begged so as not to
die of hunger — asked why he had not told the old women he was a bishop, he
said, if the Lord sends a cross He gives the strength to carry it, and He
lightens it; one’s own will has no place in it, and after a man has borne
patiently what is sent, the Lord sends spiritual joy. He begged until late
autumn 1942, wasting with hunger and cold, his body covered with boils and
lice, until his strength failed and he lost consciousness on the road, and
woke in a clean bed in a hospital ward with people bending over him — well,
almost normal; our grandfather has come round — and as soon as he could
stand he fetched water and bedpans and straightened beds and spoke kind
words until the whole hospital called him grandfather, only one young doctor
knowing that discharge meant the road and the pigsty again. When the day
came he prayed, where Thou sendest me, Lord, I will go, and the nurse said,
grandfather, they have come for you — the Tatar who had brought him every
ten days a couple of flatbreads, some eggs and some sugar, and who, he knew,
had picked him up half dead on the road and driven him to the hospital; at
the door stood the Tatar with his whip — well, hello, father — and drove him
away in his sleigh at the end of the winter of 1943: why did you take care
of me, you do not know me? — we must help each other; God told me I must
help you, save your life — how did God tell you? — I do not know how; I was
driving on my business and God said, take that old man, he must be saved. A
quiet life began; the Tatar had connexions and arranged for his spiritual
daughter Vera Fomushkina, exiled elsewhere, to come to Chelkar, and she did
not hide who the grandfather was whom the town had nursed. On 10 October
1944 he himself wrote to the NKVD asking to be released from the rank of
free exile and allowed to go to Russia and take a see by the Synod’s
appointment; the Special Board freed him early on 19 May 1945, and on 5 July
1945 the Synod created the diocese of Alma-Ata and Kazakhstan and gave it to
him. He arrived at Alma-Ata on 26 October 1945, the feast of the Iveron
icon; his zeal for the services was extraordinary, served as near the
monastic rule as a parish church allowed, unhurried and reverent — when the
choir hurried he looked out of the altar and asked, who here is late for a
train? — and he loved the Mother of God above all, and was the first in
Alma-Ata to serve the rite of the burial of her shroud at the Dormition.
Prayer did not leave him through his last illness: on Sunday 23 October
1955, after his last communion, when the nuns in the dining room began the
hymn Eternal Counsel, he called from his bedroom, mothers, mothers, let us
put a full stop to that; now let us begin the rite of the burial of a
bishop; through the night he prayed aloud, Lord, judge me not by my works
but deal with me by Thy mercy — Lord, I ask mercy, not judgement; on the
Monday he said something tender to each as if in farewell, and at five had a
heart attack and spoke no more, crossing himself a few times on the Tuesday
at the akathist to St Barbara read at his bed; at a quarter to five on 25
October 1955, at the last words of the canon at the parting of the soul,
with a lit candle in his hand, he breathed his last as the bells of St
Nicholas’s cathedral rang for vespers on the eve of the Iveron icon, to
which he had loved to cry, rejoice, good gatekeeper, who openest the doors
of paradise to the faithful. Bishop Hermogenes (Golubev) of Tashkent buried
him on 28 October from the cathedral, the coffin carried by hand the seven
kilometres to the cemetery with thousands following, so that the clergy
could hardly reach the grave; and when the mound was raised and covered with
wreaths, in the dusk and moonlight all sang the troparion of the good
gatekeeper. The Sretensky calendar’s line, which this entry stands under, is
the finding of his relics in 2000 and calls him metropolitan of Alma-Ata; he
is kept on 25 October, on 8 September for the finding, and in the synaxes of
the new martyrs and confessors of Russia, of Radonezh and of the Lipetsk
saints.
