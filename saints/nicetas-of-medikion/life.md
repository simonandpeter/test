# Nicetas of Medikion

Nicetas's homeland was Caesarea of Bithynia, and he was born of right-believing parents. His father Philaret was tonsured a monk when his lawful wife departed to the Lord on the eighth day after the child was born, and the boy was fed by his father's mother, who was then still among the living. When he had grown and learned his letters he gave himself to the service of God, serving first as sacristan in the church and busying himself with the reading of the divine books.

Afterwards he went to a certain hermit, Stephen, a man of virtue, and having taken guidance enough from him was sent to the monastery of Medikion, which the venerable Nicephorus had built and over which he was abbot. Nicephorus received him with love, seeing the divine gift in him beforehand, and tonsured him into the monastic order. Nicetas so increased in ascetic labour, in humility, in obedience and in every good work that before seven years in the monastery were out he had surpassed all the monks there; and the abbot compelled him to take the priesthood, and the most holy Tarasius, patriarch of Constantinople, ordained him.

The whole rule of the house was put into his hands at once, for Nicephorus had grown weak with age, and he governed it well in his father's place, pasturing the rational flock with care and increasing it by the pattern of his own life. Many who heard of it came there renouncing the world, and within a few years about a hundred brothers had gathered. Among them was the blessed Athanasius, who had gone out secretly from his father's house; his father came in anger, stripped the monastic clothing from him, dressed him in bright and costly garments and took him home by force, and the boy said to him: father, do you think these costly clothes will keep me from my purpose? All this world is hateful to me; for what does it profit a man to gain the whole world and lose his own soul?

In those years the heresy of the war on the icons had not ceased, though the seventh ecumenical council had cursed it. The life traces it from Leo the third of the name, called the Isaurian, who first commanded the icons to be thrown out, through his son Leo the fourth, to the peace that Irene and her younger son Constantine brought, when the seventh council was gathered and the whole Church rejoiced. After Irene reigned Nicephorus and after him Michael, right-believing emperors; and then came Leo the fifth of the name, called the Armenian, who followed the Isaurian and set the accursed heresy going again.

He found helpers for it among the nobles — two of the worst named John, called Specta, and Eutychian — and he called the bishops and abbots to the palace, saying that he himself honoured the holy icon and asking only that they dispute with the other side and let the truth appear. Emilian, bishop of Cyzicus, answered boldly that a question of the faith ought to be examined in the Church after the custom and not in the imperial palaces. Theophylact of Nicomedia answered that Christ was witness, whose holy icon the emperor had before him; and Euthymius of Sardis said that for eight hundred years and more Christ had been depicted in the churches everywhere and honoured so, and asked who was proud enough to move a tradition held from the apostles, the martyrs and the God-inspired fathers.

The emperor put his own false bishops into the sees of those he drove out, cast the holy icons from the churches, and gathered with the false patriarch Theodotus an unlawful assembly at Constantinople that anathematised the orthodox. After Pascha the confessors were given to John the grammarian to torment as he pleased, and he shut each of them in a separate prison, narrow and dark, with foul damp bread passed through a little window, a nail's worth a day, and water muddy and unclean; and to grieve Nicetas the more he seized his young disciple Theoctistus and wore him down with hunger and thirst.

Then the heretics, seeing that the fathers would rather die than leave their faith, put a snare to them: nothing else was asked of them, only that they should communicate in church with the patriarch Theodotus. Deceived by it, the fathers consented at that time, and afterwards knew the deceit and repented bitterly and set themselves right. Freed each from his own prison, they came to Nicetas and pressed him to consent as well and come out. He would not leave the prison he was bearing for Christ; and they stood against him, saying that they could not go out and leave him there, that the thing asked was small, and that they could keep their faith and fit their judgement to the straits they were in.

So, with their pressing and their long insistence, Nicetas went out even against his will — not fleeing the hard suffering nor fearing torments, but heeding the entreaty of those fathers and seeing their grey hairs. Theodotus, to take them the more easily, brought them to an oratory dressed on purpose with icons, so that the fathers seeing them should think him orthodox; there he served, and they received communion from his hands, because they heard him say aloud: if anyone does not honour the icons of Christ, let him be anathema — which he said not in honour of the Saviour's icon but in hypocrisy before them.

When each had gone back to his own monastery, Nicetas was wounded with grief in his heart that he had communicated with the false patriarch, for he counted even that small turning aside a whole apostasy in himself. He took ship for the island called Prokonnesos to repent there; then, judging that repentance belongs where the fault was, he returned to Byzantium and went about the city teaching the people without fear to hold the dogmas the holy fathers had settled at the seventh council.

The emperor heard of it, called him and asked why he alone had not gone back to his monastery and had not obeyed the command. Nicetas answered quietly that he would go neither to his monastery nor out of his faith; that he stood and would stand in the confession his fathers the orthodox bishops had stood in, who had suffered exile and prison unjustly from him; that he feared neither death nor loved this passing life; that God was his witness he had done what he ought not, having submitted unwilling for obedience to the elders and communicated with the false patriarch Theodotus, and that he grieved and repented of it; and that from then on he had no communion with them at all.

He had already been driven into exile once before this, in the worst of the winter, to a city called Masaleon, poorly clothed and hurried on by a hard and pitiless escort. Now the emperor, seeing his will unchanged, gave him to a certain Zacharias, the head of the imperial palace called Mangana, to be kept under guard — and Zacharias, a good and right-believing man, gave him honour instead of trouble — and afterwards sent him into exile on a small island that bore the name of the holy martyr Glyceria, where her relics were and a church and a monastery in her name, to the north of Byzantium.

While he was in bonds himself he was asked by Michael, bishop of Synnada, who was likewise shut in prison for the faith, to pray for that same Zacharias, who had been sent into Thrace on the people's business and had fallen into the hands of barbarians and been carried off captive; Nicetas prayed, and Zacharias was set free and came to the island with his friend to thank him. He also delivered three brothers whose boat was caught at midnight by waves that rose without warning, and brought them safe to dry land.

After a short time there, doing good to many by the gift of healing that was much in him, he came near his end. Worn by the painful labours of the exile, he fell into his last sickness and received the divine Mysteries on a Saturday; and on the Sunday, as the day was breaking, he departed to the Lord, on the third day of the month of April. The city and the country round heard of it at once, and a crowd came from both orders, clergy and lay, with the brothers of Medikion and of the other monasteries, and two bishops, Theophilus of Ephesus and Joseph of Thessalonica, who tended the body after the custom, laid it in a coffin, put it aboard a ship and carried it to his own monastery.

Paul, bishop of Plousias, met them at the shore with a multitude of monks and lay people, and they took him on their shoulders; and not a few miracles were done on the road, the weak being healed and evil spirits driven out, and a woman who had long had an issue of blood was healed the moment she touched the relics. The whole assembly singing psalms, they laid him in the grave of the father Nicephorus, the first abbot of that house, on the left side of the porch; and many miracles and healings were done there afterwards for those who came in faith.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and carries the history. saint.gr has him from Caesarea in Bithynia, orphaned of his mother in infancy and brought up by his virtuous grandmother and his devout father Philaretos, who put his schooling early into the hands of a cleric famed for his teaching, so that he got a considerable learning both worldly and spiritual. He left father, mother, brothers, sisters, kinsmen, house, country and wealth, took up his cross willingly, and while still young went to the celebrated monastery of Medikion at Trigleia, where his many virtues quickly won him the love of all the brothers; on the death of the abbot Nikephoros they elected him abbot, in the time of the patriarch Tarasios.

For his steadiness in the Church's teaching and tradition on the holy icons he was exiled under Leo V to the small town of Masaleon in Asia Minor; he was recalled from there only to be exiled again, in 815, to the island of St Glykeria near Akritas. Under Michael II he returned to Constantinople and settled at a dependency in the north of the city, which the page says probably belonged to the monastery of Pelekete. [Theodore the Studite](/saints/theodore-the-studite) wrote to him there continually, and there, in the place where he had settled, he fell asleep in peace.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same menologion day, tells the same life from Caesarea in Bithynia and the monastery of Medikion onward, and adds both a year and a story. It sets the second iconoclasm in its line of emperors: after the devout Irene and the right-believing Nicephorus and Michael, Leo the Fifth the Armenian came to the throne and the heresy against the icons flared up again; the emperor deposed the patriarch Nicephorus and sent him into exile, and raised in his place a heretic, Theodotus Cassiteras, a man of unclean life. Nicetas was shut up and tormented and did not waver in Orthodoxy; he was moved from prison to prison and tried with hunger and thirst, with frost and sweat and with mockery, and would not be shaken.

One man in particular plagued him, a certain Nicholas, with jeering and insult. But one night Nicholas's own dead father appeared to him in a dream and said to him reproachfully: depart from the servant of God. From that hour Nicholas repented, and not only troubled the saint no more but turned others away from troubling him. When Leo the Armenian perished by an evil death and the right-believing emperor Michael Balbus took the empire, he set free all the Orthodox who were suffering; and Nicetas withdrew to a solitary place near Constantinople, where he spent the last days of his earthly life in prayer and in thanksgiving to God for everything. He worked many wonders by prayer while he lived; and when he died his body was carried to his own monastery, and on that funeral journey many sick people touched it and were healed. His relics were laid beside the tomb of his spiritual father Nicephorus and of his friend Athanasius. This great hierarch reposed, the entry ends, in the year 824 — the only year any source read for this folder gives him.

The Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery keeps him on 3 апреля ст. ст. and gives his house and his grade in the line itself — «Прп. Никиты исп., игумена обители Мидикийской (824).» — and then a life the other three calendars do not have. He was born at Caesarea in Bithynia, in the north-west of Asia Minor, of a devout family; his mother died on the eighth day after his birth and his father, named Филарет, was tonsured a monk, so that the child was left to a grandmother who brought him up in the faith. From his youth he served in church and lived under obedience to a hermit, Стефан, and it was by that man’s blessing that he went to the Medikion monastery.

The abbot there was Никифор, whose memory the page puts on 13 March. After seven years in a house known for the strictness of its rule Nicetas was ordained presbyter, and the abbot, being gravely ill, gave the governing of the monastery into his hands. He cared for the house by the example of his own strict life, the brethren grew to a hundred, and when the old abbot died in deep old age they chose him abbot with one voice. The gift of wonders was given him: at his prayer a deaf and dumb boy spoke, two possessed women were healed, a man out of his mind got his reason back, and many sick were cured.

Under the emperor Лев Армянин, whose reign the page gives as 813 to 820, the iconoclast heresy rose again. A council of the heretics at Constantinople in 815 threw the patriarch Никифор off his throne and put a layman, Феодот, in his place, and the emperor called the abbots of all the monasteries to him to win them over. Nicetas stood firm, the rest followed his example, and they were put in prison together.

Then came the trick, and this book tells it against him. The prisoners were offered freedom and the icons back on one condition, that they receive the Communion from the false patriarch; the other prisoners begged him, and he went into a church where icons had been set out to deceive the confessors, and communicated. Back at his monastery he saw the persecution going on as before, repented of what he had done, returned to Constantinople and denounced the heresy without fear. He was imprisoned again and stayed six years, until the emperor died; and there, in hunger and grief, two captives were freed without ransom at his prayer and three shipwrecked men were cast up alive on the shore. He went to the Lord in 824, the year this folder already holds, under the next emperor, and his relics became a source of healings.
