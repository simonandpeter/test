# Vakhtang Gorgasali

Vakhtang came to the throne of Kartli in Georgia at the age of fifteen, in a time when the region was invaded without pause by the Persians from the north and the Ossetians from the south. After his crowning the young king called his servants of the court together and spoke to them with much wisdom, saying that the hard state of his people was owing to the sins of the king and of the people, and urging them to fight with humility and in unity for the faith and for the fatherland.

He was marked out by faith and by wisdom, spent many nights at prayer and gave alms to many poor men, and so dedicated his life to God. He could fight untiring: armoured and fully armed, he could carry a warhorse on his shoulders and climb with it from Mtskheta to the fortress of Armazi, and could outrun a stag on foot; and he held himself in hand in matters of state when hard decisions had to be taken for the country's future. On his helmet a wolf was figured in front and a lion behind, and the Persians, seeing it, would cry to one another "Dar 'az gurgsar!" — beware the wolf's head — which is where his name Gorgasali came from.

In his reign the Church of Georgia was recognised as autocephalous for the first time. When he had driven the fire-worshippers out of Georgia, he sent to Constantinople for the patriarch's judgment a bishop Michael, who leaned to the monophysite heresy the Persians had planted in the country; the bishop cursed the king and his army for rising against the monophysites, and was so enraged that, when Vakhtang came up to take his blessing, he struck him in the mouth and broke several of his teeth. The patriarch of Constantinople sent Michael to a monastery to repent.

The patriarch and the Byzantine emperor then sent on to the patriarch of Antioch the clergy the king had chosen for consecration; at Antioch twelve of them were made bishops, and a certain Peter was enthroned as the first Catholicos of Georgia. Vakhtang fulfilled the wish of the holy king [Mirian](/saints/mirian-of-georgia) by founding the Georgian monastery of the Holy Cross in Jerusalem, and he built a church of stone in the place of a wooden one. Several dioceses were founded in his reign, and at Nikozi in Kartli he built a cathedral and set in it the relics of the martyr Razhden.

He built many fortresses, churches and monasteries — strongholds at Tukhari, Artanuji and Akhiza, monasteries in Klarjeti at Artanuji, Mere, Shindobi and Akhiza — made a new royal residence at Ujarma, and laid the foundations of the new Georgian capital, Tbilisi. His political creed, the page says, had three parts: an equal union of the Georgian Church with the Byzantine, national independence, and the unity of Church and nation.

In the year 502 Vakhtang, then sixty years old, had to defend his country for the last time. In a battle with the Persians he was mortally wounded when a poisoned arrow pierced him under the arm; before he died he called his clergy, his family and his court and urged them to be strong in the faith. All Georgia mourned him. His body was carried from the royal residence at Ujarma to Mtskheta, to the cathedral of Svetitskhoveli which he had built, and buried there with great honour. Fifteen centuries later, with the blessing of Patriarch Elias II, his name was added to the title of the patriarchal cathedral of Sioni, and a cathedral was founded in his honour in the city of Rustavi.
