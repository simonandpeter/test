# Peter the Athonite

Peter was a Greek by birth, a soldier and a commander, sent with the armies to the war in Syria. The Greek force was beaten, and he was carried off with many other soldiers to the town of Samara in Arabia, beside the Euphrates, and thrown into prison in heavy irons. Sitting in his chains he searched his conscience and remembered how often he had thought of leaving the world and had not done it, and he understood that the captivity had come upon him for that; and he began to call on Saint Nicholas, to whom he had long prayed, vowing that if he were loosed he would not return home.

Nicholas came to him in a dream and said that his prayer had been heard, but that because he had himself been slow towards God's commandments he would not be loosed quickly; he was to go on asking and be patient. At a second appearing Nicholas told him to call as well upon the righteous Simeon, who received Christ in the temple at forty days, saying that the two of them praying together would be heard; and Peter did so, and was freed.

Afterwards, in a vision, he saw the Mother of God shining in glory with Nicholas standing before her and asking where this servant of hers should finish his life. She answered that his rest would be on the mountain of Athos, which her Son had given her as her own portion, and that she would defend all who struggled there in stillness. As the ship carrying him drew near Athos it stopped dead in a fair wind and deep water, and Peter asked the sailors to set him ashore there.

He lived on the mountain fifty-three years in fasting and prayer. Twice the devil came against him with the beasts and the creeping things of the mountain, and he drove them off with the sign of the Cross and the names of Christ and his Mother. In all those years he saw no human face and had no garment, the sky his roof and the ground his bed, burnt by the summer sun and frozen in winter. A hunter found him at last and heard his life from his own mouth, and was sent home for a year under a rule of abstinence, prayer and almsgiving. When the hunter came back with his brother and two monks, the saint was lying dead in the cave with his hands crossed on his breast. His brother, who was troubled by an unclean spirit, was healed at the relics; and the body was carried to a village under Macedonia, and from there by the bishop of the city to his own church.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 12 June, alongside [Onuphrius of Egypt](/saints/onuphrius-the-great), and saint.gr's account disagrees with its own head line about when he lived: the head brackets him «(; - 734)», while the narrative says he lived in the ninth century, and has him tonsured by Pope Gregory IV, who held that see from 827. It says he came from Constantinople, of devout and distinguished parents of that city, who brought him up according to the commandments of Christ; that his education was a notable one, and that with the gifts he had he was made a general. In some battle the imperial army was beaten and he himself was taken prisoner; he was carried off to Samara in Mesopotamia, shut in prison and suffered many hardships. When he was set free in a wonderful manner he resolved to give himself wholly to Christ. He went to Rome, made the round of all the Christian monuments, studied the history of the Church's struggles, and was afterwards tonsured a monk by Pope Gregory IV. He settled on the Holy Mountain, where he practised asceticism for fifty-three years and fell asleep in peace inside a cave; and his holy relic was found by chance by a hunter and buried by the monks with reverence and every honour. The page records portions of his relics at the monasteries of Saint Dionysiou and Docheiariou on the Holy Mountain and at Leimonos on Lesbos.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 12 June, the same menologion day the folder’s other two rows carry. It makes him a Greek by birth and a soldier by trade. Campaigning once against the Arabs he was taken, put in fetters and thrown into prison in the city of Samara on the Euphrates, where he lay a long time, praying only this: that God would free him from the gaol and lead him away into some desert, where he might give himself wholly to prayer. Two saints appeared to him there and touched his fetters with a staff, and the irons melted like wax, and he was all at once in the open country outside the city.

He went straight to Rome, and at the tomb of the Apostle Peter he was tonsured a monk by the pope himself, whom this book does not name. Taking ship east again, he saw the Most Holy Theotokos in a dream, and she appointed him the Holy Mountain of Athos for his struggle; until that moment, the entry says, he had never so much as heard of it. He went into a cave there and spent fifty-three years in it, against hunger and thirst, heat and frost, and most of all against the demons, until by God’s help he had overcome them all. When the first trials were past an angel began to bring him bread every forty days.

Several times the tempter came to him in the shape of an angel of light, and he drove him off with the sign of the cross and the name of the Most Holy Theotokos. A year before the end a hunter who was hunting deer on Athos came upon him and had the life from his own mouth, and his relics were afterwards translated to Macedonia. This book puts the close of his life in 374. The folder holds no date, and this is why: the Greek page brackets a year four centuries later than that, its own narrative puts him later still, and three books that cannot be reconciled are left unreconciled rather than widened into a span that would say nothing.
