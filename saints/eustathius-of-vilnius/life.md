# Eustathius of Vilnius

saint.gr's whole account of the three is one sentence: «Οι Άγιοι Μάρτυρες Αντώνιος και Ιωάννης ήταν αδέλφια και μαζί με τον Ευστάθιο μαρτύρησαν το έτος 1347 στη Βίλνα της Λιθουανίας» — the holy martyrs Antony and John were brothers, and together with Eustathius they were martyred in the year 1347 at Vilna in Lithuania. The calendar's line for the day says the same and no more, calling the two αυτάδελφοι, brothers of one womb.

Eustathius is the third of the company and not one of the brothers: the page names [Antony](/saints/antony-of-vilnius) and [John](/saints/john-of-vilnius) as brothers and then says only that they were martyred «μαζί με τον Ευστάθιο», together with Eustathius. saint.gr heads him «Αγιος Ευσταθιος (; - 1347)», which is the year of his death and the only year it gives him.

Nothing else is printed of him: no parentage, no trade, no city of birth, no account of the trial and no manner of death, no judge and no hymn. What the page adds under his name is where his body lies. Its closing sentence puts the relics of all three «στην ιερά μονή Αγίας Τριάδος Βίλνας», at the holy monastery of the Holy Trinity of Vilna, while its own list of relics gives him, separately, the church of the Holy Spirit at Vilnius in Lithuania; the page sets the two down side by side and does not reconcile them.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the three together on the same day and gives them five sentences, almost all of it new here. All three were first pagans and worshippers of fire, it says, and were servants at the court of the Lithuanian prince Olgard at Vilnius; and before their baptism they were named Kruglec, Kumec and Nezhilo. The entry gives those three names in one clause and does not say which belonged to which man, so none of them is set against a name here. All three were baptised by a priest it calls Нестор, and all three were hanged, one after another, on one and the same oak, in 1347 — the year these folders already hold.

The Christians cut that oak down, the entry goes on, and built a church to the glory of the Holy Trinity, in which they laid the martyrs' honourable relics; and out of the stump of the oak they made the holy table. Their relics are at Vilnius. The priest and the prince are named as the entry names them and are identified with nobody. The other two of the three are [Antony](/saints/antony-of-vilnius) and [John](/saints/john-of-vilnius).

**The Russian calendar keeps the three of them on this menologion day under one line and prints three hymns for them, and its life is the fullest account of the company in any source read for these folders.** The line is «Мчч. Антония, Иоанна и Евстафия Литовских (1347).» and carries no typikon icon. They served, it says, at the court of the grand prince Algirdas of Lithuania, whose reign it dates 1345 to 1377, and who took Christianity for reasons of policy, to marry an Orthodox princess of Vitebsk and enlarge his lands. For a time he favoured the faith: he let the princess's confessor, the priest Nestor, preach, and allowed three churches to be built, two at Vitebsk and one at Vilna under the name of the martyr Paraskeva.

Nestor brought several of the prince's pagan Lithuanians to Orthodoxy, among them two brothers of the local nobility, Nezhilo and Kumets, who were given the names Antony and John in holy baptism. When the princess died the prince gave way to the pagan priests and went back to the idols, and the brothers went on keeping the fasts. The priests demanded that he punish them; he argued with them, they would not change, and he threw them into prison for a whole year. There the younger held out and the elder did not: John sent word in secret that he would submit if he were freed, and the prince, delighted, freed them both.

John kept the pagan rites outwardly and stayed a Christian in his heart; his brother confessed openly, reproached him for cowardice and called him to repent. At the prince's table on a fast day meat was served; John was afraid to refuse it and Antony would not touch it, and for that Antony went back to prison. The brother who had denied was left free, but Christians and pagans alike broke off with him as a traitor, and he came to see his sin and wept over it. Antony told him that they could be brothers again only if he confessed Christ openly, and he looked for his chance.

He took it in front of a crowd of courtiers, declaring aloud that he was a Christian; they beat him on the spot and he was thrown into the same prison. People came in crowds to see the two confessors, and so many were baptised that the pagan priests pressed the prince to put them to death and stop it. He gave way, but had Antony killed first, hoping the other would deny again. Antony spent the night in prayer, both communicated in the morning, and the pagans hanged him on an oak. John was unshaken, went on preaching to the people at the prison, and was strangled and then hung dead on the same oak, as his brother had foretold. Both were buried with honour in a church of the wonderworker of Myra.

Their kinsman Kruglets, a courtier of the prince, was so struck by their constancy that the same priest baptised him with the name Eustathius. The prince, who was fond of the young man, noticed that he had let his hair grow and asked whether he was a Christian; Eustathius said that he was. He was pressed to eat meat on a Friday of the Nativity fast and refused, and was beaten with iron rods, and praised God for being counted worthy to suffer; then led naked into hard frost with ice water poured into his mouth, until his body went blue and his breath stopped at times. The prince had the bones of his legs broken from sole to knee, the hair torn from his head with the skin, his ears and nose cut off, and the torture kept up for three days. Do not weep for me, he told the Christians who wept at the sight: this earthly house of my soul is being pulled down, and I hope soon to have for it a dwelling not made with hands in the heavens.

He was condemned, and walked to the place of execution so briskly on his broken legs that the men leading him could hardly keep up and were astonished at the plain miracle; he was hung on the same oak as his kinsmen. His body was left low on the tree for the beasts and birds, and neither beast nor bird could come near it, a pillar of cloud keeping them off. Three days later it was buried beside the brothers. A church of the Holy Trinity was raised afterwards where the oak had stood, and at the translation of the bodies into it the relics were found incorrupt, and wonders and healings were worked at them. At the request of the Orthodox Lithuanians the metropolitan of Moscow asked the patriarch of Constantinople to bless their numbering among the saints, and by 1364 the patriarch had sent a cross with their relics in it to the abbot of Radonezh.

The prince himself came back to the faith and took the monastic habit at the end of his life, and all twelve of his sons were Christians; by the end of the fourteenth century half the people of Vilna were Orthodox. The relics were carried to Moscow in 1915 before the German advance, as the most precious relics of Orthodoxy in the Baltic country, by the metropolitan who became patriarch; they came back in 1946 to the monastery of the Holy Spirit at Vilnius, and the date of the return, 13 July in the old reckoning, has been kept there every year since.

**One date in the life contradicts the rest of the life and is not followed here.** It puts Antony's hanging on the morning of «14 апреля 1317 года», where its own line for the day prints 1347, where it dates the prince's reign from 1345, and where it has John killed on 24 April 1347 and Eustathius on 13 December 1347 after him. The year these folders already carry, 1347, is left standing, the page's 1317 is recorded here as the page's own and nothing was changed on its strength.
