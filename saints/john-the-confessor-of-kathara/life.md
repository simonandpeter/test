# John of Kathara

John was born about the year 778 at Irenoupolis, a city belonging to the Decapolis of Coele-Syria. His parents, Theodore and Gregoria, were known for their piety, and they took every care to pass it on to an obedient son; his childhood went between that upbringing, his studies, and the many works of charity his parents got him used to early. The same piety and the same bent towards his fellow men marked his youth, in which, by keeping to the divine will, he was held back from every vanity and every uncleanness.

Later he became a monk, and did not prosper less in that new life. His zeal and his learning made him stand out among the other brethren, and in 787 the abbot of his monastery took him along to Nicaea, where the Seventh Ecumenical Council was then being convoked. When it ended the two of them came on to Constantinople; and because both had drawn the favourable notice of the empress Irene and of the patriarch [Tarasius](/saints/tarasius-of-constantinople) during the conciliar sessions, the elder was made abbot of the monastery of the Dalmatoi, while John was ordained presbyter and, under the emperor Nikephoros who followed Irene on the throne, was appointed abbot of the monastery called of the Kathara.

He carried out that charge conscientiously, taking pains to advance discipline among the brethren and to raise their spiritual and moral life; and because the consequences of the frenzy against the icons still went on in those years, he handed on to his monks the teachings of Orthodoxy and prepared them for every boldness and every danger on their account. The day of the great trial came in the reign of Leo the Fifth, who took the throne in 813, a man carried away by a blind prejudice against the icons and pushed on by two others who had great influence over him, Theodotus Melissenus and John the Grammarian.

Leo issued decrees and violent measures for the abolition of the holy icons and set in motion a fearful persecution of Orthodox bishops and abbots and monks, which he extended to senators and patricians and even to women and virgins. The monastery of the Kathara was caught up in it: soldiers in a fury seized its possessions, the monks were ill-used and scattered, and the abbot John was taken in irons to Constantinople. The emperor had heard much of him and wanted to see him, not thinking it impossible to change his mind. But John showed the persecution of the icons to be unorthodox and harmful, reproved the emperor for his obstinacy and his impious opinions, and foretold to him that the displeasure of the saints and the outcry of his victims to God would one day bring punishment.

The emperor in his anger had him held in a dependency of his own monastery, still hoping he would think better of it and conform; and when he saw the hope was barren, exiled him to a fortress called Pentadaktylo in the land of Lampe. After eighteen months of harsh confinement he was brought back to Constantinople, where the new patriarch Theodotus the First, the Melissenus surnamed Kassiteras, tried as vainly as the others to talk him out of the holy icons; and then he was shut up two years more in the fortress Kriotauros of the Boukellarioi, and suffered everything there without his zeal for the icons being softened in the least.

His prophecy came true. Leo the Fifth was cut down, and Michael the Second, who followed him, planned to reconcile the two camps and let those Leo had driven out come back from exile. John came back with them; but the enemies of the icons persuaded the emperor to allow him Chalcedon only, and the city was shut to him. Michael was succeeded in 829 by his son Theophilus, himself a partisan and protector of the reform and an enemy of the icons. In 836 John wished to enter Constantinople to be near the clergy of his own mind, in the precinct of a church; but the patriarch John the Seventh, the Grammarian, would not allow it and exiled him to the island of Aphousia. There, after two and a half years, probably in the year 839, he fell asleep in peace.
