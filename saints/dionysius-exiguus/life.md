# Dionysius Exiguus

Dionysius — Exiguus, the Small, as he called himself for humility’s sake —
was born about 470 in Scythia Minor, today’s Dobrogea, a Geto-Dacian by
descent, and came to Rome at the end of the fifth century, where he lived as
a monk to the end of his life; later writers call him abbot, which need not
mean he ever governed a monastery. He was a great scholar of Scripture and a
strict keeper of every monastic virtue, of humility above all, and with his
excellent Greek did work as a translator that shaped canon law, Christian
chronology, the history of monasticism and dogmatics in the West, putting
into Latin many lives and writings of the saints and collections of canons.
His Collection of the Councils, in two recensions both dedicated to Bishop
Stephen of Salona, holds the fifty apostolic canons, the Greek collections
of the councils of Nicaea, Ancyra, Neocaesarea, Gangra, Antioch, Laodicea
and Constantinople, twenty-seven canons of Chalcedon, the twenty-one of
Sardica and the decrees of Carthage of 419; a good while after it came his
Collection of the Decretals, the oldest of its kind — the letters of
Siricius in fifteen chapters, twenty-one of Innocent I, one of Zosimus, four
from the time of Boniface I, three of Celestine, seven of Leo the Great, one
of Gelasius and one of Anastasius I. His paschal table, made in 525,
continued the ninety-five-year table of Cyril of Alexandria, which ended in
531, repeating Cyril’s last nineteen-year cycle and adding five more from
532; by it the Alexandrian reckoning of Easter, in use in the East since the
council of Nicaea, entered the Latin church, which until then had kept the
eighty-four-year cycle of Victorius, no small service to the unity of the
Church. And he stopped counting the years from Diocletian, the godless
persecutor of Christians, and began to count them from the incarnation of
the Lord, and so founded the Christian era: he placed the birth of Christ,
wrongly as is now known, in the year 754 from the founding of Rome, on 25
December of the first year of his era, with the incarnation on 25 March. His
Easter cycle was soon received at Rome, then in the rest of Italy, in Gaul
by the end of the sixth century and in the British church from 729, and
under Charlemagne the Dionysian reckoning, as the West called it, came into
the official use of the Church; other letters and translations of his
survive. He died between 537 and 556. The Romanian calendar keeps him on 1
September with a cross, and on 7 March 2018 the Holy Synod of the Russian
Church entered him in its calendar with his memory on the same day, 1
September of the old style, 14 September; the Russian church calendar’s life
of him is the article of the Orthodox Theological Encyclopaedia of 1903.
