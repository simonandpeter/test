# Antipas of Pergamum

In the days of the Apostles, under the emperor Domitian, a great persecution rose against the Christians, and letters went out from the emperor to the governors and rulers everywhere that Christians should submit to the imperial orders and honour the temples of the idols. It was then, the life says, that [John the Evangelist](/saints/john-the-theologian) was exiled to Patmos, where the Lord appeared to him among the seven lampstands and told him to write to the angels — that is, the bishops — of the seven churches of Asia.

Antipas was bishop of the church of Pergamum, and it is there, by the Lord's own mouth, that he is remembered: «Antipa mărturisitorul Meu cel credincios a fost ucis între voi, între care petrece satana» — Antipas my faithful witness was killed among you, where Satan dwells. From those words, the life says, one can see both what Antipas was and what the people of Pergamum were: he stood among them like a light in darkness, like a rose among thorns, like gold in the mire — better, like gold tried in the furnace.

He was not troubled by any of it, but stood against his opponents as though his nature had been changed from human to angelic, and went out often among the people with no care for the threats of his tormentors. The demons the Greeks took for gods fled the city, the life says, and appeared in dreams to their own servants complaining that they could neither taste the sacrifices nor smell their smoke, being driven out by Antipas, the chief of the Christians.

At that the crowd seized him and dragged him to the place of sacrifice, and the governor charged him with disturbing the gods so that they had left the city and its protection with them. Antipas answered that he was a Christian and would never obey a mad and heathen order, and that if gods could be driven out by one mortal man and had to seek help from their own worshippers, that alone showed the error. The governor appealed to the antiquity of the old worship and to the honour due to Antipas's great age; Antipas answered that he would not change his faith at the very end of his life for the sake of a wretched and dishonourable one.

He was put to death in a brazen bull heated red with fire. He prayed there a long time, asking to be counted in the number of the martyrs and that God receive his spirit, and praying for those who would keep his memory after him — that they be kept from passions and from sickness, and from the unbearable pain of the teeth, and be forgiven and find mercy at the judgement. When the prayer was finished he gave up his spirit, falling asleep sweetly; devout men laid his relics with honour in that same city of Pergamum, and myrrh flowed from them which healed every human sickness.
