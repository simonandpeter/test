# Anicetas of Nicomedia

Anicetas and his nephew [Photius](/saints/photius-of-nicomedia) were from Nicomedia. Anicetas, a military dignitary, denounced the emperor Diocletian, who had set up instruments of execution in the city square to frighten the Christians; the emperor had him tortured and thrown to the beasts, but the lion let loose on him turned gentle and fawned on him. An earthquake brought down the temple of Hercules and a stretch of the city wall that killed many pagans; the executioner who raised his sword fell senseless; they put him on the wheel and to the fire, and the wheel stopped and the fire went out; they threw him into a cauldron of boiling tin, and the tin went cold — so the Lord kept his servant, the life says, for the strengthening of many.

His nephew Photius greeted the sufferer and turned to the emperor: idolater, your gods are nothing — and the sword raised over the new confessor struck the executioner himself. Both were imprisoned; three days later Diocletian offered them glory and riches if they worshipped his gods — perish with your honour and your riches, they answered — and had them tied by the feet to wild horses, which dragged them unharmed; a red-hot bath-house fell in without hurting them.

At last he had a huge furnace lit, and many Christians, heartened by the two, walked into it of their own will crying, we are Christians, and died with prayer on their lips; the bodies of Anicetas and Photius were untouched by the fire, even their hair, and many pagans believed. This was in 305. The Prologue tells it likewise for the Serbian calendar — Anicetas one of the city’s governors, three years in prison — and notes that the two are invoked in the prayers at the blessing of oil and of water.

The Greek calendar keeps the two of them on the same day, 12 August, and gives the scene in the senate house. saint.gr says Photius was Anicetas's nephew and that both came from Nicomedia; that when Diocletian wanted to set a persecution going against the Christians he spoke before the Senate in the most abusive terms against them; and that Anicetas, who was present, was not frightened by the emperor's words but stood up boldly, declared himself a Christian, and told him: you are deceived, emperor, if you think that by measures against the Christians you will achieve your impious purposes — know that the Christians are today the soundest part of the Roman empire, and that they would be foolish and senseless if they believed in idols.

For that, the page says, he was tortured; and when the fire set against him went out, his nephew Photius ran and embraced him. As soon as the idolaters saw it they bound the two of them inside what was called the bath of Antoninus, and when the water had been heated beyond bearing both gave up their spirits gloriously.

saint.gr's head lines give the year as 305, where the Russian calendar above gives 305 or 306; the narrower figure is recorded and not preferred. The page's own distich plays on both names at once — «Πῦρ Ἀνίκητον συμφλέγει τῷ Φωτίῳ, / Οὓς φωτὸς οἶκος ὡς ἀνικήτους φέρει» — the fire burns Anicetas together with Photius, whom the house of light bears as unconquered ones; and a second line names the day: «Πῦρ κατὰ δωδεκάτην κτάνε Φώτιον ἠδ' Ἀνίκητον».
