# Anicetas of Nicomedia

Anicetas and his nephew [Photius](/saints/photius-of-nicomedia) were from Nicomedia. Anicetas, a military dignitary, denounced the emperor Diocletian, who had set up instruments of execution in the city square to frighten the Christians; the emperor had him tortured and thrown to the beasts, but the lion let loose on him turned gentle and fawned on him. An earthquake brought down the temple of Hercules and a stretch of the city wall that killed many pagans; the executioner who raised his sword fell senseless; they put him on the wheel and to the fire, and the wheel stopped and the fire went out; they threw him into a cauldron of boiling tin, and the tin went cold — so the Lord kept his servant, the life says, for the strengthening of many.

His nephew Photius greeted the sufferer and turned to the emperor: idolater, your gods are nothing — and the sword raised over the new confessor struck the executioner himself. Both were imprisoned; three days later Diocletian offered them glory and riches if they worshipped his gods — perish with your honour and your riches, they answered — and had them tied by the feet to wild horses, which dragged them unharmed; a red-hot bath-house fell in without hurting them. At last he had a huge furnace lit, and many Christians, heartened by the two, walked into it of their own will crying, we are Christians, and died with prayer on their lips; the bodies of Anicetas and Photius were untouched by the fire, even their hair, and many pagans believed. This was in 305. The Prologue tells it likewise for the Serbian calendar — Anicetas one of the city’s governors, three years in prison — and notes that the two are invoked in the prayers at the blessing of oil and of water.
