# Anatolius

Anatolius, while still a priest, was the one who carried the answers of the Church of Alexandria. He was ordained archbishop of Constantinople in the time of Theodosius the Younger, because the erring Dioscorus hoped to have him for a helper in his heresy. But Dioscorus got nothing by his contrivances: the blessed Anatolius deposed him at the Fourth Ecumenical Council at Chalcedon in the year 451, and put back into the diptychs the name of Saint Flavian, whom Dioscorus and his robber council had deposed.

Afterwards Anatolius sent letters to the bishops, urging them to anathematize the authors of the heresies, that is, Nestorius, Eutyches and Dioscorus. He had followed Flavian on the throne, and having shepherded the Church in the right faith for eight years he departed to the Lord, leaving [Gennadius](/saints/gennadius-patriarch-of-constantinople) in his place.

That is the whole of what doxologia's life gives. The page prints no troparion for him, and no year but the council's, so nothing further is recorded here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same 3 July and sets out the circumstances of his election more fully. saint.gr says he became patriarch of Constantinople two months after the unjust murder of the patriarch Flavian on 11 August 449 by the heretic Dioscorus and his collaborators; that he was Alexandrian born and had been ordained deacon by Cyril, then archbishop of Alexandria; and that Dioscorus supported his election in the hope of having him for a tool, and was disappointed — Anatolius proved a faithful and courageous champion of Orthodoxy.

Under his patriarchate, the page goes on, the Fourth Ecumenical Council met at Chalcedon in 451 and condemned the heresy of Eutyches; Dioscorus was deposed and the name of Flavian, whom Dioscorus had deposed at the robber council of Ephesus in 449, was entered in the diptychs. In his time too the relics of the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste were brought to Constantinople, and the empress Pulcheria built in 450 the churches of the Theotokos of Chalkoprateia, of Blachernae and of the Hodegetria. «Ο Πατριάρχης Ανατόλιος πέθανε τον Ιούλιο του 458 μ.Χ» — he died in July 458, the page says, having greatly profited the faith and the Church.

The interval already recorded here is the year of Chalcedon, 451, which the page confirms; it adds the year of death, 458, which falls just outside it, and the two hymns below.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 3 July, the same menologion day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep him on, and it supplies both ends of a patriarchate the other two books dated only in the middle. He was first a presbyter in the Church of Alexandria, it says, as both of them say; and after the death of the patriarch Flavian he was raised to the throne of the patriarchate of Constantinople in the year 449. The folder’s interval, which had been the year of Chalcedon alone, now runs from that enthronement to his death, both years read off this book and each confirmed by the Greek page, which has Flavian murdered in August 449 and Anatolius patriarch two months later, and his own death in July 458.

It also records something about the Council that neither other book does: that in his time the throne of Constantinople was recognised as equal to the throne of Rome, and that at the Ecumenical Council in Chalcedon in the year 451. He struggled much, it says, for the purity of the Orthodox faith, and suffered much from the heretics — until at last he was killed by them, in the year 458, in the time of the emperor Leo. The Romanian life has him depart to the Lord after eight years and the Greek page says simply that he died; this book alone makes it a murder, and all three readings stand here with none of them chosen. He governed the Church nearly nine years, this book ends, where the Romanian life says eight, and passed over among the holy Hierarchs in the kingdom of God.

The Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery keeps him on 3 июля ст. ст. — «Свт. Анатолия , Патриарха Константинопольского (458).» — the same menologion day, the same see and the same year as the three calendars this folder already carries. It links a life for him, which was not opened, and prints no hymn under his name; the two hymns the day page does print for an Anatolius belong to two monks of the Kyiv Caves who share the name and are not this man.
