# Polyeuctus of Melitene

In the days of Decius and Valerian, emperors of Rome, there were two soldiers of good standing in Melitene in the land of Armenia, Nearchus and Polyeuctus. They had made a friendship such that no love among brothers matched it, for each of them felt that his life and soul were in his friend. Nearchus was an Orthodox Christian, perfect in the law of the Lord; Polyeuctus was a Greek, not yet enlightened by the true faith, though he kept Christian ways and every virtue, and only this was wanting to him, that he was not yet in the house of the Lord.

Nearchus took pains to bring him to the faith, reading him the divine Scriptures and showing him the emptiness of the idols; but the hour of his turning had not come, until the emperors' edict was read out in the markets and streets, compelling everyone to the worship of their gods and threatening torments for any who would not. Preparing himself for death, Nearchus began to grieve for his friend, fearing that the man he had hoped to see a Christian would be frightened by the edict and stay in his old religion to the end, and so the way of salvation would close to him. He wept in secret, and his face was so changed that Polyeuctus pressed him to say what the matter was.

When Nearchus said at last that their friendship was about to be parted, and by something heavier than natural death, Polyeuctus understood, and told him of a vision he had been given some days before: Christ, whom his friend served, had come to him, taken his garment from him and clothed him in another, new, whose price and beauty could not be told, with buttons of gold, and had given him a winged horse. Nearchus read the vision back to him — the old garment laid aside is the change into a better life, and the winged horse is the swift course to heaven — and Polyeuctus answered that he had long known Christ, that his heart had burned when the Gospel was read to him, and that only the Christian name was wanting.

He asked why they should not confess the faith openly, and Nearchus, hearing it with delight, spoke to him of the vanity of this world and of the glory that is in heaven, saying that neither riches nor honour nor the standing of a soldier was dearer to him than the life that is in Jesus Christ. The account doxologia.ro prints for the day says that Polyeuctus was martyred in Melitene, the city of Armenia. Its kontakion for him says that the head of the victorious one was struck off, and sets that beside the Lord's bowing of his head in the Jordan, which is why he is kept in the days of Theophany.
