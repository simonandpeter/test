# Euthymius (Lyubimchenko)

Euthymius — Eustathius Lyubimchenko in the world — was born in 1795 in the
Zenkov district of Poltava province, of the freed household serfs of the
landowner Summa, and was called by grace to the monastic road in his
twenty-third year: from 1818 he is on the novices’ lists of the Glinsk
hermitage, where he passed through the hard obediences given to beginners,
the kitchen, the bakery and the black work, and by his meekness and good
conduct soon drew the notice of the abbot and brethren and was made
cell-attendant to the abbot [Philaret](/saints/philaret-danilevsky), whose
wise instructions he handed on to young monks in his old age, sealing each
with the words, so the elder Philaret taught us. In 1827, already in
cassock and kamilavka, he was made sacristan, a true spiritual comfort for
one so zealous for the house of God, and on 20 December 1832 Philaret
tonsured him with the name Euthymius. An ascetic before his vows, he added
attention to attention after them; the abbot, respecting his strict life
and wishing to make him confessor and elder to the young, offered him the
priesthood, and he refused outright — holy father, he who is to teach
others must first try to know everything himself, and I am only beginning
to learn. Thirty years a sacristan, from 1827 to 1857, he was almost always
in church, the first in at every service and the last out after all the
needs, so that little time was left for rest and food; he spent his free
hours in the garden, planting, digging and watering trees, and in the
little gardens by the brethren’s buildings; and he knew the Typikon and
the hermitage’s elaborate local order so perfectly that all doubts were
brought to him — the living Typikon of Glinsk.

His inner labours he hid, telling of them only to strengthen brethren who
came to him about the fight with the passions: once in his youth, he told
one, the spirit of fornication attacked him so fiercely that he tied a
knotted cord round his bare body, twisted it tight with a stick, and
walked the forest a whole week day and night without food or rest, crying
to the Lord from his heart, until help came and peace with it; when he
took the cord off the inflamed skin had dried to it and came away with it,
one long wound, and looking at the blood he said to himself, smiling,
well, Euthymius, is it good? perhaps you want more? then you will get
worse. The desires of our old man have no end, he taught: get one thing
and the wish flares for a higher; a deacon will surely want to be a
hieromonk, then to have the nabedrennik, then some honourable office, and
so without limit; best to bar that road at once by cutting off the causes
of honour, and put in place of all of it the one search for nearness to
God, by which the soul soonest reaches the rest it wants. And: when you
walk in church, be attentive, holding yourself outwardly as subjects hold
themselves before an earthly king, and inwardly remembering that the King
of heaven is looking at you, for here in his house he shows his special
presence. He was given the schema secretly in the fifties, when he was
retired from the staff, and so kept his name. Retired, he still hurried to
every service and stood it to the end, and asked the abbot for a stall on
the solea by the sacristy in the right aisle of the warm Dormition church,
where he as it were settled, with his few needs about him — books, a
towel, a comb, a stool and a small woollen cushion; he had long since cut
every tie with the world outside and strove to free his senses from
everything visible and created, and lived between two places only, the
church, the place of the common song and prayer, and his cell, the place of
lonely labour, which he kept unlike any ordinary human dwelling. He was
above middle height, very dry from abstinence, slender and quick, with a
long, regular, fasting face, straight nose, bright light eyes and a
moderate handsome grey beard; the drawn features were always recollected
and always bore the signs of inner spiritual joy, and to the attentive his
face showed at times an unnatural pleasant whiteness with a fine, bright,
radiant tinge.

By the end of 1865 age and labour had weakened him, but he was always in
church; at the end of January 1866 he fell wholly ill and was chained to
his bed, and the habit of the house of God, now his nature, drew him so
that he asked the brethren to lead him to the services. Near the end he
asked for the Mysteries, was anointed and communicated, and sat on his bed
peacefully awaiting his passing, a bright smile on his face and tears
falling from his eyes. Father, one of the brethren asked in simplicity, why
do you weep? are you too afraid to die? — What should I fear? To go to my
heavenly Father, and be afraid! No, brother, by God’s goodness I am not
afraid, and the tears you see are tears of joy: so many years my soul has
longed for the Lord, and now the longed-for time draws near; soon I shall
stand before him whom my soul has sought all my life, and see him — so the
tears run. Abiding peacefully in his heart with the sweet Jesus he loved,
he soon breathed his last quiet breath, and his blessed soul left the
much-laboured body and flowed to the Lord with fear and joy, on 7 February
1866, in his seventy-first year. The life is from the Glinsk Paterikon of
the schema-archimandrite John (Maslov); the Council of Bishops of 2017
entered him in the calendar of the Russian Church, which keeps him on 9
September of the old style, 22 September, in the synaxis of the Glinsk
fathers.
