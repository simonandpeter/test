# Nicholas the Presbyter (4 September, the second)

Nicholas Sretensky was born on 15 April 1867 in the village of Tolstikovo in
the Bezhetsk district of Tver province, the son of the priest Alexis
Sretensky. At ten his parents sent him to the Bezhetsk church school, and
from there he went to the Tver seminary; after it he taught half a year in a
zemstvo school and served some months as a spiritual supervisor, and in
1890, at twenty-three, was ordained to the church of the Annunciation
monastery at Bezhetsk, where he served until his arrest in 1937. From 1906
he was a member of the Tver Orthodox missionary society, from 1910 of the
St Barbara charitable society for poor pupils of the diocesan girls’
school, and in 1915 he was given the order of St Anne, third class, for
twenty years of teaching the law of God in the schools. He had two
children; his son Vladimir, born 1895, left the Ekaterinoslav mining
institute for the war, came home to Bezhetsk in the summer of 1918 when the
front collapsed, went abroad with the retreating White army, and finished a
physics and mathematics institute and a mining academy in Paris. Father
Nicholas came to be greatly loved and respected by his people. For all the
merciless persecution of the twenties, some fifty nuns still lived by the
monastery in the mid-thirties, driven from its buildings into private
lodgings but gathering in the church as before, keeping the monastic
services and the monastic way of life; when the town tried to close all its
churches they resisted, and collected the signatures of the faithful under a
petition not to close them.

He was arrested at the very start of the persecution of the summer of 1937,
on 5 August, and questioned the same day and every day after. Asked how he
had taken the closing of the Vvedenskaya church, he said he thought it wrong,
and blamed its priests and council for letting it happen out of fear; asked
whether the faithful complained to him of the government’s measures, he said
no — except that his fifty nuns, taxed heavily for quilting without a
licence, had asked his help in appealing, and he had helped. Asked on
10 August about the “anti-Soviet gatherings of the clergy” Bishop Gregory
(Kozyrev) called, he said there had been no gatherings before the bishop
came, and that he had made no anti-Soviet speeches at them; asked whom he
meant by the demons in his sermons, he said the demons the gospel speaks
of, whom he told the faithful not to believe or yield to; asked whether he
had set his nuns to collecting signatures, he said no, he knew the state
would not meet the petition anyway. He admitted one thing: that shortly
before their arrests he had shown the priest Docuchaev an article in the
local paper calling priests enemies of the people, and the two had been
indignant and concluded that the anti-religious propaganda would show
itself in arrests and banishments. On 14 August and again on 19 August, to
the questions whether he had drawn the young into the church, made
anti-Soviet speeches, warned the clergy to keep their meetings secret from
the NKVD, or belonged to a counter-revolutionary group under Bishop
Gregory, he answered no, and no, and no. On 13 September the NKVD troika
sentenced him to be shot, and he was shot on 17 September 1937 —
4 September of the old style, the day the Russian calendar keeps him, with
the bishops Gregory and Sergius and their company. The Jubilee Council of
Bishops of August 2000 numbered him among the new martyrs and confessors of
Russia.
