# Basil (Konstantinov-Grishin)

The Сретенский monastery’s calendar gives him the last line of the Julian 1 March — «Сщмч. Василия пресвитера (1943).» — and prints no life for him. Basil Matveyevich was born on 23 December 1874 in the village of Vypolzovo in the Kurmysh district of the province of Simbirsk, now the Poretsky district of Chuvashia, the son of the peasant Matthew Grishin. The double surname the calendar gives him has a story, and azbyka.ru tells it: as a boy he looked after a well-off and childless fellow villager, who came to love him so much that he offered him his own surname and made him his heir.

By his papers, the page says, his surname was Konstantinov, while the village called him by his father’s name, Grishin; the case file of 1933 has him as Grishin and the file of 1937 has the double surname. From 1908 he served as psalmist in the village of Chaadayevka in the Ardatov district, in 1911 was moved to Mariopol in the Karsun district and in 1916 to Syresi in the Alatyr district. His theological education was a course of pastoral classes. He was ordained deacon in 1919 and priest in 1923, and in 1926 was deprived of the vote as a minister of religion. By 1933 he was serving in his own village.

On 2 January 1933 the chairman of the village soviet came forward as a witness and said that a kulak grouping existed in the soviet’s area whose object was the collapse of the collective farms from within, and that the priest of Vypolzovo led the whole of it through women who stood close to him. Other false witnesses were found among his fellow villagers. He was arrested on 16 January 1933 and held in the prison at Alatyr, and questioned the same day, and answered that he did not admit the charge, had engaged in no agitation and entered into no conversations with anybody, but on the contrary had always kept himself apolitical as a churchman, and had led no grouping, and had nothing further to say.

On 31 January the prison doctor examined him and wrote out a certificate: weakness of the heart muscle, sclerosis of the vessels, slight swelling of the legs, chronic rheumatism; disablement of the third degree with loss of capacity for work; cannot go on foot any great distance, though five kilometres he can. The bill of indictment, built on the false witnesses, had him leading the grouping and spreading agitation through three named women, and quoted him saying that the power would soon be overturned and so one must not join the collective farm, and that every state had risen against the communists.

It also had him say, coming back among the arrested men after his interrogation, that whatever might be done in the USSR the truth would have its own, for so the Gospel writes, and that this tyranny would not last long: by what measure they measured us, by that it would be measured to them, by the holy Church that is never shaken. And it had him say by the smithy in May 1932 that the collective farm was the old corvée, that it took away the peasant’s freedom and his property, and — worse than that — that one worked day and night and got no more than a piece of bread. On 25 March of that year the case was dropped for want of sufficient evidence and he and the others were released.

He went on serving. For a time he was at Napolnoye in the Poretsky district, and the new wave of repression found him serving at Kudeikha in the same district. On 22 October 1937 he was searched and arrested, held first in the Poretsky district section and then taken to the Alatyr prison. At that day’s interrogation he was required to name the members of his counter-revolutionary organisation, and said he had belonged to no such organisation, had no notion what organisation was meant, had engaged in no agitation and had nothing to talk about.

He denied the close connection with two named kulaks and three named priests, and could not admit it; denied being in the church warden’s house at all; denied ever having preached a sermon about the princes Boris and Gleb as the true defenders of the people. Of the thousand-rouble tax at Napolnoye he said he had served there twenty-two days, that he had indeed been assessed — nine hundred and something, he did not remember exactly — that he had neither taken nor asked money of the faithful for it but paid it himself, and that after paying it he told the village soviet he would not serve and left, and did not run off of his own will.

Asked how often he had been to the bishop at Alatyr and why, he said twice: in 1933 to ask a blessing to leave that diocese for one in the Mordvin region, and in March 1937 for leave to go back to Napolnoye. The last charge put to him was that on returning from the bishop in 1933 he had told a kulak that the bishop had said to him: serve, and remember that the persecution of the church will last no more than two or three years. He did not admit it.

He was charged in one case file with the hieromartyr Demetrius (Voskresensky) and the priest Michael Khramtsov — bonds of spiritual kinship joined the three, azbyka.ru says, and living near one another let them meet often and keep up their friendship — and on 30 December 1937 the troika sentenced all three to ten years in a corrective labour camp. This corpus has no folder for either of the two, so the company is written here in words and carries no related row; when the day that keeps Demetrius is read, the row is owed both ways.

He served the term in the Alatyr colony until 1941, when he was sent on by order of the ГУЛАГ, and he died in confinement in the Temnikov camp in Mordovia on 14 March 1943 and was buried in an unmarked grave in the camp cemetery. The year on the calendar’s line is the year he died in the camp and not a year of execution. On the grade the two hosts read differently and the reading is the calendar’s own index: the line calls him «пресвитера» and azbyka.ru heads him «пресвитер», while the name link behind his name reads «иерей», a priest, which is what the office here records. His is the one page of the sixteen read for this day that azbyka.ru does not attribute to Игумен Дамаскин (Орловский).
