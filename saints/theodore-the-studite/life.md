# Theodore the Studite

There was at Constantinople a man of good family and rich, named Photinus, whose wife was called Theoctista, and both were believing and God-fearing. They bore this blessed Theodore, baptised him after the Christian custom, brought him up with care and gave him to the learning of letters. Those were the years of the heresy that fought the holy icons, while the unjust emperor Constantine Copronymus reigned and there was a great persecution of the faithful; and for that reason Photinus, leaving the city and the rank he held from the emperor, took counsel with his wife and gave away all they had, renounced the world, and both took the monastic life and laboured well in it to the end.

Theodore, having learned the Greek wisdom, became an orator and a chosen philosopher, disputing with the heterodox for the true faith; and he was so skilled in the divine Scriptures and in the dogmas that the heretics could never get the better of him.

When Copronymus the ill-believing emperor died, his son Leo came after him and followed his father's bad faith; but he did not reign long, and soon lost both the empire and his life. After him Irene his wife came to the throne together with her son Constantine. She being of the true belief — as her name, which means peace, declares — brought peace to the Church and stilled the disorder: she called a multitude of venerable Fathers and, together with the most holy Patriarch [Tarasius](/saints/tarasius-of-constantinople), gathered at Nicaea the Seventh Council of the whole world, which cast out the teachings of the unbelieving and decreed again that the divine icons should be honoured and venerated as before.

Three hundred and sixty Fathers were gathered there, and among them the consecrated Plato, who had lived before that on Mount Olympus, and whose memory, the life says, is kept on the fifth day of April. He was Theodore's uncle on his mother's side, and Theodore his nephew; and Plato had the Spirit of God living in him and was of use to all, being skilled in the divine Scriptures and pleasant in speech.

When the Council was over, Plato took with him the blessed Theodore, and with him two of his brothers, Joseph and Euthymius, who wished to take the monastic life; and going with them he came to a place called Sakkoudion, a place very beautiful and well suited to those who wished to live in stillness.

The life doxologia.ro prints is a very long one and closes with the wonders at his grave. A demoniac came to the tomb, and the venerable father appeared to him in sleep and blessed him, and he woke free of the enemy's torment. A man who had eaten poisoned food and was near death took oil from the lamp burning by the tomb and poured it into his mouth, and the deadly poison came out of him, and he was whole from then on. Another, very ill in the stomach, had only to look at the saint's icon and call on his name to be healed; and another, held fast by a terror so that he could no longer speak but fled from everyone in fright, was brought to the tomb and anointed with the oil and was delivered of that affliction at once, and his senses being whole again he gave thanks to God and to His servant.

The life also tells how a man who had been deceived by certain monks against Theodore turned them out of his house in dishonour as the cause of his fault, and afterwards held the venerable one in more faith than before, reading with love the writings and the hymns Theodore had composed, and begging him to forgive that fault. The Romanian life names no year anywhere in what it prints.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 11 November, and gives the dates and the three exiles. saint.gr has him born at Constantinople in 759, the son of Photinos and Theoktiste, with his uncle Plato — one of the greatest figures of the church of Constantinople — the great influence on his upbringing. He became a monk first at the monastery of Sakkoudion near Prusa, which his parents had raised on their estate called Boskition, and later its abbot, when his uncle Plato withdrew for age. For resisting the marriage of the emperor Constantine VI to Theodote he was exiled to Thessalonica; he came back to Constantinople after Constantine's death and was established at the monastery of the Studios as abbot; and again, for resisting the ordination of Nikephoros from layman to patriarch, he was exiled with his uncle Plato in 809. He returned from exile in 812, only to be exiled a third time by Leo V, because he had defended the holy icons and the Orthodox mind with great courage. He died in exile in 826, aged sixty-seven, on the peninsula of Akritas at Saint Tryphon's; his body was taken to the Prinkiponnesos and buried there, and afterwards, under the patriarch Methodios in 844, was brought up to the reigning city together with the relic of his brother Joseph of Thessalonica and buried at the monastery of the Studios. A fragment of his relic, the page adds, is at the Great Lavra on the Holy Mountain.

**The Russian calendar's own year of his birth is not the one this folder carried.** Both of the longer of the three lives it serves him say he was born in 758, the second naming his parents — a collector of the imperial taxes, Photinus, and his wife Theoctista, devout Christians — where saint.gr says 759. The folder now holds both and chooses neither. The same pages add that his father left the state service because the iconoclast court offended him, and that the parents then agreed to give their property to the poor, part, and take the monastic habit, their son following them.

The Russian account follows his exiles closely. For his zeal over the canons and his rebuke of the lawlessness of the emperor Constantine VI he was cruelly tortured and banished in 796 to Thessalonica; under the empress Irene he was brought back and given the deserted Studion monastery to govern; under Leo the Armenian he was exiled again, to the fortress of Metopa in Illyria and then to Bonita in Anatolia, and after that emperor's death he settled at Chersonesus in Bithynia.

There, heavily ill, he served the Divine Liturgy every day and taught the brethren. Foreseeing his end, he charged them to keep Orthodoxy, to honour the holy icons and to observe the monastic rule, then bade them take candles and sing the canon for the departure of a soul; at the words «во век не забуду оправданий Твоих, яко в них оживил мя еси» he died, in 826. At the same hour the abbot of the Dalmatou monastery saw heaven's light shine and heard singing and a voice: «Это душа преподобного Феодора, пострадавшего даже до крови за святые иконы, отходит ко Господу».

The day page carries a troparion and a kontakion to him, both naming him, and they are now in this folder with the English typed here. The troparion is the one the books give to a teaching hierarch — «Православия наставниче, благочестия учителю и чистоты» — and it is his because it is printed under his name and names him, «Феодоре премудре».

The Ohrid Prologue gives him one sentence of titles and one of fact, and the titles are the four this folder already keeps in `types`: the renowned abbot of the monastery of Studion, a great sufferer for the icons, the wise orderer of the monastic life, a God-inspired teacher of Orthodoxy and a wonderful ascetic.

Then the fact, and it agrees with this folder in both halves: he reposed at Constantinople in 826, in the sixty-eighth year of his age. The year is the one the folder already records, and the age the entry gives is consistent with the 758 or 759 it holds for his birth, so nothing here is narrowed and nothing is widened.
