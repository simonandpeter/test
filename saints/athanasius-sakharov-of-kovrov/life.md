# Athanasius (Sakharov) of Kovrov

The future bishop Athanasius was born Sergei Grigorievich Sakharov on 2 July 1887, old style, the feast of the Deposition of the Robe of the Most Holy Theotokos at Blachernae. His parents, Gregory and Matrona, lived at Vladimir; his father was a native of Suzdal and a court counsellor, his mother came of peasants, and their kindness and piety were the soil in which the spiritual gifts of their only son grew. He was named for the mourner of the Russian land, [Sergius of Radonezh](/saints/sergius-of-radonezh), and took deeply into himself the same selfless love of the Church and of the fatherland.

His childhood and youth were spent in the ancient city of Vladimir on the Klyazma, and his difficulties began early. He lost his father young, but found in his mother everything he needed to enter life worthily; she wished to see him in the monastic rank, and he was grateful to her for it all his life. He went gladly to the parish church and was never wearied by the length of the services. Worship, as the highest degree of prayer, was the chief love of his life; from childhood he had a foretaste of himself as a servant of the Church, and told even his companions boldly that he would be a bishop.

The pious boy learned handwork easily and could sew and embroider even church vestments. That served him later, in the exiles and the camps, where he sewed vestments and robes for icons, and once made a portable antimension and served the Liturgy for the prisoners on it. His first lessons came hard, but he did not slacken, and he finished the Vladimir seminary and then the Moscow Theological Academy, unexpectedly for everybody, with great success; it did not change his modest and humble bearing towards people.

He went deepest into liturgics and hagiology. In the services he found a theology of his own, and he was close with the text of the service books: the margins of his own copies carry a multitude of notes, corrections and explanations of difficult words. Still at the Shuya church school he wrote his first liturgical hymn, a troparion to the honoured Shuya-Smolensk icon of the Mother of God, and his academy essay, «The disposition of the believing soul according to the Lenten Triodion», already shows how much he knew of church hymnology, which stayed one of his chief loves to the end.

His first teacher and spiritual guide was Nicholas (Nalimov), archbishop of Vladimir. The next was the known theologian and strict ascetic Theodore (Pozdeyevsky), rector of the Moscow Academy, who tonsured him in the church of the Protection of the Mother of God with the name Athanasius, for the Patriarch of Constantinople of that name, and from whose hand he received ordination as hierodeacon and then as hieromonk; but it was the monastic tonsure itself that he valued in some particular way. His obediences began at the Poltava seminary, where he was at once noticed as a gifted teacher, and he came into his full strength as a scholar back at his own Vladimir seminary.

He was thirty, and a hieromonk, when the revolution came. In 1917 the representatives of all the Russian men's monasteries gathered at the lavra of Saint Sergius, and that assembly elected him a member of the historic Local Council of the Russian Church of 1917–18, where he worked in the department of liturgical questions. There he began the famous service to All Saints who have shone in the Russian land. It was his thought to take for the stichera at «Lord, I have cried» one sticheron from the General Menaion for each rank of saints, and to arrange the canon by regions, each ode closing with a troparion to the icon of the Mother of God most honoured in that region.

Metropolitan Sergius (Stragorodsky), reviewing the new service as a member of the Synod, put into it a troparion of his own composing; the first version was then looked over by Patriarch [Tikhon of Moscow](/saints/tikhon-of-moscow). Meanwhile the new power began its coarse mockery of the relics of the saints, the destruction of the clergy and the ruin of the churches, and the believing people saw in it the fulfilment of the warning prophecies about the perishing of the Russian kingdom, which the page quotes from [John of Kronstadt](/saints/john-of-kronstadt): its turning into a rabble of men of other faiths, striving to destroy one another.

In 1919 the anti-religious campaign reached the incorrupt remains of the saints. At Vladimir, as in other Russian cities, the opened relics were put on show to the people, uncovered. To cut the outrage short the Vladimir clergy, under the hieromonk Athanasius of the diocesan council, set a watch in the Dormition cathedral, where the relics lay on tables. The first watch was Athanasius himself and the psalmist Alexander Potapov; when the doors opened he proclaimed «Blessed is our God…», and «Amen» came back, and a moleben to the saints of Vladimir began. The people came in crossing themselves, bowing and setting candles by the relics, and the intended profanation turned into a festal glorification.

He was soon made archimandrite and vicar of two ancient houses of the diocese, the Bogolyubsky and the Nativity of the Theotokos at Vladimir; and then, on 10 July 1921, at Nizhny Novgorod, on the memory of Sampson the Hospitable, he was consecrated bishop of Kovrov, vicar of the Vladimir diocese, by Metropolitan Sergius (Stragorodsky), the future patriarch. His chief care and pain as a bishop was neither the opposition of the authorities nor the ruin nor even the closing of the churches, but a new schism inside the Church itself, the one known as Renovationism.

He taught his flock that the schismatics who had risen against the canonical episcopate headed by Patriarch Tikhon had no right to celebrate the Mysteries, and that the churches in which they served were without grace. He reconsecrated the churches they had defiled, urged those who had fallen away to repent together with their parish, and reproved those who would not; and while forbidding communion with them, so as to shame them, he asked his people to bear them no malice for their seizing of the holy places, since the saints, as he said, are always in spirit with the Orthodox alone.

His first arrest was on 30 March 1922, and it began many years of prisons. Strangely, he thought the prisoner's condition the easier one, lighter than that of those who stayed at liberty under endless pressure from the Renovationists; he even called the prison «an isolation ward against the Renovationist epidemic». The road through prisons and exiles was unending and exhausting: the prisons of Vladimir, the Taganka in Moscow, Zyryansk and Turukhansk; the camps of Solovki, the White Sea–Baltic, the Onega, the Mariinsk camps in the Kemerovo region, the Temnikov camps in Mordovia.

He could be arrested on the road, as happened once while he was going round the Yuryev-Polsky district, and in 1937 and 1938 he was more than once arrested and prepared for immediate shooting. At the beginning of the Great Patriotic War he was sent to the Onega camps of the Archangel region on foot, the prisoners carrying their own things, and grew so weak from the road and the hunger that he made serious preparation for death. The Onega camps gave way to exile without term in the Omsk region, where he worked as a night watchman in the vegetable gardens of a state farm near the little town of Golyshmanovo, and then to the town of Ishim, where he lived on what friends and spiritual children sent him.

In the winter of 1942 he was moved unexpectedly to Moscow, and the investigation lasted half a year. He was questioned about thirty times, usually at night, usually for some four hours, but once for nine hours together; sometimes four hours produced a single sheet of protocol and sometimes more than ten. Not once did he give anybody away, and not once did he incriminate himself. The sentence was eight years in the Mariinsk camps of the Kemerovo region, which were famous for their cruelty, and the work set for «ideological enemies of Soviet power» was the heaviest and the dirtiest there was.

In the summer of 1946 he was taken to Moscow again on a false denunciation, but the informer withdrew his testimony and he was sent to the Temnikov camps in Mordovia to finish his term. He was physically weak by then and could only plait bast shoes; two years later he was sent to Dubrovlag, in the same Mordovia, where his age and his health meant he no longer worked at all. On Solovki he had caught typhus and been in danger of death, and survived, the page says, by a plain wonder.

He never lost his faith or his great thankfulness to God. Barely alive after the torments, holding back a groan, he would often say to those near him, «Let us pray, let us praise God!» and be the first to begin «Praise the name of the Lord»; and the singing revived him. New prisoners he encouraged: do not lose heart, the Lord in his great mercy has granted you to suffer a little for him, thank God for it. He kept the Church's rule without fail, never broke off his prayer rule, prayed with his cellmates as well as alone, and kept the fasts strictly even in the camp, finding ways to cook fasting food.

The hymnography of his life was written in those cells. The service to All the Russian Saints came to its finished form after discussion with the hierarchs imprisoned with him — one of them Thaddeus, archbishop of Tver, whom the Church has since glorified as a hieromartyr — and on 10 November 1922, in cell 172 of the Vladimir prison, the feast of All the Russian Saints was first kept by the corrected service. His mother's death moved him to write his fundamental work «On the commemoration of the departed according to the Rule of the Orthodox Church», which Metropolitan Cyril (Smirnov) valued highly.

In August 1941 he composed a «Moleben service for the fatherland», full of deep repentance and of an uncommon power of prayer, taking in every side of the country's life; and in the times of imprisonment he wrote the moleben services «For those in sorrows and various afflictions», «For enemies, those who hate and wrong us», «For those in prisons and confinement», «Thanksgiving for alms received» and «For the ending of wars and for the peace of the whole world». He sang to God, as the page puts it, «while I have being», and sang even in the gates of death.

On 9 November 1951 the last of the camp terms ended; he was sixty-four. Even then he was kept in complete ignorance of what was to come, and then placed by force in the invalid home at the station of Potma in Mordovia, whose regime differed hardly at all from the camp's. He was released from it on 7 March 1955, its camp discipline having finally broken his health. He settled first in Tutayev — Romanov-Borisoglebsk — in the Yaroslavl region, and then chose the village of Petushki in the Vladimir region for his home.

Formally free from then on, he was still hemmed in at every turn: at Petushki he was allowed to serve only with the church doors shut and without his episcopal regalia. In 1957 the prosecutor's office of the Vladimir region looked again at the case of 1936 under which he had been tried; he was questioned at home, the arguments he gave in his defence were not found convincing, and no rehabilitation followed. His consolation was the services at the Trinity-Sergius lavra, since, remembering his monastic tonsure within its walls, he always counted himself among its brotherhood; several times he concelebrated with Patriarch Alexius (Simansky), and on 12 March 1959 he took part in the consecration of the archimandrite Nikon (Lysenko) as bishop of Ufa.

At one of his services the people praying noticed that during the eucharistic canon he walked above the floor of the church, as though some wave were carrying him smoothly out of the altar. He bore the new liberal persecution of the Church in the years of the thaw heavily, and multiplied his prayers to the Russian saints and to the Mother of God, the protectress of Russia; he even came to see his own retirement as a flinching from the fight with the advancing evil, and wanted to ask to be made a vicar bishop again, but his broken health would not let him return to public service. However hard his life was, he never fell into despondency.

At Petushki he received as many as eight hundred letters a year, keeping up correspondence with many of his former fellow prisoners, whose sorrows he lived through as his own, and at Christmas and Pascha he sent out thirty or forty parcels to those in need of help and comfort. His spiritual children remember how simple and attentive he was, how he valued the smallest service and always tried to repay it. He hardly noticed people's appearance; he did not love human glory or honour, and taught that good should be done only for the glory of God, so as not to lose the reward to come, and that talents are God's gift and no ground for pride.

Asked once how a man is to be saved, he answered: the chief thing is faith, for without faith even the best deeds do not save, because faith is the foundation of everything; the second is repentance; the third is prayer; the fourth is good works; and worse than any sin is despair. He taught that one should come to repentance as often as possible, at once, as soon as a sin is recognised, and cleanse the soul with the tears of repentance. His own prayer filled his whole life and was so living and strong that those who prayed with him were taken out of everything earthly, and many received quick help through it. He hid his gift of foresight, and showed it only in exceptional cases and only for his neighbours' good.

In August 1962 he began to say that it was time for him to die. When he was once told that his close children would not bear the parting, he answered sternly: can one be so attached to a man? By this we damage our love for the Lord — you are not left alone, but with the Lord. A few days before his end the lavra's vicar, the archimandrite Pimen, the dean, the archimandrite Theodorit, and the confessor, the abbot Cyril, came to him, which gladdened him greatly; it was the eve of the fiftieth anniversary of his monastic tonsure, and on the day itself, a Thursday, he was especially gracious and blessed all who were present.

Then death came near. He could no longer speak, sunk in prayer; but on the Friday evening he said quietly, for the last time, «Prayer will save you all!» and then wrote with his hand on the blanket, «Save, O Lord!» On Sunday 28 October 1962, on the memory of the hierarch [John of Suzdal](/saints/john-of-suzdal), he quietly gave up his spirit to God, having foretold that day and that hour beforehand. The Russian calendar keeps him on that day in its own reckoning, the Julian 15 October, with a polyeleos and in bold, the only entry of the day it marks so; the Greek, Romanian and Serbian calendars were read for the same day and none of the three keeps him.
