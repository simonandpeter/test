# The Martyrs of the Lavra of Saint Sabbas

In the days of Elias, the second patriarch of Jerusalem of that name, when the Holy City was under Arab rule and Constantine and Irene reigned at Constantinople, war broke out among the Arab tribes in Palestine. The barbarians laid waste not only villages but cities — Eleutheropolis, Ascalon, Gaza, Scarifia and others — sat on every road, and stripped travellers of everything down to their shirts, showing only this much mercy, that they let them go alive.

The lavra of Saint Sabbas suffered greatly. The fathers gathered there under the abbot Basil could have left it and been safe inside Jerusalem, but they would not abandon the holy place where they had first taken the yoke of Christ. Being dead to the world, they said among themselves that it would be shameful, after so short a time of struggle in the desert, to run back to the world for fear of men, and that they had no stone walls to defend them; and they put their hope in Christ, who could deliver them if He willed, or send them a quick martyrdom if that were better.

The raiders came first demanding gold. The fathers answered that they were so poor they had neither bread enough nor clothing enough, and never dreamed of gold or silver. At that the barbarians loosed their arrows like rain until their quivers were empty, striking thirteen of the fathers dead and wounding others; then they broke the cell doors with great stones, carried off the monks' few things and set the cells on fire, and would have burned the church had they not taken fright at men approaching in the distance and fled. Abba Thomas, a skilled physician, drew the arrows out and dressed the wounds.

On Palm Sunday the fathers learned that the raiders were gathering more of their fellows to fall on the lavra again. Great fear and trembling took them, but they did not run: they made ready for death and waited. On the morning of 20 March, Great Thursday, the Arabs came a second time in greater force and killed without sparing — shooting some, beheading others with swords, cutting some in two, cutting off the hands and feet of others, stoning others — and gathered the survivors into the church to be tortured until they gave up the treasure of the church, while watchers on the hills cut off every escape.

John, the young guestmaster, was caught fleeing to the mountain: they cut the sinews of his hands and feet, then dragged him by the legs over the rocks from the summit to the church until the skin of his back was flayed away. Sergius, who kept the church vessels, fled for fear that under torture he would say where he had hidden them, and was taken by the watch and brought back and beheaded. Some fathers hid in a cave outside the monastery, and when a barbarian stood at its mouth with a sword and called them out, Patricius whispered to the others to keep silent, went out alone, said that he was the only one there, and was led away to die for his friends.

The raiders demanded four thousand gold pieces for the fathers' lives and the church, and were answered that not one piece was to be found in the whole lavra, and that they might take the clothes off the monks' backs and leave them their lives. Then they drove the fathers into the cave of Saint Sabbas, lit a fire at its mouth and heaped on rubbish to make a great smoke, and tormented them with it to make them show the treasure. They brought them out and threatened them with dreadful deaths, and heard nothing from them but prayer — one crying, Lord, receive my spirit in peace, and another, Remember me, Lord, when Thou comest into Thy Kingdom.

Thrown back into the cave and smoked again, eighteen of the fathers died, John and the blessed Patricius among them; the names of the rest, who died by smoke, by the sword and by other torments, God alone knows, who has written them in the book of life. The barbarians loaded what they could find on the monastery's own camels and went away, and died soon after in the wilderness. That night — the night of the saving Passion — the brethren who were left brought the bodies into the church, which by God's protection had not been burned, kept vigil over them in inconsolable mourning, and buried them with honour; and Thomas the physician, who survived, tended the half-dead.

The page adds that the account was written at length by the blessed Stephen the Sabbaite — not the one kept on 13 July, but another of the same name, called the verse-maker, of that same monastery — who wrote it immediately after the killing, and that the compiler found it only in small hand-written Greek booklets, many of whose leaves were torn. Stephen ends it with a story a trustworthy priest told him: a Syrian who could not master Greek was visited in his sleep by one of these martyred fathers, the protodeacon Anastasius, who wiped his tongue with a clean cloth and vanished, and from that day the man spoke and read Greek well.

The Greek calendar keeps them on the same day, under a name of their own — the Abbades, those called the Black — and its account is not doxologia's in either the year or the number. saint.gr puts the monastery at Jerusalem and the raid in the reign of Heraclius (620–641), when barbarian Arabs fell on it thinking it held great treasures and were proved wrong: the only wealth the monks had, and the page makes them forty-four by tradition, was their virtues, and their keep was frugal and came by the sweat of their own faces. Finding no spoil the raiders turned on the monks in fury, gathered them and told them to deny their faith in Christ; and since not one of them would, they decided to kill them — beheading some, splitting others in two, cutting others into many pieces and running others through with their swords. The page names the two writers it rests on, [Stephen the Sabbaite](/saints/stephen-the-sabbaite), kept on 13 Ιουλίου and nephew of John of Damascus, and Antiochus Pandectes, kept on 24 Δεκεμβρίου, and says that a Theoctistus is mentioned among the Abbades — without saying which of the several the calendars keep. It adds that their memory is repeated on 16 Μαΐου. The reign it gives is a century and a half from the one the Romanian life gave, and neither has been preferred here: the corpus's interval is left as it was, and the disagreement is recorded on the Greek row.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the company on the same menologion day, and on the one point where the two other calendars disagree it sides with the Romanian. It names the monastery of Saint Sabbas the Sanctified near Jerusalem — visited, it adds, by Saint Sabbas of Serbia and endowed by several Serbian rulers, and standing to this day, attacked many times by wild Arabs, plundered and laid waste, and always restored by the providence of God. In the reign of Constantine and Irene it was attacked by Arabs and plundered.

The monks would not flee. Taking counsel with their abbot Thomas they said: we have fled the world into this desert for the love of Christ, and it is a shame now to flee the desert for fear of men; if we are killed here, we shall be killed for our love of Christ, for whose sake we came here to live. Having so resolved they met the armed Arabs unarmed, as lambs meet wolves. Some of them the Arabs killed with arrows, and some they shut in the cave of Saint Sabbas and lit a fire at the entrance and smothered with the smoke; and so many of them ended as martyrs for Christ's sake.

The Prologue puts that before Pascha in 796, in the reign of Constantine and Irene and under Elias, patriarch of Jerusalem — the same reign and the same patriarch the Romanian life gives, and not the reign of Heraclius the Greek page prints. The year falls inside the interval this folder already records, which is therefore left as it stands. It closes by saying that a just punishment soon overtook the wild attackers: returning to their tents they quarrelled, and in the fighting among themselves they all perished, in that same year.

The Russian calendar's reading tells the ruin as two raids a week apart and gives three of the dead their names. Through the eighth century the country round Jerusalem was raided again and again, and the monastery of Chariton was destroyed and left empty; twice the Saracens tried to plunder this lavra and were kept off. The monks could have sheltered inside Jerusalem and would not leave the place where they had been saved for so many years.

On 13 March, in the week before Palm Sunday, the raiders broke in and demanded everything of value. Told there was nothing in the house but poor food and worn clothing, they shot into the monks with bows: thirteen were killed, many wounded, and the cells burned. They would have fired the church too, but took a crowd seen in the distance for troops out of Jerusalem and hurried off with the little they had seized. One of the fathers, a skilled physician, tended the living.

On Great Thursday, the twentieth of March, they came back in greater number. The survivors were driven into the church to be tortured for the hiding-place of the treasure, and the house was ringed so that none could escape. John, a very young monk who had charge of the guests, was tortured, the tendons of his hands and feet cut, and dragged down the mountain by the feet with the skin torn from his back. Sergius, who kept the church vessels, hid them, tried to flee, was caught and beheaded. Patricius was in a cave with some of the brethren when a watchman on the hill called them out; he whispered to them to sit still and be silent, went out alone, and told the Saracen he had been there by himself.

Four thousand gold pieces were demanded of those left alive, and the sacred vessels with them, and the monks could not pay. They were shut in the founder's own cave inside the monastery wall, and a fire was lit before the mouth of it and fed with dung so that they should choke on the smoke; eighteen died in there, John and Patricius among them. Late in the night of Great Friday the monks who had hidden in the hills came back, carried the bodies into the church and buried them there in grief. The reading closes with the raiders struck by a sudden sickness and dying to the last man.
