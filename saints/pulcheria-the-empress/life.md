# Pulcheria the Empress

Pulcheria took part in both councils, that of Ephesus and that of Chalcedon,
as a great defender of the faith; in her was the zeal of right belief and
great wisdom, for in her pure soul and heart the Holy Spirit dwelt as in a
holy church. When the emperor Arcadius died he left his son Theodosius,
eight years old, and three daughters, Pulcheria, Arcadia and Marina;
Pulcheria was five years older than her brother, very wise and pure, and
God’s providence gave her to the Greek empire as a gift, for the help of the
young Theodosius and the defence of Orthodoxy, then troubled by heretics.
With an understanding beyond her years she was made Augusta with her
brother, and was sixteen when, taking the imperial power, she began to
order the empire not with a woman’s knowledge but with a man’s wisdom, so
that the whole world marvelled. For the love of God and the peace of the
Greeks she would not take a husband, lest there be division between husband
and brother, but promised herself to God as his bride and meant to stay a
virgin until death, and as the sign of it set up in the great church of
Constantinople a wonderful altar of gold and precious stones. She led her
sisters to the same vow, and the three lived together in fasting and
prayer, Arcadia and Marina obeying her as a mother and an empress; and she
was a mother too to Theodosius, teaching him the fear of God, and — knowing
Greek and Latin well — was herself his teacher in letters and in good
conduct, and the seed fell on good ground, so that grown to manhood he was
gentler, more patient, wiser and more merciful than other emperors. She
built a glorious church to the Mother of God and many churches and
monasteries at Blachernae, gave alms without stint, and by her care the
empire was at peace and free of heretical division. When Theodosius was
twenty she looked for a bride worthy of him, and found Athenais, a pagan of
Athens, beautiful and learned, the daughter of the philosopher Leontius,
who had come to the City to complain of her brothers — her father had left
them everything and her a few gold pieces, saying her beauty and wisdom
were enough for her; Pulcheria brought her to baptism, made her a daughter,
named her Eudocia and married her to the emperor, and their daughter was
married to Valentinian III at Rome. In their reign the Third Ecumenical
Council met at Ephesus against Nestorius, by Pulcheria’s zeal, and when her
brother began to be drawn into the heresy she counselled him and held him
in the right faith, for which the holy fathers praised her.

Then the enemy, unable to bear the scattering of his heresies, armed
himself against her. The emperor’s favourite, the eunuch Chrysaphius,
full of cunning and greed and a heretic, rose against the patriarch Flavian
— who, asked for a gift of blessing on his election, sent pure loaves, and
told that gold, not bread, was expected, answered that Chrysaphius knew
well the church’s gold and silver were God’s and could be given to none but
the poor — and, finding Pulcheria the patriarch’s shield, set about sowing
tares between her and the empress. The occasion came from a lesson she
taught her brother: Theodosius signed whatever was put before him without
reading it, so she drew up a deed by which the emperor gave his wife into
slavery to his sister, had him sign it unread, invited Eudocia to her
palace, kept her there with every honour, and when the emperor sent for
her twice answered, smiling, that he had no power over his empress, having
given her to Pulcheria under his own hand — then went to him and said, you
see, emperor, that it is not well to sign what you have not read. Out of
that Chrysaphius made the empress’s anger — she wants to make you her
slave; how long will you bear it? — and Eudocia urged her husband to take
the power from his sister and rule alone. Ashamed to dishonour her openly,
he asked the patriarch to persuade her, when she came to church, to become
a deaconess, as pure virgins were then pressed to be, and so remove her from
power by a holy service; Flavian told her in secret, and she, seeing her
brother’s contrivance and the enmity of the empress and the eunuch, laid
the power down herself and withdrew with her women to a quiet place to serve
God in silence. Then Chrysaphius turned the emperor against the patriarch,
the heretics rose without fear, and God’s church was in turmoil with
Pulcheria gone from its defence — until God opened the emperor’s eyes. An
apple of uncommon size and beauty was brought him; he sent it to the
empress, who sent it, uneaten, to the senator Paulinus, the emperor’s dear
friend, who lay sick, and Paulinus, knowing nothing, sent it to the emperor.
Where is the apple I sent you, he asked her; I ate it, she said; and he
showed it her. From that day he suspected her with Paulinus, banished him
to Cappadocia and later had him killed, would not see his wife, and,
learning at last what Chrysaphius had been, stripped him and banished him
— and God avenged Pulcheria’s innocent wrong, for Chrysaphius drowned at
sea. Eudocia, grieving beyond measure that a good and blameless man had
died for her, swore on oath after the emperor’s death that both were
innocent, asked leave to go to Jerusalem, and stayed there long, giving
alms and building churches and monasteries, until her prayers softened the
emperor’s anger and reconciled Pulcheria to her; and as a sign of peace she
sent Pulcheria the icon of the Mother of God painted by the evangelist
Luke. Theodosius begged his sister back to the throne, and she would not,
preferring to serve God alone in solitude rather than rule many lands,
until his insistence won her; she returned with great honour, and at once
the heretical storm died down and the church was calm and the empire at
peace. When the hand of the first martyr Stephen was brought from Jerusalem
to Chalcedon the saint appeared to her that night — you have your desire,
for I too have come to Chalcedon — and she and her brother went out to
meet it.

At forty-two Theodosius came to his end, and told her of a revelation
given him at Ephesus in the church of St John the Theologian: that after
his death the soldier Marcian would take the sceptre, and he asked her to
help him to it. Marcian was a soldier’s son and a brave soldier himself,
wise, merciful and good, and had been marked for the empire since his
youth: near Philippopolis, stopping to bury a murdered man he found on the
road, he was taken for the murderer and condemned, and saved only when God
revealed the true killer; a prisoner of the Vandals, he was seen by
Genseric from a height, asleep in the noon heat with an eagle shading him
with its spread wings, and was freed on an oath never to make war on the
Vandals; ill on the road to the Persian war, he was nursed at Sidina in
Lycia by two brothers, Tatian and Julian, who saw the same eagle over him
as he slept in the fields and foretold his reign, and whom he promised to
treat as fathers. On Theodosius’s death — Eudocia went back to Jerusalem,
lived some years well, died and was buried in the church of St Stephen she
had built — Pulcheria took counsel with the senate and the commanders and
chose Marcian, as a worthy man and God’s elect; he gave the two brothers
Thrace and Lycia to govern, and kept his oath to Genseric to the end. She
wished then to go back to her solitude, and the new emperor and the whole
court begged her to stay and help them govern, which could only be as the
emperor’s wife. She answered that she had promised God her virginity to the
death; he answered that he too had promised God his chastity; and for the
need of the church, troubled by heretics who had multiplied, she consented
to a marriage in which the flower of her virginity never faded — she was
fifty-one and he no longer young — so that she was called the emperor’s
wife and was in truth his sister, and he her husband and in truth her
brother, and virgin purity could be seen reigning on the throne of the
Greeks. By her zeal the council of the holy fathers gathered at Chalcedon
against the impious Dioscorus and the archimandrite Eutyches, as the one at
Ephesus had by her care before it. She lived fifty-four years, gave all she
had to the church, the monasteries and the poor, and went to God, whom she
had served with all her heart. The Greek synaxarion keeps her on
10 September and refers to her feast of 17 February with Marcian; the
Romanian calendar keeps her on 10 September.
