# Demetrius (Troitsky) (21 October)

The Russian church calendar of the Сретенский monastery names him tenth of fifteen on 21 октября ст. ст., in a line of eleven priests, two deacons and two monks of 1937. His father Ivan Troitsky was an official of the excise administration, and all four of his sons became priests. Dmitry was the youngest, born on 20 July 1905. He had three years of elementary school at Kashin and then the theological school, which outlived the revolution and in 1921 was renamed a Soviet school of the second grade; he finished it in 1923. His father was dead by then and his priest brothers paid for his schooling.

He was ordained priest at twenty, to the church of the village of Peretyerye in the Sonkovo district, knowing quite well that he was beginning his ministry in an unbroken persecution. He preached at every service and prepared his sermons with care, lived his parishioners' troubles with them, and tried to keep the fire of faith alight in a surrounding darkness of state unbelief, teaching them to care first for what is of the spirit and to fear sin more than the authorities. The parish came to life.

People came oftener to confession and communion, and brought their children up in the Church. For this the authorities began looking for a way to take him out of the parish, and in 1930 arrested him for keeping fifty-one silver roubles and sentenced him to three years, which he served building the White Sea–Baltic canal.

He came back to the same church unchanged, and the search for a pretext began again. In 1936 he was charged with breaking the rules for registering births and deaths and sentenced by the Sonkovo court to six months of corrective labour; he refused the charge, appealed to the regional court, and was released. Returning to his parish and expecting the persecution to grow rather than end, he used every opening he had. Preaching on the first Sunday of Lent in 1936 he called his people to repentance and told them to fall down before the Cross, and on the Protection he preached that the people of Constantinople, surrounded by their enemies and knowing their own weakness, did not lose heart but went into the church to pour out their prayer.

On 28 April 1937 a precinct inspector reported to the Sonkovo militia that a schoolgirl at Purshevo, asked by the head teacher why she went to church, had answered that the priest told them that if they prayed their lessons would come easier, that they should not listen to the teachers, and that they should wear their crosses; her mother, who had three children at the school, had threatened the girl for repeating it.

The report was left alone until the summer, when the NKVD had its orders for the destruction of the Church, and then it became the ground of the charge. Asked for more, the chairman of the village soviet wrote what reads as a testimonial: that since the priest's arrival church life had quickly revived, that he followed every service with a sermon, that he drew the schoolchildren in through their parents, and that his presence in the village showed itself in the lateness of every campaign.

His house was searched on 19 September 1937 and he was arrested and taken to the prison at Bezhetsk. The head teacher and a teacher of the Purshevo school testified. He was charged with turning the children from the school, with discrediting Stalin because a notebook of religious verses had the leader's portrait on its cover and a brochure lay inside a cover printed with the address of the first all-union congress of collective-farm shock workers, with frightening schoolchildren by the word of God, with complaining that the state persecuted the clergy as under Ivan the Terrible, and with writing to acquaintances that materialists do not value the soul and destroy the spiritual foundations of a family.

He answered each charge and admitted none: the brochure was not his and he had not read it, the portrait was a cover and nothing more, his sermons were moral teaching for adults and for children who wished to come, and confessions were held on Sundays when the school was not in session.

The investigation closed the same day. The indictment bore witness, against its own intention, to the priest's confession of faith: he was, it said, a finished counter-revolutionary churchman, an opponent of socialist construction in every fibre of his thought and action, who on his return from imprisonment had not given up his convictions but had gone back to serving in church — and who, taken on by a short-sighted collective farm board, had earned a hundred labour-days in 1936. The troika condemned him on 1 November and he was shot two days later, on 3 November 1937. He was numbered among the saints at the Jubilee Council of Bishops in August 2000.
