# Theodore Stratelates

Theodore was born at Euchaita, not far from the city of Heraclea by the Black Sea, and the emperors had set him as *stratilat* — voivode, the commander of the place — with Heraclea given into his keeping as a man worthy of it for the courage he was famous for. The life calls him brave and manly, very beautiful of face, wise and a chosen orator, so that men called him *Vriorítor*, which is interpreted "a fountain of oratory". What follows is what his servant Varus, the notary, wrote down as the passion of his master.

His courage was already known from the dragon of Euchaita. North of the city lay an empty plain with a great chasm in it, and in the chasm a dragon that devoured whatever came before it, man or beast. Theodore rode out alone, telling nobody his mind, armed as usual and with a costly cross at his breast, saying to himself that he would deliver his inheritance from it by the power of Christ. An old believing woman named Eusebia, who years before had asked for the body of Theodore the Tiron and buried it with spices in her house at Euchaita, found him asleep in the grass and woke him to warn him off; he told her to go and stand far away and see the power of Christ.

The persecution was that of Licinius, who had taken the sceptre from Maximian and followed him in everything: he had killed the forty martyrs at Sebaste and forty-five soldiers at Nicopolis in Armenia, seventy chosen soldiers and nobles of his own palace and three hundred men of Macedonia, and then gave orders to seek out only the most notable Christians and force them to the idols. Word came to him at Nicomedia of the stratelates of Heraclea, who had shown himself a Christian and was turning many to Christ, and he sent officers to bring Theodore to him with honour.

Theodore feasted the envoys and gave them gifts, and on the third day would not go; he sent a letter asking the emperor to come to Heraclea himself, and to bring the great gods with him, on the pretext that the city was falling away and needed both a sacrifice and its emperor. He wanted, the life says, to suffer in his own city and to hallow it with his own blood. Licinius read it and rejoiced, and came with about eight thousand of his army and of the officials of Nicomedia, and with the greatest idols of gold and silver. In the night Theodore saw the roof opened and a light like fire come down on his head, and a voice: «Take courage, Theodore, for I am with thee.»

Set up in the middle of the city, Licinius praised the place and the man and called on him to sacrifice before the people. Theodore asked to keep the gods of gold and silver in his own house for two nights first, and that night broke every one of them into small pieces and distributed them to the poor. A centurion named Maxentius had seen the golden head of Artemis in a beggar's hands and told the emperor so. Asked before the whole city, Theodore said it was true and well done, for what could not help itself when it was being cut apart could not help the emperor; and he set his own titles against the emperor's, that Licinius was of Thrace and he a nobleman of Rome.

Then he was stretched out naked and beaten with an ox sinew by soldiers relieving each other, six hundred strokes on the back and five hundred on the belly; then beaten with rods of lead, torn with iron claws, burned with lighted torches and his wounds rubbed with sharp potsherds, and he said nothing but "Glory to Thee, our God". After five days shut up in irons without food he was crucified, nailed hand and foot as Christ was under Pilate, and young men and boys shot arrows into his face until the light of his eyes was put out. Varus threw down the little book he was writing and wept at his feet, and was told to keep to his service and write the day of the end.

Licinius thought him dead and left him hanging on the cross. Before the changing of the night watch an angel of the Lord took him down and made him whole and sound as he had been, and told him to finish his course. The two centurions sent to fetch the body and sink it in the sea found the cross empty and Theodore sitting and praising God, and they believed, and seventy soldiers with them, and then the three hundred sent to kill those who had believed. When the crowd rose to kill the tyrant Theodore quieted them, loosed the prisoners and opened the prison doors with a word, and bowed his head to the sword on the eighth day of February, a Saturday, at the third hour of the day; his body was carried to Euchaita in glory on the eighth of June.

The life ends by distinguishing the two great martyrs named Theodore who belong to Euchaita. Theodore the Tiron suffered earlier, under Maximian and Maximin, in the year 289 from the birth of Christ, and is written of on the seventeenth of this month; Theodore Stratelates suffered twenty-two or twenty-three years after him, in the days of Licinius, and in the year 312 he too was carried to Euchaita. Both, it says, are men of Euchaita.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and tells the martyrdom out. It has him come from Euchaita in Galatia and live at Herakleia on the Euxine Pontus, a soldier by profession who was marked out for his courage and was quickly raised to the highest ranks of the army, and who was at the same time a pattern of modesty, as befits a true Christian. In the year 320, while Licinius was staying at Nicomedia, the emperor heard that Theodore was a Christian and abominated the idols, and sent senior officers to Herakleia to bring him with honour to Nicomedia; but Theodore sent word back by the same messengers that for many reasons his own presence at Herakleia was the more useful, and urged the emperor to come there instead. Licinius accepted, came, and was met with splendour by Theodore, and he put out his hand to him in the hope of drawing the Christians to the religion of the idols through him. One day, before the people, he called on Theodore to sacrifice; and Theodore refused, and asked instead that the gold and silver figures of the gods be given him, so that he might offer them in sacrifice privately at his own house and then sacrifice publicly. He took them, broke them in pieces, and shared the gold and silver of them among the poor. The centurion Maxentius saw the head of the goddess Aphrodite in a poor man's hands and reported it, and Licinius, taking him for a mocker and a despiser of the idols, had him seized and punished in many ways at once: they beat him, burned him and flayed his body, then crucified him, drove a pin through his feet, his hands and his secret parts, and shot at his face until his eyes were put out, and left him on the cross. Fearing the anger of the crowd, Licinius ordered him beheaded, and so fear gave place to joy, and grief and labour to rest. The page adds that his body was moved on 8 June from Herakleia to his ancestral estate at Euchaita, as he had asked his secretary Ouaros before the beheading, and that the Church keeps the translation of his relics on that day. Its couplets have him a general in rank and, once beheaded, a general of God, and count the day: on the eighth they cut through Theodore's strong neck.

The Serbian church keeps him on the Julian 8 February, and Bishop Nikolai's Ohrid Prologue opens on the worth of a martyrdom: there are martyrdoms more precious than precious, and the worth of one depends on the greatness of what a Christian gives up to take suffering in its place, and on the greatness of the suffering he bears for Christ's sake. Theodore, a Roman commander in the army of the emperor Licinius and governor of the city of Heraclea, despised his youth, his beauty, his rank and the emperor's favour, and took frightful torments instead of them all. He was scourged first, six hundred strokes across the back and five hundred across the belly; then raised on a cross and shot through and through with arrows; and at last beheaded with the sword.

Why all that, the Prologue asks, and answers itself: because he loved Christ the Lord above everything in the world; because he despised the stupid idolatrous superstition of the emperor Licinius; because he broke the idols of silver and gold in pieces and shared the pieces out among the poor; because he turned many to the faith of Christ; and because he called on Licinius himself to renounce the idols and believe in the one living God. All through the torments he said without ceasing, "Glory to thee, my God, glory to thee." He suffered on 8 February 319 at three in the afternoon — the hour is in no other source here, and the year is the Russian calendar's against the Greek page's 320, so his recorded death is now the interval the two leave. He is held, it adds, the protector of soldiers who call on him for help.

One thing in it cannot be reconciled with the Greek page and is left unreconciled. The Prologue has his wonderworking relics carried from Euchaita to Constantinople and buried in the church of the Blachernae, and refers the reader on to 8 June for it; saint.gr keeps 8 June as a translation too, but moves the body from Herakleia to his ancestral estate at Euchaita. The two sources send the relics in opposite directions on the same day, and neither is preferred here.

The same book keeps him a second time, on the Julian 8 June, and says in its first sentence what that day is: his life is written at the 8 February, and the 8 June is the translation of his relics from Herakleia to Euchaita. So it is recorded as a note on this church’s feast and he does not render on it, one row to a church being the schema. **The book gives the warrant for the place in the saint’s own mouth**: he had bequeathed it at his passion to his servant — lay my body at Euchaita, on the estate of my forefathers — which is the ancestral estate the Greek page names, said by the man himself. That is also the opposite direction to the one this book’s own 8 February entry gives, where the relics go from Euchaita to Constantinople and the church of the Blachernae; the disagreement is inside the one book and neither reading is preferred here.

The second entry adds one wonder, with a named witness. Anastasius of Sinai recorded it, the book says: at a place called Karsata near Damascus there was a church in the name of Saint Theodore Stratelates, and when the Saracens had subdued Damascus a party of them settled in that church with their wives and children. On the wall was a fresco of the saint’s face. One of them loosed an arrow and struck the face of the image, and blood ran at once out of the painting; and soon after that the whole party died there in the church. Anastasius says that he was himself in that church, and saw the saint’s face on the wall and the traces of the dried blood.
