# Joannicius of Devič

Joannicius was a Serb of the region of Zeta, which is the Montenegro of today, saint.gr says, and he lived in the thirteenth century. From a young age he had a great love for Christ, and while still young he decided to leave the life of the world and his father's house and begin the monastic life, for which he had a great zeal.

He lived first as a monk on a mountain called Crna Reka, the Black River, where another well-known ascetic had been a monk before him, the venerable Petar Koriški. But when he became known among people for the greatness of his asceticism he went away to Drenica and hid himself in the thick forest, where he passed his whole life in asceticism and prayer.

According to tradition, the page goes on, the despot of Serbia Đurađ Branković brought his sick daughter to the saint, and Joannicius healed her; and in thanks the despot built a monastery in that region, which is known today as the monastery of Devič. In that monastery, which lies in the region of Kosovo, the holy relics of the venerable father are kept.

The two halves of that account do not agree about when he lived: Branković ruled in the fifteenth century, and the page's own opening sentence puts Joannicius in the thirteenth. It does not notice the difficulty or resolve it, and no year is recorded in this folder on either account. It prints no hymn for him.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, where the Greek keeps him on 2 December, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović tells the same life in its own words. He was a Serb of Zeta. As a youth, seized with the love of Christ, he left his house and his own people and withdrew into the region of the Ibar, into the gorge of the Crna Rijeka, into a narrow cave in which, by tradition, Petar Koriški had struggled before him. But when his fame began to be spoken of among men he fled to Drenica and hid himself in the thick forest of Devič, and there he passed years in solitude, in silence and in prayer.

By tradition, this book goes on, the Serbian prince Đurađ Branković brought him his daughter, who had lost her mind, and the saint healed her; and out of gratitude Đurađ raised a monastery on that place, known by the name Devič to this day. There the holy and wonderworking relics of Joannicius rest. **The Prologue gives no year of any kind**, so it offers nothing to settle the century the Greek page contradicts itself about, and nothing was recorded here on its account.

The entry closes with a woman it does not head. In that monastery, almost into our own days, there struggled a famous and God-pleasing ascetic, the nun Ефимија, better known in the Kosovo country under the name Блажена Стојна; she fell asleep in the Lord in 1895. The heading names Joannicius alone, so nothing in this corpus was written for her, and whether she belongs in it is a question for its author.

The Russian calendar keeps him on this same day, marking its line «(Серб.)» for a Serbian commemoration, and tells the life in a few sentences that add nothing the two other books had not given — the boy leaving the house of his childhood in Zeta for solitude, the cell in the wooded country of the Black River where Petar Koriški had passed long years in prayer and in war with the demons, the crowd of disciples drawn by his struggles, the flight from men's praise into the forest of Devič, the gift of unceasing prayer with tears and of casting out demons, the despot's sick daughter healed and the monastery built in thanks, the death in old age, and the relics which to this day are a spring of wonders and of healings.

What it does is make the difficulty about his century a third time. Its line heads him «(XIII)», the thirteenth, while its own life has the despot bring him his daughter; and that is the same pair of statements the Greek page set side by side without noticing, and which the Prologue left open by giving no year of any kind. This source does not notice it either, and nothing was recorded here on its account. It is also the first of the three to print hymns for him, a troparion and a kontakion, where the Greek page says in so many words that it has none.
