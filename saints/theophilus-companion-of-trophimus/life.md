# Theophilus, companion of Trophimus

[Trophimus](/saints/trophimus-companion-of-theophilus) and Theophilus, and with them other faithful and true servants of Christ, thirteen in number, suffered in the reign of Diocletian. They would not submit to the will of the pagan tormentor, nor sacrifice to the soulless idols, nor deny Christ, and for that they were hung up to be tortured and their bodies torn with sharpened irons.

Then the pagans stoned the patient sufferers, and having broken the bones of their legs threw them into a fire. But by the all-powerful strength of God they were kept alive and whole in the middle of the great flames, and came out of the fire unharmed. At that the tormentor, burning the more with anger, as with the fire of hell, commanded that they all be killed with the sword; and their honourable heads were cut off for the sake of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Head of the whole Church.

That is the whole of what the Romanian calendar prints of them. It names these two and counts the others without naming any of them, so the thirteen are left here as the page leaves them, unnamed; and it gives no country, no city and no year.

The Greek calendar keeps them a little earlier in the month, on 21 July, and in the same breath: «Άγιοι Θεόφιλος, Τρόφιμος και άλλοι Δεκατρείς Μάρτυρες». saint.gr tells their passion as doxologia does and adds the years. These saints lived in the days of the emperor Diocletian, it says, whose reign it dates 284 to 304, and they were distinguished for their Christian courage in the persecution of the Christians. They stayed faithful to the confession of our Lord Jesus Christ and were put through a series of torments: they were stoned, and their flesh was torn with iron instruments, and then their wounds were burned with lighted tapers. But because their life still held out, and their mouths still repeated the Christian confession, they were beheaded. The Greek page counts the thirteen companions and names none of them, as the Romanian page does; and its verse for Trophimus says that he had thirteen men as messmates and fellow-contenders, and was cut down together with them.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two of them on the Julian 23 July, which is the Romanian day and not the Greek one, and its heading names the two of them and thirteen more with them without naming any of the thirteen. They suffered in Lycia in the time of the emperor Diocletian. Since they would in no way deny Christ nor offer sacrifice to the idols, they were put to various torments: they were beaten with stones, scraped with sharp iron, their knees were broken, and at the last, so tormented and more dead than alive, they were thrown into the fire.

The power of God kept them unharmed in the fire. Then they were brought out and beheaded with the sword. But the Lord glorified them both on earth and in his own heavenly kingdom. They suffered honourably in Lycia, the entry ends, in the year 308.
