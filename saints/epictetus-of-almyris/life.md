# Epictetus, teacher of Astion

In the days of Diocletian there lived in the parts of the East a man named Epictetus, a monk in the order of priest, who had served Christ from his youth and worked many wonders: for the purity of his life he had received from God the gift of healing every disease, and he gave light to the blind, cleansed lepers, raised the paralysed and drove devils out of men. A count brought him a daughter of fifteen who had lain three years without the use of her limbs, and Epictetus prayed long, anointed her with blessed oil, and she rose up well; and he told the man that if he wished no sickness in his house, he and all his household should communicate every Sunday with a clean heart.

A young man of eighteen named [Astion](/saints/astion-of-almyris), the only son of the chief man of the neighbouring city, came upon his cell one day while out walking with companions, and went in to see who lived so far from men. Epictetus spoke to him of the true gold tried in the fire, which is Christ, and of the true Father and the true Mother, which is the Church, and the young man was pricked in the heart; he came back alone the next morning and asked to be made a Christian secretly and taken away to a distant country, so that his parents' tears should not stand in the way of his salvation.

The elder set him to fast and to pray, taught him the faith, baptised him, and one night the two of them left together, taking a little silver for the road. They found a ship bound for the parts of the Scythians and after many days reached a city called Almyris on the borders of Scythia, by the river Danube; not far from it they found a quiet place and built themselves a small cell. There their holiness could not be hidden: at one healing — a boy of about fifteen, deaf, dumb and withered, who spoke at once when Epictetus had spat three times in his mouth — more than a thousand men and women of Almyris believed in Christ that day.

When Latronian, the governor of that country, came to Almyris, the idol-priests told him that the two were sorcerers who turned men from sacrificing, and he had them seized and thrown into prison. Epictetus told his disciple that if they were asked their name, family and country they should answer nothing but, We are Christians: that is our name, our family and our country. And so they answered at the tribunal, and were beaten, hung up, torn with iron claws and burnt with torches, saying over and over, We are Christians; the will of our God be done in us.

One of the torturers, Vigilantius, took those words for a spell and turned them over day and night until he cried them out himself in public, and was baptised with his whole house. The governor rubbed the old wounds with vinegar and salt, boiled a great cauldron of pitch and threw the two in, and they sat in it unharmed as in a bath; then he shut them up thirty days without bread or water. At the last hearing he called them devils in the flesh, because they would give him no name, and condemned them to the sword.

At the place of execution Epictetus asked that Astion be struck first. The young man protested that the priest, his father and teacher, should be crowned before him; but the elder said he had kept him pure seventeen years and would not lose that labour by leaving him alone at the end, and that he would be as Abraham offering Isaac. Astion bowed his head and died, and Epictetus fell across his disciple's body and begged the executioners to strike him so, and was beheaded. Pagans and Christians alike saw the two bodies shine like rays of the sun, and a sweet smell filled the place.

That night Vigilantius and other Christians took the bodies secretly, wrapped them in clean linen with spices and buried them; and that same night a devil fell upon Latronian, who ran mad with a sword among his own people and was strangled in two days. Astion's parents, Alexander and Marcellina, arrived by ship too late, were taken in by Vigilantius and brought to the faith, and were baptised by Evangelus, the Christian bishop of that land of the Scythians; afterwards they sold their goods, gave them to the poor and lived to their end in God.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two of them on the Julian 7 July, where the Romanian calendar keeps them on the 8th — two calendars and two days, and both are recorded. Its first sentence is the pair’s own story as this corpus already had it: the presbyter Epictetus won Astion, his parents’ only son, to the faith of Christ, baptised him and made him a monk. Then, it says, they moved from somewhere in the eastern parts into the Scythian country, and settled in the Scythian town of Almyris — now Ramzina — at the outfall of the Danube into the Black Sea. They were tortured and beheaded for the faith of Christ about the year 290, a year inside the persecution the Romanian page names without dating their death in it, so the folder’s recorded interval is left standing rather than narrowed to it.

And both of them, this book ends, appeared after their death in a great light to Astion’s parents, who were converted to Christ and baptised by the bishop Evangelus; and he too was beheaded for Christ afterwards, and of him it was sung: Evangelus, a second angel. The bishop’s own folder stands on this same day in two other calendars, and this reading writes him no Serbian row, because the heading of the entry names the two monks and not him.
