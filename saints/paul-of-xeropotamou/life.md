# Paul of Xeropotamou

Paul came of Constantinople. His father was the emperor Michael Kouropalates, also called Rangabe, who could not bear to see the disorders of his days, wrought by men without order, and being a lover of peace and a God-fearing man gave up the empire and became a monk in a monastery he had built himself, called Myrelaion, where he lived in a manner pleasing to God and rested in the Lord. His mother was Procopia, wonderful in good works, the daughter of the emperor Nicephorus who was also called Genikos, and the sister of the emperor Staurakios.

When she was great with the saint, on the night she was to bear him, she saw in a vision that she had given birth on top of a stack of wheat to a male lamb. When it had come down from the stack two lions came to tear it, and the lamb fought with them; and the empress, seeing this, ran in haste to help it, but coming near she saw that it was not a lamb but a male child, who held a cross in his hands and with its power killed the lions. Waking from sleep, his mother bore the blessed Procopius, for that was the name he was given at holy baptism.

That vision, the life says, has this meaning: the lamb was the guilelessness and gentleness of the child, and its being male was his manliness and courage; that he killed both lions with the cross showed that he would be a monk and take upon himself the Cross of Christ — the putting to death of the passions and the many afflictions that came upon him — and with the cross would overcome and kill the two fearful lions, that is, the monk's two enemies, the devil with all his powers and the world with all its glories and sweetnesses; and the stack of wheat signified what his word would do. What the page tells of his life after that is very long, and only its beginning and its end are given here.

At the end, his disciples set out by ship at evening to go to the forest, and in the morning — wonders of Christ the King — they found themselves at Constantinople. When the emperor, the council, the patriarch and all the clergy learned of it, they put on the sacred vestments, lit candles with censers, took up the holy relics of the saint with praises and the singing of psalms, and laid them in the great church, kissing them with great reverence and thanking the Lord who had enriched them with such a treasure.

His disciples, having kissed the relics and called on the prayers of the saint for help, bought warm loaves, went aboard their ship and set off back, talking of him; and in a little while they were in the harbour of their own monastery. Going up into the holy monastery they told the fathers what had happened, and showed them the loaves, which were warm still; and hearing it the fathers were amazed at the great boldness that the ever-remembered Paul had before God.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 28 July, and gives the Athonite account the Romanian line did not. saint.gr says the venerable Paul lived in the tenth century and was the son of devout Byzantine nobles, and that his own life earned him the title of consul of the philosophers; he embraced virtue early. He left Constantinople as a beggar and came to the Holy Mountain unrecognised, and near what is now the monastery of Xeropotamou, of which he was the founder, he set up his ascetic hut, with the earth for a mattress and a stone for a pillow. His virtue made him admired and people came from everywhere to know him, and on a short return to his own country he became a source of great benefit there. The longing for a greater silence took him away from Xeropotamou to the foot of Athos, where his many disciples obliged him to build a new monastery in honour of Saint George, known today as Saint Paul's. He knew his end a good while before it and made ready, teaching his disciples: have love, my children and brothers, and prayer, and humility, and obedience, for a monk who has not these virtues ought not to be called a monk but a man of the world. Writings of his survive at Xeropotamou, among them six canons to the Forty Martyrs, an iambic canon to the honourable Cross and a discourse on the Entry of the Theotokos, and a later service was composed for him by the monk Gerasimos Mikragiannanitis.

The same page then sets a note against part of its own story, and it is given here as the page gives it. According to S. Eustratiades, it says, the life of the venerable Paul which stands in the Neon Eklogion is completely fictional. In that life he is made the son of the emperor Michael I Rangabe, who reigned from 811 to 813, and of the daughter of the emperor Nicephorus the General; he is castrated in childhood, reaches great heights of philosophy, renounces the world and goes to the Holy Mountain, where he builds the monastery of Xeropotamou and becomes its abbot. Athonite tradition, the page adds, makes him instead a contemporary of Saint Athanasius, the founder of the Lavra. The Greek calendar prints both the account and the objection and settles neither, and no year is recorded here for that reason.

The Охридски пролог for the Julian 28 July tells the same life and adds his name in the world. He was first called Прокопије; and with his brilliant education, his rare wisdom and his meekness together, he was in his young years the wonder of the whole of Constantinople. In a charter the emperor Romanos the Elder called him „највећим од свих философа“, the greatest of all philosophers. But fearing that his soul would be puffed up and ruined by human glory, this brilliant young man dressed himself one day in a beggar's rags and came to the Holy Mountain, where he took the monastic habit from a famous saint Козма whom the book does not further identify.

After long labours in solitude he restored the monastery of Xeropotamou, and a little after that built the new monastery of Saint Paul, where he ended his days in old age. When that monastery was consecrated the emperor Romanos sent as a gift a great part of the Honourable Cross, which is kept there to this day. It is said of this saint, the book adds, that he preached the Gospel in Macedonia and in Serbia. He bore many torments from the wicked emperor Leo the Armenian the iconoclast, and reposed in 820 — the first year of any kind this folder has carried.

Before his death Saint Paul said to the brethren: „Ево стиже час, који је душа моја увек желела и од кога се тело моје увек страшило“ — behold, the hour is come that my soul has always longed for, and that my body has always dreaded.
