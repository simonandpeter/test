# Theotecnus of Antioch

The Russian calendar's 10 October prints «Мч. Феотекна (ок. 284-305)», and the life behind it makes him a famous military commander at Antioch under the emperor Maximian. The emperor came to Antioch once and required all its people to sacrifice to the pagan gods, and Theotecnus, a Christian, refused the order. Mocking the faith of Christ, the emperor had him dressed in women's clothes and set to spin with the slave women.

After three weeks the emperor called him in, thinking the humiliation had broken his spirit, and heard the confession of the name of Christ again. You will destroy your life if you do not submit, Maximian threatened; Theotecnus said nothing. Hardened, the emperor ordered the martyr's feet burned and his tendons cut, and then had him thrown into a cauldron of boiling pitch. As soon as he was in it the flame beneath went out and the heat in the cauldron cooled in an instant. Terror took hold of the emperor; unwilling to try the martyr any further, he sent him to prison and charged his centurion with finishing him.

In the prison with him was a Christian confessor named Alexandra, and he helped her to escape. When the centurion learned of it he put Theotecnus to savage tortures and at last ordered him thrown into the sea with a stone round his neck. Some while afterwards the martyr's honoured relics were found near the town of Rusov on the Cilician coast and given Christian burial.

Two other saints of this name are in this corpus and neither is he. The Greek calendar keeps a Theotecnus on 3 October who was stoned, and a Theoteknos on 10 December who was killed by the sword; this one is the commander of Antioch, kept on the Russian 10 October, who was drowned. Three entries, three days and three deaths, and the Antioch in this folder's English name is the epithet the Russian index gives him, «Антиохийский», which is what keeps the three apart.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 10 October, which makes this folder’s second calendar, and gives him four sentences: a Roman officer at Antioch in the time of Maximian, who, when the emperor offered him the sacrifice to the idols, answered that he believed in Christ God and would offer him himself as a sacrifice, a living sacrifice; and who, after great torments, was sunk in the sea with a stone about his neck and so suffered honourably for Christ and was crowned with the martyr’s crown. The words are his own in this book and in no other read for this folder.

The identification is made on that scene and not on the name. The officer’s rank, the city, the reign and the drowning with a stone are four things this entry and the Russian life share, and the drowning is what tells this Theotecnus from the one who was stoned and the one who was killed with the sword. The heading’s own spelling of the name is kept inside the quoted line of the Serbian attestation and is not stored as a name form, because mending or adopting a source’s spelling of a person’s name is not a reader’s to do. Of the long Russian account — the women’s clothes and the spinning, the burnt feet, the cauldron of pitch that went cold, the prison, the confessor he helped to escape and the finding of the relics on the Cilician coast — this book has none.
