# Mocius of Amphipolis

In the reign of Diocletian, when Laodicius and Maximus were proconsuls in Europe and the persecution of the Christians stood everywhere, there was a priest of the holy Church of God named Mocius in the city of Amphipolis in Macedonia. While the unbelievers were keeping the foul feast of Dionysus, he stood in the middle of the people and cried out to them: how long would they wander in the darkness of the madness for idols, and not see their own ruin, and not turn and know the Master God, made known through His only-begotten Son?

He said this not once but daily. The proconsul Laodicius, come from Apollonia to sacrifice to Dionysus, was told that this Christian teacher had turned many away from the feast, and had him seized. Brought to judgement, Mocius answered that from the Holy Scripture he had learned that the idols of the pagans are demons and that hope set on them is empty; and when he was told to sacrifice and save the life he was throwing away, he said: «O, antipate, moartea cea pentru Hristos îmi este mie mare câștig!» — O proconsul, death for Christ is great gain to me.

He was hung up naked and torn with iron claws from head to foot, and prayed aloud while they tore him. The torturers wearied, and the proconsul drove them off as idle and useless; the saint was loosed and stood before him stronger in body and bolder than before. A great furnace was made ready with pitch and tow and brushwood, its flame going up some sixty cubits and darkening the air with smoke, and still he would not sacrifice, answering that a thing made by men's hands, unmoving and without voice, is no god but an idol. The life then tells how the idol of Dionysus was broken and Laodicius burned; the saint said that not he but Almighty God had done it.

Sent on to the proconsul Maximus, he was bound between two torture-wheels to be broken, and said as they turned: how sweet is the love of God. He prayed, and the wheels shattered, and he was loosed whole; the tormentor, shamed, threw him into prison. After three days he was brought out to the spectacle and two lions roaring with hunger were let loose on him; they came near, bowed before him, and took his feet and licked them as though kissing them, and the people cried out that a righteous man whom God loves and the beasts honour should be set free.

Maximus sent him to the governor Philippus at Perinthus in Thrace, the city now called Heraclea, who kept him eight days in prison and then had him taken to Byzantium to be beheaded there. At the place of execution he lifted his eyes to heaven, blessed God who founded the ages and brings the struggles of the saints to their end, and asked that his soul be received in peace; and a voice came from heaven bidding him rejoice, for he had plainly overcome the tormentor's power, and come and dwell with the fathers in the heavenly kingdom.

He was beheaded on the eleventh day of the month of May. The emperor Constantine afterwards kept his feast, having built a church in his name and laid his honoured relics in it; and on the day of his memory he made the renewal of the city that he called by his own name, Constantinople.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and names his parents, his see and his judges. Euphratius and Eustathia were a light in the idolatrous darkness of Rome under Diocletian, saint.gr says, and these pious parents passed the light of the Gospel on to their son Mocius; so that from a very young age he had a great longing to serve the Church, and God counted him worthy to fulfil it. Having studied the sacred letters with particular diligence and been properly trained in the knowledge and the imparting of the truths of religion, he was made a cleric at a fitting age; and later his excellent services to the Church raised him to the rank of Bishop of Amphipolis in Thrace. From that place he let fly burning reproofs against the lie of idolatry, and laboured feverishly to strengthen the faith and the patience of the faithful of his diocese.

But his vigorous work as a minister roused the anger of the idolatrous prefect Laodicius, who tortured him in various ways; and afterwards another prefect, Maximus, tore his flesh and threw him as food to the wild beasts — and saw that Mocius always came out alive. Then, in the year 288, he sent him bound to Byzantium, where they beheaded him, and so he took the crown of martyrdom. Later Constantine the Great raised a magnificent church in honour of Saint Mocius and laid his holy relics in it; at Mid-Pentecost the emperor came to that church in procession, and the relic of Samson the Hospitable was kept there as well. Fragments of his own relic, the page says, are at the monasteries of Saint Dionysius and of Panteleimon on the Holy Mountain; and the page for the dedication of Constantinople, which the Greek calendar keeps on this same day, says that the day of the dedication was chosen on purpose because it fell on the day of his martyrdom, since he was the patron of Byzantium.

The Ohrid Prologue, read for the Julian 11 May, gives him five sentences. He was a Roman by birth, it says, and a priest at Amphipolis, a Macedonian city — where the Greek page puts Amphipolis in Thrace, and neither reading is chosen here. He suffered in the time of Diocletian. By his prayer he shattered the image of the god Dionysus, which embittered some of the heathen against him and brought others of them to the faith; and he was beheaded for Christ in the year 295.

The Greek page dates that beheading to 288 and puts it at Byzantium, where it has him sent bound after the prefects Laodicius and Maximus had tortured him; so his death now stands as the two years together and neither is chosen, and this book, which names no place for it, has none of the rest of that account in it. The breaking of the idol is its own, and so is the Roman birth.

The Russian church calendar keeps him on 11 мая ст. ст. and gives him a life. He was a presbyter in Macedonia, in the city of Amphipolis, and during Diocletian’s persecution — the calendar dates that reign 284 to 305 — he went among the pagans gathered to keep the feast of Dionysus, or Bacchus, and urged them to leave off the lawless and foul customs that went with it, to repent, to turn to the Lord Jesus Christ and to be cleansed in holy baptism. He was brought before the governor Лаодикий, confessed the true faith to him, and answered his threats: «Смерть за Христа – великое приобретение для меня» — death for Christ is great gain to me.

He bore the tortures that followed with wonderful patience and did not stop rebuking the idolaters’ impiety. Brought into the temple of Dionysus, he broke the idol with the Name of Jesus Christ, and was thrown into a heated furnace and left unharmed, while the flame that came out of it burned the governor. A newly appointed official put him to cruel torments and he bore those too; given over to wild beasts, he was untouched, and the lions lay down at his feet. The people, brought to their senses by such wonders, began to demand that the martyr be set free.

The governor had him sent away to the city of Perinthus and from there to Byzantium, and there he was executed. Before his death he thanked the Lord in prayer for giving him strength to go through the martyr’s course to the end; «Господи, приими в мире дух мой» were his last words, and then his head was struck off. The calendar puts his death about the year 295, and says that the emperor Константин afterwards built a church in the hieromartyr’s honour and carried his holy relics into it.
