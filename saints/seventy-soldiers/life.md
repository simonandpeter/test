# The Seventy Soldiers

saint.gr's whole account of them is one sentence: «Οι Άγιοι Εβδομήκοντα Στρατιώτες πίστεψαν στον Χριστό δια του Άγιου Σάββα και μαρτύρησαν δια ξίφους» — the holy seventy soldiers believed in Christ through the holy Sabbas, and were martyred by the sword. It names not one of them, and this folder therefore stands for the company and not for seventy men.

The occasion is on [Sabbas Stratelates](/saints/sabbas-stratelates)'s own page for the same day. Sabbas was a Goth by descent serving at Rome, who carried food to imprisoned Christians and was denounced for it; his sides were torn and his wounds burned with lighted torches, and when nothing would move him his death was decreed. Because his rank was high, the news that he was to be put to death brought crowds of spectators to the place of execution; and there a cauldron of pitch was boiled and the officer in charge ordered the martyr thrown in.

By a wonder he stayed unharmed. The sight stirred great amazement, and seventy of the idolaters standing by believed in Christ and were beheaded on the spot — these seventy. Sabbas himself, a great stone tied to his neck, was thrown into the river Tiber. His page puts that in the year 272; this page gives the seventy no year, no city and no judge, and no hymn.

The couplet above the entry is the only other thing the page has of them, and it counts them: numbering the heads cut off by the sword, you would find seven times ten men fallen.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović gives the fullest account of him read for this folder, and names the emperor. In the time of the emperor Aurelian this glorious Sabbas lived at Rome and held the rank of a commander; by descent he was of the Gothic people. He often visited the Christians in the prisons and helped them out of his own property, and for his great purity and fasting authority over unclean spirits was given him by God. When he was denounced as a Christian he stood boldly before the emperor, **threw his soldier's belt down in front of him** and confessed Christ the Lord openly.

He was racked with various torments — scourged, scraped with iron, scorched with candles — and did not succumb to them but appeared alive and whole; and his comrades the soldiers, seeing plainly that God was helping Sabbas, received the faith of Christ. **Seventy of them in number** were beheaded at once by the emperor's order. To Sabbas in the prison Christ the Lord Himself appeared in a great light and strengthened His martyr. Then Sabbas was condemned to death by drowning, and thrown into a deep river, where he gave up his soul to God in 272.

What the entry adds to this folder is why the seventy believed and how quickly they died: they were Sabbas's own comrades in the ranks, they saw plainly that God was helping him under the torments, and they were beheaded **at once**, by the emperor's order, while Sabbas himself was still alive in the prison. **It gives them no names and no year.** The 272 it prints is the commander's, and his death came after theirs, so no date was added here and this folder stands undated as it was.

The Russian calendar gives them the moment of their confession and no names. They were seventy soldiers who stood by while their commander was beaten, scorched with candles and thrown into a cauldron of pitch and came out of it unhurt; and «Видя его мучения, уверовали во Христа 70 воинов, которых тут же усекли мечом» — seeing his torments, the seventy believed in Christ, and they were cut down with the sword there and then, without trial and without a word recorded of any of them. The commander was thrown back into prison, saw Christ at midnight, bore new torments in the morning and was drowned in the river in 272, which is the year the calendar heads the whole entry with and therefore theirs.

The calendar gives no name for a single one of the seventy, and no link to any name page: on this day they exist only inside his line. So no name form was written into this folder from the Russian reading, and none could be.
