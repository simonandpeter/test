# The Forty-two Martyrs of Amorion

Theophilus, emperor of the Greeks, son of the emperor Michael who was called Balbus or Traulos and whose family came from Amorion, had frequent wars with the Ishmaelites, sometimes beating them and sometimes beaten by them. Going out once with a great army against the Hagarenes, he surrounded and battered the city called Sozopetra, the native place of Amirmumn, the commander of the Saracens; and although Amirmumn, who was also called Abisak, sent begging him to leave that city alone, Theophilus took it, levelled it utterly, and went home with much plunder.

Amirmumn, in grief and rage for the ruin of his own country, gathered soldiers from everywhere with much gold — from Babylon, from Palestine, from Coele-Syria and from far Africa — and assembled at Tarsus, meaning to go into Phrygia against the beautiful city of Amorion, the native place of Theophilus's father, and so avenge Sozopetra. Theophilus gathered his own army from the East and the West and from Persia, and came to Dorylaeum in Phrygia, three days' journey from Amorion. His counsellors advised him not to give battle, and to move the people of Amorion into stronger cities, but he said it was a shameful thing to draw back from war and leave so fair a city empty.

There was a great battle, and afterwards Amirmumn went straight to Amorion and surrounded it. He would not be moved by any entreaty, being still angry for Sozopetra; and when he had begun to despair of taking the city, a paper was shot out over the wall by an arrow, and what was written in it gave him the city. The commander Vaditis, who sold the place to the enemy, denied Christ and became a pagan. So the fair city of Amorion perished in one day by fire and the sword, and the commanders whom the emperor had sent to defend it were taken alive.

The prisoners were kept bound in a close and wretched prison. The Hagarenes, urged on by their commander, came again and again to coax them over to their faith: some entered as almsgivers and sat beside them; others, whom the life calls gymnosophists, came to dispute, arguing that God gives victory to those whose faith is right, and that the Greeks had been beaten. The saints answered them out of the prophets, until the disputants were ashamed of being beaten in words; and Bassoes answered them smiling.

Seven years had gone by in those chains when the commander sent to them again. One of the prisoners, a man who had been secretary to the lord Constantine the patrician and was himself called Constantine, was called to the little prison window and told to advise his master to escape death by a pretended agreement to the Saracen faith. Constantine the patrician asked who had called him to the window and why; and then, turning to his companions, said: brothers, let us pass this whole night in prayer.

Early the next morning an officer came to the prison with armed men, and pressed them once more with the commander's supposed love for them; they answered him as they had answered the rest. Then the traitor who had sold the city was brought out, and those who put him to death said among themselves that a man unfaithful to his own would never be faithful to strangers; so they cut off his head. On the following day, by the commander's order, the bodies of the forty-two martyrs were thrown into the river Euphrates, and the traitor's body with them.

After a little while the bodies of the martyrs were found whole by the bank, on the other side of the river, the head of each one joined again to its own body and lying decently with it; but the traitor's body was found far from the saints, and his head apart from his body. The faithful took up the bodies of the saints and buried them with honour; the body and head of the lawless betrayer were torn and eaten by crocodiles.

The Greek calendar keeps them on the same day and gives the war its shape. saint.gr says the forty-two lived in the years of the reign of Theophilus the iconoclast and were generals and commanders, rich and noble men, and it names ten of them with the office each held: Theodore was general and protospatharios, Theophilus general and patrician, Callistus a tourmarches, Constantine a droungarios, Bassoes a courier, Melissenos and Aetios generals, one Krateros a eunuch and another Krateros a general, and Cyril also a general. At that time, the page says, the Amir came out of Syria with a countless army against the eastern parts of the Roman dominion, and soldiers were sent by the emperor to protect the city of Amorion, the capital of Phrygia; and when they saw the endless multitude of the Saracens they went into the inner part of the castle and fought there with endurance.

There they were taken, in the year 838, by the caliph Mutasim, and led away to Samarra in Mesopotamia and shut in prison. The caliph promised to restore them to their offices if they would change their faith and become Muslims, but the holy martyrs refused with a noble courage and confessed their faith in Christ; and having suffered many hardships and inhuman tortures they were beheaded, in the year 842, and so sealed with their blood the confession they had made for Christ. The page names ten of the forty-two and no more, so they stand here as one company still, as the Romanian calendar left them.

The Serbian church keeps them on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović spends most of the entry on what they said in prison. They were all commanders of the Byzantine emperor Theophilos, it says; and when Theophilos lost his battle against the Saracens near the city of Amorion, the Saracens took the city and carried off many Christians, these commanders among them. The rest of the Christians they either killed or sold into slavery, and the commanders they threw into a prison where they lay seven years. Again and again the Muslim leaders came to counsel them to accept the faith of Muhammad, and the commanders would not so much as hear it.

When the Saracens told them that Muhammad was the true prophet and not Christ, the commanders put a question to them. If two men were to dispute over one field, one saying the field is mine and the other saying no, it is mine, and one of them had many witnesses that the field was his while the other had none but himself — whose would you say the field was? The Saracens answered: truly, the one with many witnesses. You have judged rightly, said the commanders: so it is with Christ and with Muhammad. Christ has many witnesses, the prophets of old whom you also acknowledge, and all of them from the first to the last bear witness of Him; Muhammad bears witness of himself that he is a prophet, and has not one witness besides.

The Saracens were put to shame, the entry goes on, and then tried to defend their faith another way: that ours is the better faith is plain from this, that God gave us the victory over you, and gave us the best lands in the world and an empire far greater than the Christian one. To which the commanders answered that if it were so, then the idolatry of Egypt and of Babylon and of the Greeks and of the Romans, and the fire-worship of Persia, would all of them have been true faiths, because each of those nations in its time conquered the rest and ruled over them. Plainly, they said, your victory and your power and your wealth do not prove your faith true; and we know that God sometimes gives the victory to Christians and sometimes allows them torment and suffering, to correct them and bring them to repentance and to cleansing from sins.

After the seven years they were beheaded, in the year 845. Their bodies were thrown into the river Euphrates, but they floated across to the other bank, where Christians gathered them and buried them honourably.

The Russian church keeps them on this same day, and its line is the only one of the four that names seven of the forty-two in the day's own words: «Мч. 42-х во Амморее Константина, Аетия, Феофила, Феодора, Мелиссена, Каллиста, Васоя и прочих с ними (ок. 845).» The Сретенский monastery's life behind it is short and sets the siege inside a war between the Greek emperor Феофил, whose reign it gives as 829 to 842, and the Saracens: Амморея in Galatia fell through the treachery of a commander it calls Вадитзис, and forty-two of its defenders, soldiers, were taken prisoner and sent into Syria. Seven years of wearying confinement followed, in which they were urged in vain to leave the Christian faith for Islam, first by flattering offers and then by fearful threats; after many tortures, their spirit unbroken, they were condemned to death.

The Russian page prints one exchange from the last hour that none of the other three carries. Феодор was told «Нам известно, что ты, оставив священный сан, стал воином и проливал кровь. Ты не можешь надеяться на Христа, — признай Магомета» — we know that you left the priestly rank, became a soldier and shed blood; you cannot hope in Christ, acknowledge Mahomet — and he answered «Я не отрекся от Христа, а за то, что оставил священный сан, должен пролить свою кровь», that he had not denied Christ, and that for having left the priestly rank he must shed his own blood. They went calmly and fearlessly to the executioners, were beheaded, and their bodies were thrown into the Euphrates. The same page says their service praises five of them by epithet — Феодор «всеблаженный», Каллист «непобедимый», Константин «доблий», Феофил «чудный», Васой «крепчайший» — and that the traitor did not escape a shameful end either: his own new masters knew that a man who betrays once is not to be trusted, and put him to death.

So the Russian calendar agrees with the Serbian about the year, about 845, and against the Greek, which has them beheaded in 842; it agrees with the Greek and the Romanian in giving the city to a traitor, and its spelling of his name, Вадитзис, is the fullest of the three. Seven of the forty-two are named on its line and in its life, the same seven the name index behind the line gives a page each to, and no more; the seven are not stored as name forms of this folder, because they are not names of the company, and the Russian form kept here is the company's own, «42 мученика в Амморее».
