# Irene of Magedon

She was born Penelope, the daughter of a king named Licinius who ruled the city of Magedon and of his queen Licinia. At six she was already more beautiful than any of the maidens around her, and her father built her a tower outside the city, richly furnished, and shut her up in it with thirteen companions and his golden gods, an old woman named Caria to teach her manners and an old and honourable man named Apelian to teach her letters every day. She lived there six years and three months, and when she was twelve her father began to think of marrying her to a king's son.

As she sat in her room a dove came in at the east window with an olive branch in its beak and left it on the table; an hour later an eagle brought a crown woven of flowers and laid it there too; then a raven came in at another window and dropped a small snake. Apelian, who was secretly a Christian, read the signs for her: the dove was her gentleness and her virgin wisdom, the olive branch the grace of God she would receive in baptism, the eagle a victorious king — and of the raven's snake he told her what was to come. When her father came to speak of marriage she put him off, and asked his golden idols whether she should marry; they were dumb, and she spat at them and turned to the east and asked the God the Galileans preach.

That night an angel came to her in sleep: from now on she would not be called Penelope but Irene, a refuge and a peaceful harbour to many, and through her many souls would be saved; what old Apelian had said of the birds was true, and this same night the man of God, Timothy, would come to her. The priest came, led by angels, taught her and her governess and her servants, baptised them, made Irene the bride of Christ, left them the apostolic letters and went away. She threw her father's golden gods down from the tower one by one — «De sunteți zei, mântuiți-vă singuri!», if you are gods, save yourselves — and they broke like dust on the ground.

When her parents came again for the wedding she told them plainly that she was the servant of Christ, and preached to them the making of the world and the cross and the resurrection, and they listened, and took her back into the city in procession. But the devil turned her father's heart, and he ordered her bound and thrown under the hooves of wild horses. One horse tore loose, took the king's right arm out of its shoulder in its teeth, killed him, and then stood quiet; and by God's command it spoke with a human voice and called her blessed, the dove of Christ who had trodden on the serpent. Irene loosed herself, raised her father from the dead by prayer and healed his arm, and he and the queen and their house and some three thousand people believed. The king gave up his kingdom and went to live in his daughter's tower.

There followed four more judges and one deliverance after another. Sedechie, who came to the throne after her father, threw her into a pit filled with snakes and vipers and basilisks, where she lived ten days unharmed while the creatures died; he tried to saw her apart and two saws broke and the sawyers fell on the pieces and died, and when the third had begun on her a storm of thunder and hail killed the servants and an angel rolled the stone off her breast and healed the wound; he bound her to a mill wheel and the water stood still as stone. His son Savah came against the city with a hundred thousand men and was struck blind with all his army; she prayed and their sight was given back, and afterwards he too turned on her.

Under Numerian, at Callinicum, three brazen oxen were heated for her; the first and the second cooled, and the third walked a quarter of a stade and broke open, and she came out whole, and ten thousand believed. His prefect Babodon, who was to kill her, bound her on an iron grate and poured oil over the fire; an angel loosed her, and the prefect fell at her feet and asked to be made a Christian. At last Savorie, who reigned at Mesembria in Thrace, had her seized and killed with the sword, and she was buried outside the city; and the power of God raised her, and she walked back into Mesembria with an olive branch in her hand, so that the king fell down before her and the whole people were baptised by the priest Timothy.

She went home to Magedon, found her father fallen asleep in the Lord, stayed a little with her mother, and was carried off by a cloud to Ephesus, where she preached and healed. Old Apelian came to her there, sent by God. The next day she took him and six reverent men outside the city, found a new empty tomb cut in the rock, went into it and told them to close it well with the stone so that no one should open it before the fourth day. On the fourth day they came back and the body was gone, and they said among themselves that Christ had taken her to paradise; and the people ran and saw the empty tomb and glorified God. This account, the page says, Apelian wrote.

A note at the end of the page argues the chronology: her father Licinius is not the Licinius who later shared the empire with Constantine and married his sister, but another of the same name, many years earlier, king of a city called Magedon on the border of Macedonia — a kinglet rather than an emperor, «Vasilisc, iar nu Basileus», as the prologue writes it. That Licinius lived at Nicomedia and died a pagan; this one lived at Magedon and ended his life a Christian. So she belongs to the first century, near the days of the apostles, since the priest Timothy who baptised her had his teaching and his priesthood from the Apostle Paul.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and tells the same story, with two differences worth setting down. It dates her: it says she contended in the fourth century after Christ and that she remained at Ephesus, working many miracles and honoured as a true equal of the apostles, until the day of her falling asleep in the year 315 — which is three centuries away from the chronology argued on the Romanian page above, and neither year is written into this folder. And it names what the Romanian account left unnamed: after her father's household was baptised she suffered much from the Persians and their kings Sedekias and Sapor the First; the three heated brazen oxen stood at Kallipolis of the Hellespont, where Noumerianos reigned; and it was at Ephesus that she found an empty coffin in which no one had yet been buried, went into it, and fell asleep in peace.

The Greek tells the sealing of that tomb the other way about. She had given orders that no one should move the stone with which Apellianos was to cover the coffin before four days had passed; but after two days Apellianos and the others came to the grave, and saw that the stone had been raised and the coffin was empty. The page adds that fragments of her relics are kept at the monastery of Kykkos in Cyprus and in the church of Saint George of the Greeks at Venice, and it closes by reporting two traditions against its own: that by the western martyrologies she was martyred at Thessalonica, thrown into the fire, and that by the Menologion of the emperor Basil II she was perfected by beheading.

The Serbian church keeps her on this same menologion day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović tells her at length and takes the earlier of the two chronologies this folder already carries. She lived in the apostolic times, it says, in the Balkans, in some city of Magedon where her father Licinius was a petty king; and some, the book adds, think that she was a Slav. It gives her instruction in the faith to Apelian her teacher, where the Romanian page has him only read the three birds for her, and it has Saint Timothy, the disciple of the Apostle Paul, baptise her together with her handmaids and bring her the Apostle's letters to read. Her refusal to marry angered her father, and when he meant to torture her she turned him to Christ by a wonder.

Four kings besides her father put her to torments, and God preserved her through his angels each time. Sedekiah buried her up to the head in a ditch filled with snakes and scorpions, and an angel of God killed the venomous vermin and kept the virgin unhurt. The same king tried to saw her through, and the saw rebounded from her body as from stone; he bound her to a wheel under a mill and let in the water, and the water would not run but stood still, and she was left alive and whole.

His son Savah shod her with nails, loaded a sack of sand upon her, bridled her, and had her led like a beast far outside the city; and she ran bridled behind her tormentors saying that she was indeed as a beast before her Lord. Then an angel of God shook the earth, and the earth opened and swallowed them. She went on to Kalipolj, where king Numerian threw her into three white-hot metal oxen one after another, and she came out alive and many believed; the eparch Vavodin took her to the city Constantina and set her on heated gratings, and that too did her no harm and brought many to the true faith.

Last of all she came to Mesembria, where king Savorije put her to death and God raised her again; and the king, seeing it with a great crowd of his people, believed in Christ and was baptised. By her sufferings and her wonders, this book reckons, she brought more than a hundred thousand pagans to the faith of Christ — a number neither of the other accounts gives. At the end she lay down in the grave of her own accord and ordered Apelian to close it; and when it was opened after four days she was not in it.

**No year was taken from this page and none could be.** „Живела у времена апостолска“ — she lived in the apostolic times — is not a year or a century with bounds, and this folder deliberately holds no `dates` because the Romanian page argues the first century and the Greek page says the fourth and the year 315. The Prologue is a **third** account and it stands with the first of those readings, which is recorded here and settles nothing: a book siding with one of two sources is not a bound, and nothing was written into `dates`.

The Russian church calendar keeps her on the same day and gives her three lives, the longest of which is the fullest account this folder has. She lived in the first century and bore the name Пенелопа before her baptism; she was the daughter of a pagan, Ликиний, the ruler of Магеддон in Macedonia, who built her a separate and sumptuous palace where she lived with her governess Кария among girls of her own age and her servants. A teacher named Апелиан came to her daily to instruct her in the sciences; he was a Christian, and while teaching he spoke to her of Christ the Saviour and taught her the Christian faith and the Christian virtues.

When she was grown and her parents began to think of marrying her, three birds flew in at her window one after another — a dove with an olive branch, an eagle with a crown and a raven with a snake. Her teacher explained the sign: the dove, which stood for her humility, meekness and chastity, had brought the olive branch, the grace of God received in baptism; the eagle, the sign of a mind lifted up by the thought of God, had brought a crown for victory over the invisible enemy; and the raven had brought the snake because the devil would array himself against her and bring her griefs, sorrows and persecutions. He ended by telling her that the Lord wished to betroth her to himself and that she would suffer much for her heavenly Bridegroom.

She refused marriage, was baptised at the hands of the apostle «Тимофей», the disciple of the apostle «Павел», and was named Ирина. She began persuading her parents to accept the faith; her mother rejoiced, and her father at first did not hinder her but then demanded that she worship the pagan gods. When she refused firmly, he had her bound and thrown under the hooves of savage horses — and the horses stood still, except one, which broke its tether, seized him by the right arm and tore it from the shoulder, then knocked him down and trampled him. The girl was untied, and at her prayer he rose unhurt before the witnesses with a sound arm; and seeing that wonder he and his wife and about three thousand of the people believed in Christ.

He gave up the government and went to live in his daughter's palace, and she began to preach among the pagans. The new ruler, Седекия, questioned her teacher about how she lived, called her in, and tried to make her stop preaching and sacrifice. She confessed her faith without trembling; so she was thrown into a pit full of snakes and reptiles, and stayed in it ten days unharmed, an angel of the Lord keeping her and bringing her food. He set that down to sorcery and ordered her sawn apart with an iron saw: the saws broke one after another and did her body no harm, and only the fourth drew blood. When he mocked her, asking where her God was, a whirlwind rose, a blinding lightning struck many of the torturers, there was thunder and heavy rain, and many who saw that sign from heaven believed.

He went on with new tortures and the Lord kept her whole, until the people rose against him and drove him out; and the rulers who followed him tortured her likewise and likewise failed. In all, the account says, more than ten thousand pagans were turned by her preaching and the wonders. She went from her own city to Callipolis and preached there, and its ruler Вавадон, who had put her to fresh executions, came to his senses and believed, and a great number of pagans with him, all of whom were baptised by the apostle Тимофей. She preached afterwards at Constantina and Mesembria.

At Ephesus the Lord revealed to her that her end was near. She went out of the city with her old teacher and other Christians to a cave in the hills, signed herself with the cross and went in, telling those with her to close the entrance with a great stone, which they did; and when the Christians came back on the fourth day they did not find her body in it. The shortest of the three lives adds that her memory was greatly honoured in ancient Byzantium and that several magnificent churches were built in her honour at Constantinople.
