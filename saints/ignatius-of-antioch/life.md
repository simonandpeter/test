# Ignatius of Antioch

When Trajan took the sceptre of the Romans, Ignatius was bishop of the Church of Antioch — the Godbearer by name and in deed, who received the throne after Evodius. The life tells that when he was a small child, and the Lord was living among men on earth, his parents stood near in the crowd listening, and the Lord called the child to him, set him in the midst and took him in his arms, saying that unless you turn and become as children you shall not enter the kingdom of heaven. He was called the Godbearer for having been carried in the hands of God incarnate, and because he bore God in his heart and in his mouth.

He was first a disciple of [John the Theologian](/saints/john-the-theologian), together with [Polycarp](/saints/polycarp-of-smyrna), bishop of Smyrna, and was then set bishop of Antioch — where the name of Christian was first spoken — by the counsel of all the Apostles. He sowed the preaching of the true faith with much labour and sweat, and it was he who set the divine hymns to be sung in two choirs, in the likeness of the choirs of angels, having seen them in a vision singing so, each choir listening while the other sang. From Antioch all the Churches took that good order.

Trajan, having beaten the Scythians, resolved to thank the idols with sacrifices throughout his empire, and raised a persecution against the Christians, who would not sacrifice. Passing through Antioch on his way to war against the Persians, he heard Ignatius accused of honouring as God the Christ whom Pilate condemned, and of laying down laws for virginity and for the contempt of riches. Called before him, Ignatius said he bore Christ in himself, and told the emperor that had he known Christ, his purple and his crown and his throne would have been the fairer for it.

The emperor offered him the high priesthood of Zeus and the title of father of his court. Ignatius answered that he had no need to be Zeus's priest when he was a priest of Christ, to whom he offered daily the sacrifice of praise and longed to be wholly a sacrifice himself. He was sent to Rome to be given to the beasts, and wrote to the Romans on the way, from Smyrna. In the arena he cried out to the Romans watching that he suffered for no crime but for his God alone: he was God's wheat, and wished to be ground by the teeth of the beasts that he might be made pure bread for him.

The lions tore him at once and ate him, leaving only the bones. The faithful gathered what was left and laid it with honour in a marked place outside the city, on the twentieth day of December; and when they sat weeping by the grave he appeared to them at night and comforted each of them. Trajan, hearing how he had gone to death without fear, was sorry for him, and hearing that the Christians were good and gentle people who opposed his empire in nothing, ordered that they should no longer be hunted. His relics were afterwards brought back to Antioch.

The life adds a story told of him. Because he had the name of Jesus Christ continually in his mouth, the pagans asked him why; he answered that he had that name written in his heart, and confessed with his lips the one he always carried there. After the beasts had eaten him, his heart was found whole among the bones. The unbelievers, remembering his words, cut it in two to see whether he had spoken the truth, and found written inside, on both halves, in letters of gold: Jesus Christ.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr puts a year to it. It says his time of birth and his nationality are unclear, that he came to the throne of Antioch between 68 and 70, and that he shepherded as an apostolic teacher and stood as a guard over the souls of his flock. When Trajan ordered the persecution of the Christians, Ignatius presented himself before the emperor as he was passing through Antioch and defended the rights of the Church and the truth of the Christian faith; Trajan had him seized and taken to Rome, where the Christians of the city meant to save him from martyrdom and he wrote to them to let the will of God be done. On 20 December in the year 107 he was thrown into the amphitheatre. Only the larger of his bones survived, buried with honour at Antioch and carried later to Rome, a second translation in 540 laying them in the church of Saint Clement.
