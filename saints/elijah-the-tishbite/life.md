# Elijah the Tishbite

The Romanian life opens by naming what it is about to tell: Elijah the Tishbite, the glorious seer, the zealot for God, the rebuker of lawless kings, the teacher of a people that had gone far from God, the punisher of false prophets, the wonderful worker of wonders, the one to whom the elements submitted and whom heaven obeyed, the great pleaser of God — who lives to this day in the body and is to come as a forerunner before the second coming of Christ. To show his zeal plainly, the page says, it will set out the deeds he did.

It begins with the division of the kingdom. God's chosen people, grown into twelve tribes after the number of the twelve sons of Israel, had been undivided, joined in one counsel and led by one guide, from Moses and Joshua the son of Nun and the rest of the judges of Israel down to King David and to Solomon. When Rehoboam the son of Solomon took the kingdom and, being young, would not hear the good counsel of the wise elders but listened to the rebellious counsel of young men like himself, he began to oppress the people of Israel, burdening them with taxes of many kinds and punishing them with beatings and scatterings.

Then ten of the tribes broke away from him and chose another king for themselves, named Jeroboam. This Jeroboam had earlier been a servant of Solomon, and when Solomon wished to punish him for some cause he fled into Egypt and stayed there until Solomon's death; and after Solomon died he came back, and the tribes of Israel that had broken from Rehoboam made him their king, so that Rehoboam the son of Solomon reigned in Jerusalem over two tribes only. That is the state of the kingdom in which the page sets the prophet.

The Romanian page's account of his life is very long and the middle of it is left here unsummarised. At its end Elisha, seeing him taken up, cried out that his father had been the whole strength of Israel, who with his prayer and his zeal had helped the kingdom of Israel more than a great multitude of brave men and armed soldiers; and he saw him no more. He took hold of his own garments and tore them, lamenting. Then Elijah's mantle fell from above, and Elisha took it and stood on the banks of the Jordan and parted the water with it as Elijah had done and crossed on dry ground, and became heir of the grace that had worked in his teacher.

Elijah, the page ends, taken up in the body in the chariot of fire, is alive in the body to this day, kept by God in the dwellings of paradise. He was seen on Tabor by the three holy apostles at the Transfiguration of the Lord, and he will be seen again bodily by mortal men before the Lord's second coming to earth; and in that time the man who once escaped the sword of Jezebel will suffer by the sword of Antichrist, and then not only as a prophet but as a martyr in the company of the saints he will be granted greater honour and glory. The page gives no year for anything it tells.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 20 July, and saint.gr adds the years and the parentage the Romanian life does not. He was the son of Sobak, it says, and came from Thesbe — which is why he was called the Tishbite — the El Istib of today, in the region of Gilead, and he belonged to the tribe of Aaron. When he was born his father saw a divine vision: two men in white named him Elijah, swaddled him in fire and gave him flame to eat; the priests at Jerusalem, hearing the vision described, told the father that his son would become a prophet and would judge Israel with a two-edged sword and with fire. He exercised his prophetic gift for twenty-five years in the days of king Ahab, whose reign the page dates 873 to 854 BC, and his war was against Ahab and still more against Jezebel, who was no Israelite and was the cause of the faith being adulterated with idolatrous customs; she hunted him as Herodias hunted the Forerunner. The page sets his place among the prophets by the New Testament's own witness — Zacharias's word that John would come «ἐν πνεύματι καὶ δυνάμει Ἠλίου», Christ's that John was the Elijah who was to come, and the sight the disciples had on the mountain at the Transfiguration of Moses and Elijah speaking with Christ. One thing it tells that the Romanian life does not: eight or ten years after he was taken up, Elijah sent a letter, perhaps by an angel, to king Joram, foretelling his death because he had turned from the worship of the true God, and the page quotes it from 2 Chronicles. It closes with the praise of him in the Wisdom of Sirach, where the prophet rose up like fire and his word burned like a torch.
