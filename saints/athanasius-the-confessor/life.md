# Athanasius the Confessor

This venerable man was born at Constantinople, doxologia's life says, of devout parents who honoured God and were very rich. He was reverent from his earliest years and wanted to put on the monastic habit; so he went to a monastery that stood near Nicomedia, close to the sea, and was tonsured there. He rose so far in the virtues that he became known even to emperors.

In the days of Leo the breaker of icons he was denounced for honouring the most pure icons, and for that was given over to many torments, to a bitter banishment and to afflictions. He did not bend. Keeping the Orthodox faith to the end, he passed over to the Lord. The page names no year and no monastery beyond the one near Nicomedia, and prints no hymn for him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and names his monastery. saint.gr has him born at Constantinople of rich and devout parents, and at a young age he came to the monastery of Paulopetrion — which stands, the page says, in the gulf of Nicomedia between the villages of Panteichion and Touzla — and was tonsured there. He was joined in friendship to [Theodore the Studite](/saints/theodore-the-studite), whose memory the page puts on 11 November, and to John, abbot of the monastery of Kathara, kept on 27 April, and he struggled hard for Orthodoxy through the iconoclast period: under the emperor the page calls Leo the Isaurian, giving his years as 813 to 820, he was persecuted with the other confessors of the faith on behalf of the holy icons, and exiled. He was shown to be a spring of Orthodoxy and a harbour of the Church, the page ends, and fell asleep in peace. The distich over him makes him a nursling of Paulopetrion who is now in the company of the apostles Paul and Peter, for whom the house was named.

The Russian calendar keeps him on the same day and gives the monastery his epithet comes from. He was born at Constantinople of rich and devout parents, and from childhood wished to give himself wholly to God; when he was grown he withdrew to one of the houses of Nicomedia called Paulopetria — named for the two chief apostles — and was tonsured there, and the height of his ascetic life became known at the emperor's court. In the reign of the iconoclast whom its page calls Leo the Armenian, and dates 813 to 820, he was put to various torments for honouring the icons, bore exile, and endured much grief and suffering. Confessing the Orthodox faith to the end of his life, he died in peace, the calendar says, in the year 821, which is the year its day line prints beside his name.
