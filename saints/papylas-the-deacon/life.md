# Papylas the Deacon

Papylas and Carpus were born at Pergamum of right-believing parents, and the page calls them great pillars and foundations of the Church. Both gave their bodies only what was needed, and so little even of that that they differed from the angels in body alone. Rising by such a life to the height of the virtues, they were trusted with the governing of the Church: [Carpus](/saints/carpus-of-thyatira) was chosen bishop and preached the right faith to those in Thyatira, and Papylas, honoured by him with the rank of deacon, matched him in his labours.

The devil found servants of his malice who slandered them to the pagan emperor Decius, and the proconsul Valerie ordered everyone in that country to the place of sacrifice; the two were found elsewhere, offering the pure sacrifice of prayer, and were brought before him. As they answered, the earth shook and the idols broke and scattered like dust. Valerie had iron chains put on their necks, had them led naked through the streets, and then tried soft words and got a harder answer.

He gave their property to their accusers, tied them to horses and had them driven from Thyatira to Sardis in a single day; their servant [Agathodorus](/saints/agathodorus-servant-of-carpus-and-papylas) suffered with them and was beaten to death there with ox sinews. Brought forward as though for the first time, Papylas was asked his family, his country and his trade. He was born of the best stock, he said, in the city of Pergamum, and his craft was medicine — not the medicine made of the herbs that grow on the earth, but that which is received from God above, which heals not the body only but the pains of the soul.

The judge answered that skilled medicine is impossible without what Galen and Hippocrates wrote. Then he called his servants to invoke the gods and heal the blind eye of one of his counsellors, and all day they cried to Apollo, to Asclepius, to Zeus and to Hermes, sacrificing to things without soul and shouting to the deaf, and could not give light to one bodily eye, being themselves blind in soul; but Papylas raised his eyes, bodily and spiritual, to heaven, and the eye was opened.

For that benefit Valerie repaid him by having him hung on a piece of wood and beaten without sparing; and the page says he felt no pain from the blows but grieved that he was not given heavier torments. Fire was kindled under his thighs, and he was ordered to be stoned; but the stones fell aside, as though they too honoured a body suffering for Christ, and struck those who threw them instead. The judge rested from beating them, hoping their wounds would fester, and instead the wounds healed until no trace of them could be seen.

Beasts were loosed on the martyrs and would not touch them: a she-bear lay down and embraced their feet, and a lion, roaring, spoke with a human voice and rebuked the tormentors. Iron shoes full of sharp nails were put on them and they were made to run. Then Valerie heated a furnace and threw them into it, and [Agathonica](/saints/agathonica-sister-of-papylas), the sister of Papylas, went into the flame with them of her own will; a great rain came down, the fire went out, and they came out unhurt and sang to God in the prison as in a church.

The judge, not knowing what more to do, was ashamed and beaten, and gave his last sentence: that their heads be cut off. They were led out to the place of killing rejoicing, and lifting their eyes and their hands to heaven they prayed not only for themselves but for those who were killing them; and so they ended their struggle, dying by the sword. The faithful took up their bodies in secret, which had been thrown out without honour and left unregarded, and buried them with honour. The page names Decius as the emperor and Valerie as the judge, and gives no year, and none is supplied here.
