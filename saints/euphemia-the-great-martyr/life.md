# Euphemia

Euphemia lived and was martyred in the years of the emperor Diocletian. She was born at Chalcedon to a God-fearing and noble family; her parents Psilophron and Theodoriane took care that their daughter should grow in every Christian virtue, and she grew into a person of rare gifts and strong Christian spirit, which she showed when the pagan proconsul of Asia Minor, Priscus, ordered all the inhabitants of Chalcedon to attend a festival he was holding in honour of the pagans’ god Ares: Euphemia resolved with other Christians to stay away from the pagan festival, and for this was seized and imprisoned.

During her captivity the enemies of Christ tried in every way to persuade her to deny her faith and embrace the idols, and when they realised that she would not change her faith for words they tortured her horribly; but by divine grace she suffered nothing from the tortures, and at last the executioners threw her to wild beasts, and Euphemia found her death from a bear; its verse says, slain for God by the jaws of a bear, you must be crowned with praises, Euphemia. The Romanian Lives call her a very beautiful virgin of good family, daughter of the devout Philophron and Theodorosia; the Greek and Romanian calendars keep her on 16 September with a full service and kontakia — her relics, the Romanian kontakion recalls, received the decree of faith from the six hundred and thirty God-bearing fathers at Chalcedon.

The Russian calendar keeps her on 16 September old style and dates her about 304, and its life names her parents as the senator Philophron and Theodosia. Forty-nine Christians, Euphemia among them, hid in one house during Priscus’s festival of Ares and were tortured for nineteen days; then Priscus sent the others to the emperor Diocletian and kept back the youngest, Euphemia, hoping that alone she would give way. She was bound to a wheel set with sharp knives, which stopped of itself, and an angel took her down and healed her.

The soldiers [Victor](/saints/victor-of-chalcedon) and [Sosthenes](/saints/sosthenes-of-chalcedon), ordered to throw her into a burning furnace, saw two terrible angels in the flame, refused, confessed themselves Christians and were given to the beasts; other soldiers threw her in, and she came out unharmed, and afterwards walked unhurt over a pit of knives hidden under earth and grass. Condemned at last to the beasts in the circus, she prayed that God would let her die; none of them attacked her but a she-bear, which gave her a small wound in the foot, and she died at once.

An earthquake scattered the guards and the crowd, and her parents buried her near Chalcedon. The great church later built over her grave was where the Fourth Ecumenical Council met in 451, when she confirmed the Orthodox confession, remembered on 11 July. After the Persians took Chalcedon in 617 her relics were brought to Constantinople, about 620; in the iconoclast years the shrine was thrown into the sea, recovered by sailors, came to Lemnos, and was returned to Constantinople in 796. The Russian calendar keeps her with two troparia and a kontakion.

The Ohrid Prologue, for the Serbian calendar, names her mother Theodorisia, sets the torments of the forty-nine at nineteen days and her separation from them on the twentieth, has her thrown into a pit of water full of venomous creatures, which she signed with the cross, and dates her death to 304; it adds that she is remembered on 11 July as well.
