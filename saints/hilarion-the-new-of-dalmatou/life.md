# Hilarion the New of Dalmatou

The blessed Hilarion was the son of Peter the Cappadocian, who set the bread on the imperial table, and of Theodosia; both his parents were devout, and being lovers of God they taught him the holy Scripture well from his youth. When he was twenty he left, as the Gospel says, father and mother and house and riches, and became a monk in the monastery of Hesychius near Byzantium. Afterwards he came to the monastery of Dalmatou and there received the angelic and holy great habit, and became a disciple of Saint Gregory the Decapolite, who was living there at that time.

He lived in obedience, in silence and in humility, having his service in the monastery garden. He laboured in that obedience ten years, and strove to follow in his own life the venerable Hilarion the Great, who bore the same name as he; reading that father's amended and God-pleasing life often, he made himself like him so far as he could, in fasting, in all-night prayer and in every monastic struggle, and for that he was called Hilarion the New.

His soul being lit up like the sun, he received from God power over the unclean spirits to drive them out; and the abbot of that monastery made him a priest, even against his will, after Saint Gregory the Decapolite had gone away to other places. Some years later, when the abbot of that monastery died and Hilarion learned that the brethren meant to make him their abbot, he went out of the monastery by night, unknown to anyone, and went to Byzantium, looking for Gregory his teacher, whom he hoped to find there; but Gregory had gone to Rome, and from there had returned and settled on Mount Olympus.

So Hilarion entered a monastery in Byzantium, and the monks of Dalmatou, learning of him, sent to the most holy Patriarch Nicephorus and begged him to make Hilarion their abbot even against his will. The patriarch told this to the emperor, and they sent and brought Hilarion to them and urged him to take the abbacy of the monastery of Dalmatou. Being unable to resist the imperial and patriarchal will, he submitted to their order, took the rule of the house, and shepherded well for eight years the rational sheep entrusted to him.

After this Leo the Armenian — the tyrant with a beast's name — took the throne of the Greek empire, and began to trouble the Church of Christ with the heresy of the fighting against icons. He forced many to join him in his heresy, and those who would not submit he tormented or drove into distant lands. At that time, by his order, Hilarion was brought from the monastery of Dalmatou to the imperial palace and pressed to that heresy; but the good soldier of Christ not only did not submit at all, he rebuked the emperor boldly, calling him godless and a new breaker of the law, and so moved him to great anger. The emperor laid many beatings and torments of many kinds upon him and shut him in prison.

After a time he set him before an examination again, and gaining nothing of his purpose gave him over to the patriarch of his own mind, Theodotus surnamed Kassiteras, who had taken the throne after the most holy Nicephorus was driven out for the right faith. That false patriarch did to Hilarion what the emperor had done: shutting him in a dark prison, he tormented him many days with hunger and thirst, ordering that neither bread nor water be given him. Then the monks of his monastery went to the emperor and begged, "O emperor, give us our shepherd, and we promise to submit to thy will." The emperor rejoiced at their promise and gave them their father at once.

So the saint went to the monastery and stayed a while, and having taken a little rest after those sufferings and that hunger he turned back to suffering again. For the emperor, waiting for the monks' promise, learned that he was being mocked by them, and sent his soldiers against that monastery, who harried the monks and brought Hilarion away and shut him in prison. Afterwards they sent him to the monastery of Phaneon, ordering him to be shut in a narrow cell; and he suffered there six months, taking many troubles and insults from the harsh abbot of that house. Then he was brought out again by imperial order and taken to Constantinople, where the emperor pressed him, now with enticements and now with threats, to renounce the icons.

But Christ's sufferer Hilarion would not obey the emperor's command, and was sent to another monastery called Kyklobion; and he lived in it two years and six months, held and tormented in a foul-smelling prison. From there he was brought before the emperor again, and after a very severe beating was banished to the city of Protilia. Afterwards that evil-believing emperor, who had destroyed many, perished himself dreadfully, cut down with swords in church by his own soldiers, in the very place where he had first mocked and thrown down the holy icon of Christ, and so gave up his wretched soul.

When Michael the Stammerer took the throne he ordered all the right-believing to be loosed from their bonds and let out of the prisons. Hilarion, being freed then, did not go back to his own monastery, since the heresy of the icon-fighters had not ceased and the episcopal thrones were held by false shepherds and teaching hierarchs, but lived with a certain faithful woman who gave him a quiet place on her estate, made him a cell and a garden, and served him for God's sake with all he needed.

At that time Saint Theodore the Studite, who had likewise suffered many evils from the heretics for the right faith, returned from banishment and passed to the Lord; and his holy soul, borne up to heaven by the angels, was seen by Hilarion, as is written of it in Theodore's life. On the day Theodore departed, Hilarion was walking in his garden, working and singing the psalms of David, when he suddenly heard most wonderful voices and smelt a fragrance beyond telling. Wondering where they came from, he looked up to the height of heaven and saw a multitude of heavenly ranks in white garments, shining with bright faces, coming down from heaven with songs to meet some honoured person.

Seeing this, the blessed Hilarion fell to the ground in great fear, and heard one saying to him: "Behold the soul of Theodore, abbot of the monastery of Studios, who suffered for the holy icons unto blood and endured in his troubles to the end; and now he has fallen asleep and goes up in dancing, and the heavenly powers come to meet him." Made worthy of so wonderful a vision, Hilarion was filled with much consolation and received a great spiritual sweetness in his heart, and remained many days rejoicing in spirit, his face shining with gladness like an angel's.

He lived with that woman seven years and more. After Michael the Stammerer his son Theophilus came to the empire, who gathered all the confessors and began again, like the evil-believing emperors before him, to press them to the fighting against the holy icons and to torment those who would not submit. Then Hilarion too was taken and brought to the emperor, and being pressed did not submit to the imperial command but rather rebuked him as a pagan and a breaker of the law who was ruining the right dogmas of the faith. For that he received a hundred and seventy blows and was banished to the island of Aphousia; but there he had this relief, that he was held neither in prison nor in bonds, and so he built himself a very small cell and lived in it until the end of Theophilus.

After that emperor's death the empress Theodora came, who gathered all the confessors into the imperial city, confirmed the right faith and set the holy icons in the churches of God. Then Hilarion too was freed and took the monastery of Dalmatou, where he shone with wonders; and having lived three years and guided his disciples with divine pleasingness, he went to the Lord. His honoured and holy soul, like Theodore's which he had seen, was carried by angels to the heavenly things and stood in the rank of the holy confessors before the throne of the glory of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. The page gives no year.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and puts names and years to the whole course of his life. saint.gr says he was born in 775 and came of Cappadocia; that his parents, Peter, who was the bread-purveyor of the palace, and Theodosia, were devout and virtuous people who nursed their little son on the waters of the Orthodox faith. When he came of age, longing for the road of virtue and of ascetic labour, he went to the monastery of Xeronesia in Constantinople and gave himself there, soul and body, to asceticism, strict fasting, silence and the study of the divine Scriptures. Later he went to the monastery of the Dalmatians and was clothed in the great habit; there he stayed ten years as the gardener, and quickly became an example of asceticism, humility and magnanimity to all the brethren, who elected him abbot of the house by one voice. When the storm of the iconoclasm broke, the emperor Leo the Armenian and the impious patriarch Theodotos Melissenos tried and failed to bend the high-hearted purpose of the venerable one, and the relentless persecution of him began. For eight whole years he bore without a murmur, and with a wonderful endurance, confinement in distant monasteries, imprisonments, beatings and exiles, holding out as a good soldier of Jesus Christ. After the triumph of Orthodoxy he came back to his monastery, lived three more years, and reposed in peace at seventy years old, in 845.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 6 June, the menologion day both the other calendars keep, and it is the third book to tell this life and the first that adds nothing to it. Abbot of the Dalmatou house in Constantinople; disciple of [Gregory the Decapolite](/saints/gregory-the-decapolite); an imitator of [Hilarion the Great](/saints/hilarion-the-great), whose name he took; mighty in prayer, steadfast and brave in suffering, and he suffered much for the icons in the days of the wicked iconoclast emperors, Leo the Armenian and the rest.

The two sentences it dwells on are the two this page already has. Leo was cut down with swords by his own soldiers in the same church and on the same spot where he had first mocked the holy icons and from which he had taken the first icon down; and Hilarion was let out of prison then, but not for long, being tormented again and held until the right-believing empress Theodora came. And he was clear-sighted: he saw the angels of God carrying the soul of [Theodore the Studite](/saints/theodore-the-studite) up to heaven. It puts his repose in 845, in his seventieth year, which is the year and the age the Greek calendar gives as well, so nothing here is widened.
