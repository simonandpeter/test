# Theoprepius, son of Philetus and Lydia

saint.gr's calendar for 27 Μαρτίου prints one line for a household and the officers who tried it: «Άγιοι Φιλητός ο Συγκλητικός, Λυδία σύζυγος αυτού, Θεοπρέπιος και Μακεδόνας τα τέκνα αυτών, Αμφιλόχιος ο Δούκας και Κρονίδης ο κομενταρήσιος οι Μάρτυρες» — the holy martyrs Philetus the senator, Lydia his wife, Theoprepius and Macedon their children, Amphilochius the duke and Cronides the commentarisius. Theoprepius is the elder of the two children the line names.

In a time of persecution, in the year 125, when Hadrian was emperor, [Philetus](/saints/philetus-the-senator) with his wife [Lydia](/saints/lydia-wife-of-philetus) and their two sons were seized and required to deny Christ; parents and children alike held to their confession. Because they could not stand against the wisdom of Philetus, their accusers handed them over to the duke [Amphilochius](/saints/amphilochius-the-duke), duke in Slavonia, who hung Philetus and Lydia on a tree and flayed them. At the sight [Cronides](/saints/cronides-the-commentarisius) the commentarisius believed in Christ, and was shut up in prison with the rest.

That night, while the saints were singing and praying, angels came and gave them courage for the struggle. The next day Amphilochius had them thrown into a bronze cauldron, heated and filled with oil and resin; the saints made the sign of the Cross and the cauldron went cold. The duke believed, threw himself in after them, and a voice from heaven answered his prayer. When the emperor heard that the saints had been kept safe and sound he set them free, and so, the page ends, they ended their lives praying. Its couplet for the two brothers says otherwise — «Θνῄσκει Θεοπρέπιος σὺν Μακεδόνι, Θεοπρεπῶς ᾄδοντες ὕμνους Κυρίῳ», Theoprepius dies together with [Macedon](/saints/macedon-son-of-philetus), the two singing hymns to the Lord in a manner befitting God — and the page does not reconcile the two endings.

The Russian calendar keeps the company four days earlier in the menologion than the Greek, on the twenty-third of March, old style, and tells the whole of it in one reading. Philetus was a man of rank at the court of the emperor Hadrian, and for confessing Christ openly he came before the court with his wife and their two sons. By Hadrian’s own order the family was sent into Illyria, to the commander there, to be put to the torture; and he had them hung on a tree and their bodies scraped with knives.

They were then shut up in prison together with the governor of the prisons, who had come to believe. In the night an angel stood before them and strengthened them for what was coming. The next day the martyrs were lowered into a cauldron of boiling oil, and the oil went cold in an instant and they were unhurt. The commander was so struck by it that he believed in Christ and stepped into the boiling oil himself, praying, Lord Jesus Christ, help me — and lived.

The same torture was tried again when Hadrian himself came into Illyria. The holy martyrs were thrown into the boiling oil again and again and by the power of God stayed alive, and the emperor went back to Rome shamed, while they gave thanks and glorified God and gave up their souls to him in prayer. The calendar sets the whole of it inside Hadrian’s reign and gives no closer year than that.

His name page is the only one of the six that gives a second form, «Боголеп», the Slavonic rendering of the Greek name — comely to God — and the stored form keeps both as the page prints them. The folder had no date before this.
