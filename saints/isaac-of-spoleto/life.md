# Isaac of Spoleto

The account of him is [Gregory the Dialogist](/saints/gregory-the-dialogist)'s, as the Ohrid Prologue says in its first sentence. He had come into Italy in the Gothic time, and in the city of Spoleto went into a church one day to pray. He asked the sacristan to leave him locked in the church overnight, and so passed the whole night at prayer without moving from the spot; and the next day the same, and the next night too. The sacristan called him a hypocrite and struck him in the face — and in that hour went out of his mind. Seeing the man tormented so cruelly, Isaac bent over him, and the evil spirit fled from him, and the sacristan was made well.

People heard of it and the whole city gathered about this strange foreigner. They offered him money and lands, but he refused it all and took nothing, and withdrew instead into the forest, where he made himself a cell; and that cell turned before long into a great monastery. Isaac was renowned for his wonderworking, and above all for his second sight, and the entry tells three stories of it.

One evening he told the brethren to carry all the mattocks out into the vineyard and leave them there. The next day he set out for the vineyard with the brethren and had a dinner carried along, and the brethren wondered who the dinner could be for when they had no labourers. When they came, there were as many men digging as there had been mattocks: thieves had come to steal the mattocks, and by the power of God had been held there digging the whole night through.

Another time two men came to Isaac all but naked and asked him for clothing. He sent a monk to a hollow tree by the roadside, to bring whatever he found in it; the monk went, found some clothes, and brought them to the monastery. The abbot took those clothes and handed them to the beggars — who were put to deep shame when they recognised their own, which they themselves had hidden in that tree.

And once a man sent two beehives to the monastery. The monk carrying them hid one beside the road and brought the other and handed it over; and the saint said to him: take care on the way back — a venomous snake has crept into the hive you left by the road. Beware it does not bite you.

That is the whole of the entry. It gives him no year, saying only that he came into Italy in the Gothic time, and nothing has been set down here beyond that. The Prologue heads him «Isaac the Syrian II» and notes in the same breath that the Isaac the Syrian it keeps on 28 January is another man, which is why this is a folder of its own and not a second day for [Isaac the Syrian](/saints/isaac-the-syrian).

**The Russian calendar keeps him on the same menologion day, gives him a year the folder did not have, and calls him by the name this corpus prints for another man.** Its line is «Прп. Исаака Сирина в Сполете Италийском (550).» and the index behind it heads him «Преподобный Исаак Сирин, Сполетский, Италийский» — Isaac the Syrian, of Spoleto, of Italy. That is the clash the Serbian form already on this folder was written to hold off, «Исаак Сирин II», the second Isaac the Syrian; the Russian form stored here keeps both of the calendar's own epithets for the same reason, and the bare name belongs to the father of Nineveh.

Of the two readings the index lists, one is a life and one is a stub: the first says he was a Syrian by birth who ascetised in a desert place in Italy, was granted the gifts of discernment and of wonders for his holy life, and drew so many lovers of the monastic life by his holiness and his strictness that a monastery formed about him. He was marked by meekness and by owning nothing, and when his disciples asked why he refused what was offered him he answered that a monk who acquires possessions is no longer a monk. He reproved men's sins wisely and without malice, wishing the salvation of all, and reposed about 550. The second reading, life840, carries his name and nothing else at all — a title with no body.

The year is the folder's first date of any kind. It stood undated because the Greek page it was written from gave none, and both the day's line and the life now give 550, the line flatly and the life as «около 550 г.», about 550; the reading taken is the circa, which is the weaker of the two and the one that cannot be wrong if the other is right.
