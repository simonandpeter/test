# Pachomius the Great

Pachomius came of the parts of the Egyptian Thebaid, born of pagan parents who worshipped idols — a lily, the life says, out of a thornbush. As a small child he could not keep down what was given him from the sacrifices of the idols; and when his parents took him once to the idol temple by the Nile, where the demon in the idol answered those who questioned it, the demon fell silent and the servant of the temple cried out that they had brought in the enemy of his gods and should take him away. His parents did not understand it, and kept quiet, and afterwards put him to Egyptian letters and the old philosophy.

In the time of the emperor Constantine, when an army was being gathered against Maxentius, the officers came as far as Egypt and took many young men by force into the ranks, Pachomius among them, at the age of twenty. The ship brought them to a Christian city of the Thebaid called Oxyrhynchus, where they were kept under guard, and the townspeople, pitying them, brought them food and drink and comforted them in their trouble. Pachomius, who had never heard the name of Christ or of Christians, asked who these people were, and was told: men who fear God, who believe in one God in heaven, the Maker of all, and in His only-begotten Son the Lord Jesus Christ, and in the Holy Spirit, and who are merciful to everyone and look for their reward from God alone.

He drew a little apart from his companions, stretched out his hands and prayed: Lord, God of the Christians, who made heaven and earth, if Thou wilt look on my lowliness and give me the knowledge of Thy godhead and deliver me from this trouble, I will serve Thee all the days of my life and live by Thy commandments. From then he kept himself carefully from everything displeasing to God, and after his release and his baptism he went not home but into the desert, to the elder Palamon, whose obedient and abstinent disciple he became.

He went barefoot for firewood over a hillside full of thorns, and bore the bleeding of his hands and feet gladly, remembering the nails of his Master. Once, having gone far from his cell to a place called Tabennisi, he heard a voice from above in his prayer: Pachomius, dwell here, and in this place make a monastery, for many will come to thee who wish to be saved, and thou shalt guide them in the monastic life after the order which I shall presently send thee; and an angel stood before him in the habit of that order. Palamon would not grieve his disciple: he went with him and built a little cell there, and afterwards told him to stay, on the promise that the two would visit each other; and not long after, Palamon fell sick of his long abstinence and died.

Pachomius's elder brother John, who had also come to the faith and had long been looking for him, found him there, and the two lived together and built cells. They quarrelled over it once, John wanting the enclosure small and strict and Pachomius wanting it larger for the brethren to come, and Pachomius was angry; he said nothing back out of respect for the elder, but shut himself in his cell that night and wept and confessed to God that the fleshly mind was still in him. John died soon after and Pachomius buried him with psalms.

The demons came at him openly then. They walked before and behind him like the retinue of a lord, crying make way for the servant of God, and he took them for barking dogs; they shook his cell as with an earthquake; they harnessed an oak leaf with a long rope and hauled at it, shouting, to make him laugh; they sat by him in the form of beautiful women when he ate, and he shut his bodily eyes and lifted the eyes of his mind to Christ. Once he was beaten by them a whole day, from morning to evening, and was wounded like a martyr and did not despair. He walked over serpents and scorpions unhurt, and the crocodiles carried him over the river at his word.

The angel came again at midnight and said three times that it was the will of the Lord that he should serve Him and bring the race of men to Him, and from then Pachomius received those who came. The first were Psentais, Suros and Psoe, who asked why he alone should labour at the monastic works and begged him to give them a share. Others followed — Pecusie, Cornelie, Pavel, another Pachomius, another John. He would call a priest from the churches of the nearby villages for the liturgy on the feasts, and would not have any of his own disciples raised to the priesthood, saying it was of great profit to monks living the common life not to seek honour or headship; but a priest-monk who came to live with them he received with love and honoured as a father.

Once, coming apart from the business of the monastery, he was met by the devil in a shining form saying, Rejoice, Pachomius, I am Christ and have come to thee as to my friend; and he reasoned that the coming of Christ brings no disturbance but joy and fearlessness, and said, Go from me, accursed one, thou and thy vision and thy crafty counsels; and the apparition went out. Visiting a brother once who had come to the monastic life from high office and knew no Egyptian but only Greek and Latin, he prayed three hours, and a written sheet was sent him from heaven into his right hand, and reading it he understood the speech of all tongues.

He came to deep old age, having led a great multitude on the way of salvation, and after the feast of Pascha fell into a bodily sickness, nursed by the blessed Theodore. When he died the brethren gathered and wept, as sons for their father, as disciples for their teacher and as sheep for their shepherd, and buried his much-labouring body with honour.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same civil day and adds the years, the family and the relics. saint.gr says he was born in 292 in the Lower Thebaid of Egypt of pagan parents and lived in the days of Constantine the Great; that he was enrolled in the army at twenty and there came to know Christian soldiers who taught him the faith; and that when he was not discharged he left the world, went up into the Upper Thebaid, was baptised and was tonsured a monk. Wanting greater stillness, he withdrew into the desert under the guidance of the famous hesychast [Palamon](/saints/palamon-the-hermit), whom the page sends to 12 August and whose perfect imitator he became. After Palamon's repose, about 320, he went to a deserted island of the Nile, Tabenne in the Upper Thebaid, where, helped by his own brother John who had taken the monastic habit, he founded a small monastery; and the fame of his holiness and prudence drew so many monks that it grew continually. The page gives his death as 348 in its head line, and says that portions of his relics are kept in the monasteries of the Great Lavra on Athos and of the Holy Trinity at Examilia in Corinthia, and in the chapel of Saint Xenia the Russian at Mandra in Attica.
