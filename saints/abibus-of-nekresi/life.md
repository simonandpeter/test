# Abibus of Nekresi

Abibus of Nekresi was one of the twelve disciples whom [John of Zedazeni](/saints/john-of-zedazeni) chose out of his Syrian community and brought with him into Georgia, in obedience to a vision in which the Mother of God told him to take twelve worthy men to strengthen the faith in her newly founded Church there. doxologia.ro prints no separate life for him. Its life of their teacher opens by naming all thirteen together — «Sfântul Cuvios Ioan Zedazneli, dimpreună cu 12 ucenici din Georgia» — and calls them Syrian ascetics and the founding fathers of Georgian monastic life.

Of him the life says more than of most of his brethren. When the archbishop came to the place where the fathers were living, Abibus was serving the Liturgy as hieromonk and Jesse as deacon; the archbishop took hold of the one with one hand and the other with the other, embraced them and took them with him to Mtskheta, where in a short while he ordained them bishops. Abibus he sent to Nekresi. There, the page says, he fought much against idolatry and was killed in the end, confessing Christ before the worshippers of fire. When the twelve were divided he is counted among those sent into Kakheti, in eastern Georgia, «care, după cum am văzut, era deja hirotonit episcop al Nekresiului și mai târziu a fost împodobit cu mucenicia».

Their teacher sent them out to preach as the apostles did, to baptise in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and to lead those in darkness to worship Christ; and he divided among them relics of the saints he had brought, so that they might be worthier on the road and might follow the example of those saints. The life says that they suffered for faith and truth as the apostles and the martyrs did, wandering in deserts and in mountains and in caves and in the clefts of the earth; that most of them held themselves to a complete lack of necessities and would not even use clothes, going out to preach in skins through the heat of the day and the cold of the night; that the faithful came to them for teaching and the sick for healing; and that where the idolatrous altars were destroyed churches were raised in their place.

doxologia's own life of him, printed under its calendar line for 29 noiembrie, tells the story of his martyrdom. In the middle of the sixth century Chosroes I, shah of Persia, took Kakheti and then Kartli and set satraps over them, and worked to abolish Christianity and impose Mazdeism, ordering fire temples with unquenched altars built in every town and village. One such temple was built at Nekresi, where Abibus was bishop; and the good shepherd, not able to bear the cult of demons in his own diocese, walked up to the altar and threw water on the flame and put it out.

From that day he went about his diocese with the cross in his hand, preaching, uprooting the error, working signs, and bringing back to the Church both the Georgians of the Caucasus who had gone over to Mazdeism and many Persians besides. The invaders seized him, whipped him without mercy and threw him into a dark prison, and then sent him bound to the satrap at Rekhi. There he told them that they kept their fire alight by feeding it with wood, while he had put it out by throwing water over it, and asked how a thing that serves a man in his daily needs could be called a god. The satrap broke off the trial, had him whipped, and, seeing with what hope he bore the pain, ordered him stoned to death.

His broken body was dragged out of the city with ropes to be eaten by beasts and birds, and watched from a distance so that no Christian should take it; but for all the days that passed neither beast nor corruption touched it, and the persecutors left it and went away. Then the brethren of the monastery of Samtavro, built by the blessed Isidore, another disciple of John of Zedazeni, took the body secretly and buried it with honour in their church. In the years of the prince Stephen (639–663) his relics were carried in procession from Samtavro to the cathedral of Mtskheta and laid in the holy table, where they rest to this day.

The Greek calendar keeps the company on the same day and tells how it was chosen. saint.gr prints one line and one page for [John](/saints/john-of-zedazeni) and his disciples, and gives each of the thirteen a head line of his own, his reading «Οσιος Αβιβος Μαθητης Του Οσιου Ιωαννη». John came out of Syria, it says, and lived about the end of the fifth century; and when the crowds that came to him had broken his stillness and he had hidden deep in the desert, he saw the Mother of God in a dream, who commanded him to choose twelve disciples and go with them into Iberia to establish the Christian faith of its people. All his subordinates wished to go, so he wrote every name on a separate paper and laid them on the holy table; and after a night of prayer, in the sight of them all, an angel of the Lord took twelve papers from the altar and put them into his hands.

Abibus is named among the twelve the papers chose. An angel warned Eulogius, bishop of the Georgian capital, of their coming, and the bishop went out with his clergy and his people to receive them; and there followed a wonder like the one at Pentecost, for John spoke the Georgian tongue. The thirteen preached through all Georgia and venerated the blessed places through which Saint Nina had passed, whom this calendar keeps on 14 January. The page gives no year for any of them, no hymn, and no word of where each of the twelve settled afterwards.
