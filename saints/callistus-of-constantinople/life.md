# Callistus I of Constantinople

Callistus struggled first on Mount Athos under the spiritual guidance of Gregory of Sinai, whose life he afterwards wrote, and whom the Romanian calendar keeps on 8 August. In 1350 he was chosen patriarch of Constantinople, and he held the throne under the emperors John Cantacuzene and John Palaiologos. Doxologia's life gives the reigns as 1341 to 1355 and 1341 to 1376, and gives no year for his birth.

In 1354 he withdrew into the quiet of the monastery he had raised in honour of Saint Mamas at Tenedos, and later he was set a second time upon the patriarchal throne, between 1355 and 1363. He died in 1363 in Serbia, where he was as ambassador of the emperor John Palaiologos.

He is known also as a spiritual writer: his works are printed in the eighth volume of the Philokalia, together with those of his good friend Ignatius of Xanthopouloi. The calendar prints no hymn for him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 20 June. saint.gr says that the ecumenical patriarch Callistus I was a partisan of the views of Gregory Palamas, and that as a monk of the monastery of Iviron on Athos he was widely known for his learning and held in great esteem above all for his gentleness and his self-restraint, being recognised as one of the weightiest representatives of the hesychasts on the Holy Mountain. He was born about the end of the thirteenth century and was trained in the monastic life and in the theory of hesychasm at the skete of Magoula, under the brothers Isaiah, Cornelius and Macarius; there he met Athanasius of Meteora, whom he later helped to build the monastery of the Transfiguration. He was the beloved disciple of Gregory of Sinai, whose life he wrote, and he followed his teacher into the desert of Paroria. In 1342 the Holy Community sent him at the head of a delegation to reconcile the warring emperors, and on his return he was set on the committee against the Bogomils. He was elected and enthroned patriarch on 10 June 1350, resigned on 27 November 1353 rather than consent to the setting aside of the lawful emperor John V Palaiologos, and held the throne a second time from 22 June 1354 until 1364. The page says he died unexpectedly at Serres, that delegations from the greatest monasteries of Athos, and especially from the Lavra, came asking Elizabeth for his body, that the Serbian ruler would not be moved by their entreaties, and that she buried him in a funerary chapel she built in the forecourt of the metropolitan church, so that she and the city of Serres might have his protection; sure indications confirm that the chapel to the left of the entrance of the metropolitan church of the Holy Theodores houses his tomb.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 20 June, the same menologion day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep him on. It gives his Athonite years a count and a neighbour that neither other book gives: twenty-eight full years at the skete of Magoula by the monastery of Philotheou, under his teacher from Sinai; and it says that there, later, he built the house of Saint Mamas — where the Romanian page put that house at Tenedos. Both places are written here and neither is chosen. He was elected patriarch in 1350, as both other books say, and withdrew again after four years, which is a span and not a date and is recorded in neither.

He wrote, this book says, a fine guide for ascetics together with Ignatius, and the lives of two saints, and many homilies. And he died in the year 1368 on the road to Serres, where he was going to the Serbian empress Jelena to ask her help against the Turks — five years later than the Romanian page’s 1363, so that his date now reads 1363 or 1368, widened to hold both rather than deciding. The Greek page had him die unexpectedly at Serres and named the Serbian ruler Elizabeth; this one names her Jelena and has him die before he arrived.

One scene it tells that no other book has. As he came up to the Holy Mountain on his way to Serbia, a saint of Kapsokalyvia saw him and said that this elder would not see his flock again, for behind him could be heard the funeral chant, Blessed are the undefiled in the way. The Prologue calls it a thing worth mentioning, and so it is; the man who said it is named in the attestation note and not here, because a folder may not take a relation merely from being prophesied over.
