# Simeon of Persia

Christians had multiplied in Persia and had their churches, their bishops, their priests and their deacons, and the magi — the teachers and defenders of the old Persian religion, who traced their descent from the magi of long before — were moved to anger against them, and others whom the page names joined them in it. They urged Shapur, king of Persia, to raise a persecution; and first of all they accused Simeon, bishop of the cities the page calls Salic and Ctesiphon, of being an enemy of the Persian kingdom and a friend of the Greek emperor who told him all that was done in Persia.

Shapur first laid a great and heavy tax upon the Christians and set savage tax-men over it. Then the priests and ministers of the Church began to be killed without shame, the goods of the churches plundered and the churches themselves levelled with the ground. Simeon he ordered to be seized and brought before him. He came in iron chains with two priests, Abdelas and Ananias, and would not bow. Asked why he did not do reverence as he had before, he answered that he had then been brought as a subject and was now brought to deny his God, and would not bow to God's enemy.

The king urged him to worship the sun, promising gifts and honours and threatening the ruin of all the Christians of his kingdom, and when he found him unmoved by either he had him thrown into prison. As he was led out of the palace Husdazat the eunuch saw him — a very old man who had brought Shapur up from childhood and held the first place in the royal house — and rose from his seat and bowed to the ground before the bishop. Simeon turned his face away and rebuked him aloud as a breaker of the law, for he had been a Christian and had afterwards worshipped the sun for fear of the king.

Husdazat put off his costly clothes, put on a poor black one, and sat weeping at the palace doors. Called in and questioned, he told the king that nothing in that house had ever grieved him as this did: that he had lived to be old, had bowed to the sun as to a god, and had honoured the creature above the Creator — in pretence, he said, and not from the heart, which made him guilty both toward God and toward the king. He swore he would not bend his knees to the sun again. The page says the king pleaded with him as a son with a father, and by turns threatened him, and found him unmoved.

In the days of the Lord's Passion the king had every Christian in the prisons brought out to death — a hundred of them, many priests, deacons and other clerics — to be killed with the sword before Simeon's eyes, and Simeon last of all. The chief of the magi offered life to any of them who would worship the sun with the king; not one answered him, for none would choose the life that passes. The bishop strengthened them out of the Scriptures, and when they had all been beheaded he bent his own head under the sword.

The two old priests taken with him were beheaded also. As Ananias bent down he began to tremble, and a nobleman of the household named Fusic, a Christian in secret, called out to him not to fear but to shut his eyes and be brave, for he would see the divine light at once — and by saying it was known for a Christian. He was killed by a crueller death than the sword, and his daughter Askitreia, a virgin, was tortured and killed with him.

A year later, on Great Friday, Azad the king's best-loved eunuch was killed, and with him a thousand martyrs, whom the Church, gathering them, numbers one thousand one hundred and fifty. The page cites the historians Sozomen and Nicephorus for the multitude who in those days came out of their houses of their own will and gave themselves up to death; and it says that after Azad was killed the king wept for him and ordered the killing to stop, sparing the people and giving only the bishops and priests to the magi. No year is given anywhere on the page.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and names three of the men who died with him. saint.gr says there was a Christian community in Persia whose people were of blameless life and great self-denial, ready to be sacrificed for the name and the glory of Christ, and that the heart of it was their bishop Simeon, an example of the Christian life and head of the churches of Ctesiphon and of Salik in Seleucia. The Persians looked on the Christians as a thorn in the eye and slandered them to king Shapur II on the pretext that they had revolutionary designs; Simeon assured the king that the Christian religion makes law-abiding citizens and not foolish rebels, but the king was so prejudiced against him that he ordered him imprisoned.

In the prison Simeon met [Gothazat](/saints/gothazat-companion-of-simeon), a Christian deserter who had changed his faith to save his head, and found the way to move his heart towards Christ again. When Shapur learnt of it he beheaded the two of them in his anger, and with them another 1150 Christians of that church, together with the presbyter [Audelas](/saints/audelas-companion-of-simeon); and at the last moment the king's own kouropalates [Phousik](/saints/phousik-companion-of-simeon), a secret Christian, was added to the martyrs' camp for urging one of the 1150 to hold out, and had his tongue cut out and was flayed alive. The page's couplet for Simeon turns on his two titles: «Ἐπίσκοπόν σε, Συμεών, ἐγὼ μέγαν, Ἐκ δὲ ξίφους μέγιστον ἀθλητὴν ἔγνων» — I knew you a great bishop, Simeon, and from the sword the greatest of contenders.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same day as the other two calendars and tells the same story in one short entry, calling the king Савориј or Сапор. Simeon was examined for Christ with his two presbyters Abdelas and Ananias, it says; before them the king's eunuch Husdazat died, who had first denied Christ and then, stung by Simeon's rebuke, confessed the true faith before the king again. A thousand Christians were brought out to the place of execution with Simeon, and Simeon put himself last on purpose, so that he might be beheaded after them and could hearten them to the end, that not one should waver for fear of death. When Ananias laid his head on the block he shook all over, and the king's officer Fusic, a Christian in secret, called out to him not to fear but to shut his eyes and be a man, that he might see the light of God — and for saying it he was named a Christian and accused to the king, who killed him with great torments and his daughter the virgin Askitreia with him. Simeon was beheaded last, having first sent his flock on into the other world. The next year, on Great Friday, the king's best-loved eunuch Azad was killed for Christ and a thousand other faithful with him, and then the king grieved for his eunuch and stopped the killing of the Christians. The entry closes with a year neither other page gives, and gives it twice over: they all suffered in 341 or 344, which is the interval this folder now holds where it held no date at all.

**The Russian calendar tells this company's story at far greater length than the Greek page these folders were written from, and it is a different story in its shape.** The Greek has the bishop meet an apostate in the prison and bring him back; the Russian has the apostate meet him on the road to the prison and be shamed into repentance in the street. Shapur II, whose reign it dates 310 to 381, issued a decree in 344 laying a heavy tax on the Christians, and when some refused to pay it — which was presented as a revolt — the persecution began. The bishop was brought to judgement in iron fetters with two presbyters, and would not bow to the king: he had bowed to his rank before, he said, but now, brought there to deny his God, it was not fitting.

Pressed to worship the sun and threatened with the wiping out of Christianity in the country, he was taken to prison; and on the road the king's own former tutor, a eunuch who had worshipped the sun out of fear, stood up and bowed to him, and was turned away from and reproached. The eunuch repented, put off his costly clothes for coarse ones and wept at the palace doors, and when the king asked what grieved him he said openly that he would worship the sun no more. He was condemned, and asked only that heralds should proclaim through the city that he died as a Christian and not for any crime against the king; and the king granted it.

Called before the king a second time the bishop refused again, and the king had every Christian then in the prison beheaded before his eyes; they laid their own heads under the sword as he blessed them. So the first of the two presbyters died. When the turn came to the second he began to tremble, and one of the nobles, a secret Christian, cried out to him not to fear but to close his eyes and he would see at once the divine Light of our Lord Jesus Christ — and with that cry gave himself away. His tongue was torn out and his skin flayed from him, and his daughter was tortured to death with him. Last of all the bishop laid his head on the block with a prayer, on 13 April 344, and the killing went on through the whole of Pascha week to the twenty-third of April.
