# Theophilus of Selention

saint.gr's calendar for 10 Οκτωβρίου keeps Theophilus the Confessor together with [Longinus the Stylite](/saints/longinus-the-stylite), and gives this Theophilus a life of his own. He came, the page says, from a village near Tiberioupolis, and was brought up on the right principles of the faith. While still young he visited the monk Stephen, who widened his spiritual and intellectual horizons; and with the help of his parents, who were devout people, he built a monastery on Mount Selention, where he lived as a monk.

In the time when Leo the Isaurian reigned, who made war on Orthodoxy, Theophilus judged that he had to be at the centre of the battle. So, leaving his skete, he began to preach for Orthodoxy; and for that work he was imprisoned at Nicaea and pressed with beatings and other torments to deny his faith. Keeping the flame of faith unquenched, he submitted to the torment of the rods. Bearing the honourable marks of that witness he returned to his monastery, where he was received with honours; but he withdrew again to his skete, and there gave up his spirit to the Lord.

The same calendar keeps another Theophilus the Confessor, also under Leo the Isaurian, on 2 Οκτωβρίου: the corpus's [Theophilus the Confessor](/saints/theophilus-the-confessor), whose entry has him going about Constantinople, imprisoned, exiled, and dying in exile. The two are separate entries on separate day pages with separate lives, and the settled rule of this corpus is that two entries on one calendar are two commemorations; this folder is the Theophilus of Mount Selention. saint.gr gives him no year and prints no hymn. The page carries no asterisk and not the sentence «Δεν είναι διασταυρωμένη η ύπαρξη του Αγίου», so the site vouches for the entry as it stands.

The Russian calendar keeps him on its own 10 October, «Прп. Феофила исп (ок. 717-741)», and its index page gives him the two epithets the day line leaves out, of Tiberiopolis and of Constantinople. Its life says he came from the country round Tiberias, and that at thirteen he left home in secret for the lavra on the mountain of Selention, where he grew up under the direction of a holy elder named Stephen; three years later he was tonsured.

When his parents learned where their son was they came to the house and asked the abbot to release Theophilus and some of the brethren, so that they might found a new monastery nearer his home. The abbot ordered all the monks to fast and pray for a sign, and on the third day a voice was heard in the church blessing the release of Theophilus, because he was to be glorified by many spiritual labours in the new house.

Then came the reign of the lawless iconoclast emperor Leo the Isaurian, which the page dates 717 to 741, and Theophilus stood up openly against the iconoclast madness. By the emperor's command he was beaten and led bound through the city like a criminal, and then handed over to the official Hypatius, who pressed him in every way to renounce the holy icons and could not shake him. Theophilus persuaded Hypatius instead, bringing in evidence the bronze serpent Moses raised, the cherubim set over the ark of the covenant, and at last how the Saviour himself gave Abgar, the prince of Edessa, his own image not made by hands.

Convinced by that conversation, Hypatius obtained the emperor's leave to release him, and the confessor went back to the monastery he had founded. He did not live long there — and here the page contradicts itself, for it ends by saying he went peacefully to the Lord in 716, a year before the reign of the emperor it has just had him resist. The folder's interval carries both ends and chooses neither.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 10 October and says, in its first line, something no other book read for this folder says: that he was by origin a Macedonian Slav from somewhere about Strumica. That is a reading of where Tiberiopolis stood rather than a different birthplace — it puts the town in Macedonia, not in Palestine, and it makes the confessor a Slav. Nothing in this folder is changed on it, because a display name is not a reader’s to settle and no coordinate has been read; the reading is recorded and left.

The rest is the story the Russian life tells, told in four sentences. He became a monk while still young and founded his own monastery. He suffered much for the icons in the time of Leo the Isaurian, and would have died then had he not succeeded in convincing the prince Hypatikos, who was his judge, of the grounds and the necessity of the veneration of icons. The prince set him free, and he went back to his monastery and ended there in peace, in the year 716 — which is the earlier of the two ends this folder’s interval already holds, and this book knows nothing of the later one.
