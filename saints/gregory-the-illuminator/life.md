# Gregory the Illuminator

Gregory was the son of Anak, the Greek synaxarion says, a kinsman of Kousaros, king of Great Armenia about 290. Anak, in league with Artaseiras, king of the Persians, killed Kousaros, and the satraps of Armenia avenged the murder by killing Anak and his whole family; only two of his children were saved, and one of them was Gregory. At Caesarea in Cappadocia, where he was studying the Scriptures under the archbishop Leontius, the murderer’s son met Tiridates, the son of the murdered king; far from shunning him, Gregory came to him in love, condemned what his father had done, and helped him through an illness.

When Tiridates later became king of Armenia he tortured Gregory, by then bishop of Armenia, horribly, and had him thrown into a pit of snakes and other creeping things; Gregory came to no harm and lived there fifteen years on bread a widow brought him in secret. Then God let Tiridates go out of his mind, and through Gregory’s prayers he was healed, repented, and was baptized with his whole nation. The synaxarion dates Gregory 257–331, and says his head and left hand are at the Great Lavra on Athos, his right hand at the Armenian patriarchate of Etchmiadzin, and a fragment of his relics at the monastery of the Panagia Spiliani on Nisyros. The Greek calendar keeps him with two apolytikia, one of them the hieromartyr’s common «Καὶ τρόπων μέτοχος», and a kontakion.

The Romanian life, kept with a troparion and kontakion, tells it at length and differently in places. Anak, a Parthian lord, fled to Armenia pretending to escape Artasir, won the confidence of Cursar and cut him down at Mount Ararat, and the dying king ordered Anak’s whole house destroyed; kinsmen hid two infant sons, and Gregory was brought up in Roman lands, taught the faith at Caesarea, married, and had two sons, Ortan, who became a priest, and Arostan, a hermit, before his wife died. He served Tiridates faithfully in the Roman army to repay his father’s crime, and when Tiridates, having beaten the king of the Goths in single combat for the emperor, was set on his father’s throne (287–330), Gregory went with him to Armenia.

Refusing to sacrifice to Artemis, he was hung head down with a block of salt on his neck, smoked, beaten, shod with nailed boots, scraped, drenched with molten lead, and then, when someone told the king he was Anak’s son, thrown bound into a pit at Artaxata full of mud, snakes and scorpions, where he lived unharmed for fourteen years. In those years the virgins who fled Diocletian — [Rhipsime](/saints/rhipsime), their abbess [Gaiane](/saints/gaiane-companion-of-rhipsime) and the others with them, thirty-three by this life’s count where the Greek calendar keeps [thirty-two](/saints/thirty-two-virgin-martyrs) — were killed at Ararat, and Tiridates and his household were struck with madness and took on the likeness of boars.

The king’s sister was told in a dream that Tiridates would not be healed unless Gregory was brought out of the pit. He was found alive, and asked first for the virgins’ bodies, which had lain unburied nine days; he buried them, taught the king and his men, had a church built and laid the martyrs in it, and the king prayed before them and was healed. All Armenia turned to Christ, and Gregory was consecrated bishop at Caesarea by the archbishop Leontius and came back to baptize the king, his court and his people, ordaining priests, founding schools and monasteries, and bringing Persians, Assyrians and Medes to the faith as well. At the end he withdrew into the desert and died there, and his son Arostan, consecrated in his place, sat at the Council of Nicaea among its three hundred and eighteen fathers.
