# Peter the Aleut

He was born around 1800 on Kodiak Island in Alaska, named Cungagnaq by his parents and Peter at his baptism by the Russian monks of the Alaskan mission. At fourteen he joined a party hunting otter and seal, and near California their ship was seized by Spanish sailors; the fourteen Aleuts were taken to San Francisco and imprisoned. A priest came to convert the captives to Roman Catholicism, and finding them immovable had them pricked with swords and separated.

The next day he took Peter and Kychaly, and when Peter would not renounce the Orthodox faith, ordered the fingers cut from his hands, one by one, then the toes; then the hands and feet themselves. Feeling his life ending, Peter prayed for the strength to endure to the end. Pressed once more to renounce and refusing, he was disembowelled, and so received the martyr's crown - the first native American among the choirs of the martyrs. As the rest were being readied for the same, an order arrived for their release. His story is told in the life of St Herman of Alaska - "a holy new martyr Peter has suffered," Herman said, crossing himself - and the Orthodox Church in America glorified him in 1980, with his feast on 24 September, the day Saints Herman and his companions had first landed in America.

The Greek calendar keeps him on 13 December, together with [Juvenal](/saints/juvenal-of-alaska) and all the saints of Alaska, and saint.gr tells his martyrdom at second hand and from a different mouth. Having said that it has no details of these saints' lives, it prints for Peter alone a passage from the volume on [Herman of Alaska](/saints/herman-of-alaska) in the series «Φιλοκαλία των Ρώσων Νηπτικών», as Petros Botsis translated and edited it: Yanovsky telling Herman himself how the Spaniards had taken fourteen Aleuts captive in California.

In that telling the Jesuits pressed the captives to accept the Catholic faith, and they would not have it on any terms: we are Christians, they answered. The Jesuits countered that they were heretics and schismatics, and that if they did not give in they would be tortured to death. They were put in prison two by two, and in the evening the Jesuits came in with lamps and lit candles and began again. We are Christians, the Aleuts answered; we do not change our faith.

Then they began to torture one of them before the others' eyes, breaking one joint of his feet and then another and then the joints of his fingers one after another, and afterwards cutting his feet and hands to pieces. The blood ran, but the martyr endured and kept repeating the one answer: I am a Christian. In the end he died of the torture and the loss of blood. The next day the Jesuits threatened to torture his friend the same way; but that same night an order came from Monterey to bring all the Aleut captives there, and so the next day all of them left except the one who had been killed.

The man who told Yanovsky this was the friend of the one who was martyred. When Yanovsky had finished the account, the page says, Father Herman asked him what the Aleut's name had been. Peter, he answered, but I do not remember his surname. The elder stood up, stood before the icon, reverently made the sign of the Cross and said: Holy new martyr Peter, intercede for us.
