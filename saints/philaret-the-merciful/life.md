# Philaret the Merciful

Philaret lived in a village called Amnia in the parts of Paphlagonia, of good family out of Galatia; his father was named George and his mother Anna, and from them he learned in childhood the good faith and the fear of the Lord. Grown to manhood, he took an honourable wife of good family and rich, whose name was Theozva, and she brought much property with her out of her parents' house; and he had three children, a first-born son named John and two daughters, Hypatia and Evanthia. God blessed him as he once blessed the righteous Job, with many herds, fields that bore well, barns full and a great household of servants, so that in those parts he was reckoned among the notable men.

Having so much, and seeing others in bitter want, he bent to compassion and said within himself: did I receive such goods from the Lord's hand to feed myself alone and spend them on the pleasure of my belly? Shall I not divide them among the poor, the widows, the orphans, the ruined and the strangers, whom the Lord at his dread judgment will not be ashamed to call his brethren? What good will this property be to me on the day of judgment, which is without mercy for those who have shown no mercy? Better to give it back to God through the hands of the poor, and God will not forsake me, nor my wife, nor my children.

So he became merciful to the poor as a father to his own sons: whoever needed anything of him — an ox, a horse, an ass, clothing, food, anything at all — he gave it. A ploughman's ox fell dead in the field, and Philaret unyoked one of his own and gave it to him; and when the man said it was not good to part an ox from its fellow, he gave him the other too. When a poor soldier's horse died the day before he was to ride out, he gave him his own. His wife reproached him, saying he had done it for himself, so as never to carry the pack on his own shoulders again; and he answered that if they were to receive a hundredfold for one ox, there was no cause to grieve for the ox he had given for God's sake to the man who needed it.

In those days Irene, the empress who loved Christ, held the sceptre of the Greek empire together with her son Constantine; and since he was now of age, men of standing and judgment were sent through the provinces to find a maiden fair, well-mannered and of honourable family, worthy to be joined in marriage to the emperor. They came to Amnia, and were told that in that great house lived only an old man poorer than all men, and they did not believe it.

Philaret received them gladly and set a table in the upper rooms, and the emperor's men wondered to find in so poor a man such fair chambers and a table of ivory; and when his grandchildren came in, carrying the dishes, they wondered at their bearing and their order and their service, and said that among these maidens they would choose the one most fair and most worthy of an imperial marriage. The one who was tallest of stature was named Maria; and the emperor's envoys were filled with joy and took the maiden with her father and mother, her grandfather and all her kindred to Constantinople.

He was ninety years old when he died. He gave the abbess of a convent a quantity of gold for the common needs of her house and asked of the sisters a new grave for himself, telling them to know but tell nobody that in a few days he would leave this life; and at his end, saying the Lord's Prayer, at the words "Thy will be done" he lifted his hands, stretched out his feet on the bed and gave his soul to the Lord, his face cheerful and his look beautiful. A kinsman of his swore to the vision he had seen of Philaret standing beyond a dreadful river and stretching out his hand to help him across, which is told so that we may know what mercy those who show mercy to the poor for God's sake are granted from God.

After her husband was buried, the blessed Theozva went from Constantinople to her estate in the land of Paphlagonia; and the great wealth she had received from the emperor and the empress she spent on the building and renewing of churches that the godless Persians had long before burnt, giving them holy vessels for the service, vestments and every adornment, and building there a monastery and a house for the receiving of strangers and the rest of the poor and the sick. Then she returned to Constantinople, to the empress Maria, her own kinswoman, and lived out the rest of her days there in a manner pleasing to God; and she was laid in the grave beside her honoured husband.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr gives the household. He was a pattern of every virtue and particularly of good works, and he lived in the years of Constantine and of his mother Irene the Athenian, which it dates 780 to 797. He was born in the village of Amnia of Gangra, in Paphlagonia, of devout parents, George and Anna; he married Theosebo and had three children, a son John and two daughters, the first called Hypatia and the second Evanthia. He was a farmer, and out of his income he dealt out alms to the poor lavishly: he found a man hungry and filled him, naked and clothed him, a widow or an orphan and helped and comforted them.

But God allowed him to come at last to great poverty. His neighbours seized his fields and his living was scattered, and he bore it all without ever grieving or blaspheming or growing indignant. At the end there were left him only his bees, two hundred and fifty hives, strong and productive; and when a poor man came to him and he had nothing else to give, he would take him to the hives, harvest one, and give the honey to the poor man to fill himself — and in that way, in season or out of it, he did away with all his bees.

Then God, who had seen his unrivalled faith, so arranged it by his providence that Constantine, the son of the empress Irene, took the saint's granddaughter Maria to wife, because she was very beautiful in soul and in body. Saint [Tarasius](/saints/tarasius-of-constantinople), then patriarch of Constantinople, performed the marriage in November of the year 788, and the emperor honoured Philaret with the dignity of consul; so that he became the owner of much wealth, which he dealt out to the poor more abundantly still.

A little before he died he called his relatives and said: my children, never forget hospitality, do not desire what belongs to others, never be absent from the services and liturgies of the Church, and in general live as I have lived. And having said that, he breathed his last with the words «γενηθήτω τὸ θέλημά σου» — your will be done.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 1 December and tells his almsgiving in instances of its own. He gave away one ox and then the other; he gave his last horse to a man summoned with his horse to the war; and when he had given a calf from the last of his cows, and saw the cow bellowing for the parted calf and the calf for the cow, he called the man back and gave him the cow as well. So the aged Philaret was left in an empty house with nothing in it to eat. He was not frightened of his poverty, but prayed and set his hope in God, who had said that the merciful are blessed, for they shall obtain mercy; and God did not let a righteous man be shamed in his hope.

This book makes Maria, the girl the emperor’s envoys carried off to Constantinople, the daughter of his own daughter Hypatia. In his ninetieth year he called his children, blessed them and taught them to hold to God and to God’s law, and with a foreseeing spirit told each of them how he would live out this age, as the forefather of Israel once did to his sons. Then he lay down in the monastery of Rodolphia and gave up his soul. Before death his face shone like the sun; after it a strange sweet fragrance came from him, and wonders were worked by his relics. His wife Theozva and all his children and grandchildren ended their days in the Lord.

The year this book gives for his repose is 797, where the Greek calendar gives 792; the folder carries both and chooses neither.

The Russian calendar keeps him on the Julian 1 December and sets him in the eighth century, in the village of Amnia of the Paphlagonian country. It gives the household as the other books do — George and Anna his parents, Theozva his wife out of a rich and notable family, and the three children, the son Joat and the daughters Hypatia and Evanthia. One episode is its own: the Ishmaelites, that is the Arabs, fell on Paphlagonia, laid the country waste and plundered his goods, leaving him two oxen, a cow, a few hives and the house; and this last he gave away by degrees to the poor as well.

He bore his wife’s reproaches and his children’s mockery steadily and meekly, telling them he had in hiding-places they did not know of such wealth and such treasure as would be enough for them if they lived a hundred years without labour or care. When the last measure of wheat had been given away an old friend sent him forty measures, and when a warm garment had gone to a beggar his wealth came back to him.

This book has him order his servant to make three boxes and fill them separately with gold, with silver and with copper coin: out of the first the wholly destitute received their alms, out of the second those who had lost their means, and out of the third those who were wheedling money out of him hypocritically. Foreseeing his end in his ninetieth year he went to the Constantinopolitan monastery of Rodolphia, gave away there all he had with him, charged his kindred to love the poor and to hold nothing, and died in peace in 792, which is the Greek year and not the Prologue’s 797.

It adds a wonder after his death, which the folder had not held: as the body was carried to the burial a man with a demon caught hold of the coffin and went with the procession, and at the cemetery the demon threw him on the ground and came out of him. Many other healings were worked at the saint’s tomb. His wife Theozva, it says, laboured after his death at rebuilding the monasteries and churches of Paphlagonia that foreign invasions had thrown down.
