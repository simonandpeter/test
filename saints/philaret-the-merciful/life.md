# Philaret the Merciful

Philaret lived in a village called Amnia in the parts of Paphlagonia, of good family out of Galatia; his father was named George and his mother Anna, and from them he learned in childhood the good faith and the fear of the Lord. Grown to manhood, he took an honourable wife of good family and rich, whose name was Theozva, and she brought much property with her out of her parents' house; and he had three children, a first-born son named John and two daughters, Hypatia and Evanthia. God blessed him as he once blessed the righteous Job, with many herds, fields that bore well, barns full and a great household of servants, so that in those parts he was reckoned among the notable men.

Having so much, and seeing others in bitter want, he bent to compassion and said within himself: did I receive such goods from the Lord's hand to feed myself alone and spend them on the pleasure of my belly? Shall I not divide them among the poor, the widows, the orphans, the ruined and the strangers, whom the Lord at his dread judgment will not be ashamed to call his brethren? What good will this property be to me on the day of judgment, which is without mercy for those who have shown no mercy? Better to give it back to God through the hands of the poor, and God will not forsake me, nor my wife, nor my children.

So he became merciful to the poor as a father to his own sons: whoever needed anything of him — an ox, a horse, an ass, clothing, food, anything at all — he gave it. A ploughman's ox fell dead in the field, and Philaret unyoked one of his own and gave it to him; and when the man said it was not good to part an ox from its fellow, he gave him the other too. When a poor soldier's horse died the day before he was to ride out, he gave him his own. His wife reproached him, saying he had done it for himself, so as never to carry the pack on his own shoulders again; and he answered that if they were to receive a hundredfold for one ox, there was no cause to grieve for the ox he had given for God's sake to the man who needed it.

In those days Irene, the empress who loved Christ, held the sceptre of the Greek empire together with her son Constantine; and since he was now of age, men of standing and judgment were sent through the provinces to find a maiden fair, well-mannered and of honourable family, worthy to be joined in marriage to the emperor. They came to Amnia, and were told that in that great house lived only an old man poorer than all men, and they did not believe it.

Philaret received them gladly and set a table in the upper rooms, and the emperor's men wondered to find in so poor a man such fair chambers and a table of ivory; and when his grandchildren came in, carrying the dishes, they wondered at their bearing and their order and their service, and said that among these maidens they would choose the one most fair and most worthy of an imperial marriage. The one who was tallest of stature was named Maria; and the emperor's envoys were filled with joy and took the maiden with her father and mother, her grandfather and all her kindred to Constantinople.

He was ninety years old when he died. He gave the abbess of a convent a quantity of gold for the common needs of her house and asked of the sisters a new grave for himself, telling them to know but tell nobody that in a few days he would leave this life; and at his end, saying the Lord's Prayer, at the words "Thy will be done" he lifted his hands, stretched out his feet on the bed and gave his soul to the Lord, his face cheerful and his look beautiful. A kinsman of his swore to the vision he had seen of Philaret standing beyond a dreadful river and stretching out his hand to help him across, which is told so that we may know what mercy those who show mercy to the poor for God's sake are granted from God.

After her husband was buried, the blessed Theozva went from Constantinople to her estate in the land of Paphlagonia; and the great wealth she had received from the emperor and the empress she spent on the building and renewing of churches that the godless Persians had long before burnt, giving them holy vessels for the service, vestments and every adornment, and building there a monastery and a house for the receiving of strangers and the rest of the poor and the sick. Then she returned to Constantinople, to the empress Maria, her own kinswoman, and lived out the rest of her days there in a manner pleasing to God; and she was laid in the grave beside her honoured husband.
