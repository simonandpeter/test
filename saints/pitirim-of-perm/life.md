# Pitirim

Gerasim, Pitirim and Jonah were bishops of Great Perm and Ust-Vym, and the
Sretensky calendar’s life takes the three together. Gerasim, third bishop of
the Zyrian people and worthy successor of Stephen, the enlightener of Perm,
was raised to the see after 1416, when only part of the Zyrians had been
converted; with great zeal he cared for his flock, which suffered from the
constant raids of the Novgorodians and the pagan Voguls, going fearlessly
into the Voguls’ camps to persuade them to stop plundering the defenceless
Christians of Perm, and on one such journey died a martyr’s death in 1441,
killed — strangled, tradition says, with his own omophorion — by his Vogul
servant, and was buried in the Annunciation church of Ust-Vym, near Yarensk
on the Vychegda; his memory is 24 January.

His successor was the learned archimandrite Pitirim. In his time too the
Voguls never ceased attacking the peaceful Zyrians of the Perm country, and
Pitirim, like his predecessors, stood up for his flock: in 1447 he went to
Moscow and asked the grand prince in person for help for the Zyrians. He
often visited his flock, scattered over an enormous territory, teaching it
the word of God and coming to its aid in its troubles, and to enlighten the
pagan Voguls made long journeys on which his life was often in danger and he
had to bear every kind of privation; but he spared no strength, enlightening
and teaching people in their houses, in churches and in the open.

By his preaching he converted many Voguls living along the tributaries of
the Pechora, and so roused the terrible anger of their leader Asyka, who
killed the bishop in a field while he was serving a moleben, near Ust-Vym,
on 19 August 1455 — the calendar’s own day-index prints 1456 beside his
name, and both are left standing here. Pitirim composed the life of the
metropolitan Alexis and the canon for the finding of his relics. After him
Jonah took the see of Perm and converted the rest of Great Perm, the pagan
tribes along the Vishera, the Kama, the Chusovaya and other rivers; by his
efforts the idols were destroyed and churches raised in their places, with
schools at them, and experienced pastors were moved from Ust-Vym to the
newly converted to preach and teach in those schools.

Jonah died on 6 June 1470, and his relics rest with those of Gerasim and
Pitirim in the Annunciation church at Ust-Vym, in the Vologda region. Their
common memory acknowledges their apostolic work in the eastern marches of
Russia; the Russian calendar keeps Pitirim on 19 August (Julian) with a
troparion and a kontakion.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the twenty-ninth of January, and not alone: saint.gr's page sets him between two other bishops of Great Perm, [Gerasimus](/saints/gerasimus-of-great-perm), who fell asleep in 1441, and [Jonas](/saints/jonas-of-great-perm), who fell asleep in 1471, and gives Pitirim's own repose as 1455. All three, it says, came from Russia, lived in the fifteenth century, held the see of Great Perm, and fell asleep in holiness and in peace; and it puts the relics of all three in the church of the Annunciation of the Theotokos at Ust-Vym in the region of Vologda. The Greek page says nothing of the martyrdom the Russian calendar records for him, and prints for him no apolytikion and no kontakion.
