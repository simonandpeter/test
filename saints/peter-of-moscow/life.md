# Peter

The Greek calendar keeps 24 August for the translation of his relics and refers his life to 21 December, where its own entry has no details; what follows is the Orthodox Church in America’s note for the day.

Peter, metropolitan of Moscow and all Russia, died on 21 December 1326. His relics were first translated on 1 July 1472, and a feast established; the second translation followed the consecration of the rebuilt Dormition cathedral on 24 August 1479, and this date replaced the earlier one. A feast of the appearance of his relics (4 August) recalls his appearance to the Tsaritsa Anastasia, wife of Ivan the Terrible, when he would let no one open his grave and commanded that it be sealed. Three of his epistles survive, all to priests: to pursue their service worthily and tend their spiritual children with zeal, to be true pastors and not hirelings, and — with the laity — to keep the commandments of Christ. His contemporaries compared him with Basil the Great, Gregory the Theologian and John Chrysostom; his principal labour was for a united Russian state with Moscow at its head.

The Russian calendar keeps on 24 August (Julian) the translation of his relics in 1479, with troparia and kontakia — today dawns the all-honoured feast of the translation of your honoured relics, hierarch Peter, one of them begins — and the same day the Petrovskaya icon of the Mother of God, so called because Peter painted it while still abbot of the Ratsky monastery in Volhynia. Its life of the Moscow hierarchs, Peter, Alexis and Jonah, notes that their joint feast was set by Patriarch Job on 5 October 1596, with Philip joined to them in 1875.

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 21 December, the day of his repose, and prints a full life of him. He was born of right-believing Christian parents in the country of Volhynia; his mother, still carrying him, dreamed that she held a lamb between whose horns grew a tree full of leaves, flowers and fruit, with candles shining among its branches. He learned slowly until a man in bishop's vestments touched his tongue in a dream, after which he took in the whole of Scripture quickly. At twenty he became a monk, carried wood and water and washed the brothers' clothes, was ordained deacon and priest, and learned to paint icons.

With his elder's blessing he withdrew to the river Rata, built a church there and gathered many brothers. When the hierarch Maximus came through that country, Peter gave him an icon of the Theotokos he had painted himself. After Maximus died an abbot named Gerontius seized his vestments, staff and that icon and sailed for Constantinople to have himself made metropolitan of Russia; contrary winds held him up, while Peter — pressed by the prince of Volhynia, the nobles and the synod — had a calm crossing. The icon appeared to Gerontius and told him he laboured in vain, for the man who had painted her would be raised to the throne. Patriarch Athanasius received Peter, the church filled with fragrance, and the synod found him worthy.

He counselled the prince to build a stone church in the name of the Theotokos, promising that his city would be glorified, that hierarchs would dwell in it, and that his own bones would be laid there. He dug his own grave beside the altar, and being told of his death by revelation served the Liturgy, prayed for the emperors and princes and for his spiritual son the prince John and for all the Christians of the Russian land, gave the city's governor Protasius his purse for the finishing of the church, and died with the prayer of Vespers still in his mouth. A man who doubted him in the funeral crowd saw him blessing the people from the bier. Miracles followed at the tomb: a young man's withered hands, a hunchback, a deaf man, a man blind for many years.
