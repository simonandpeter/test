# Tikhon of Zadonsk

Tikhon was born in 1724 in the village of Korotsk in the Novgorod diocese, Timothy, son of the sexton Savely Kirillov — the surname Sokolov was given him later at the Novgorod seminary. After his father’s early death the family lived in such want that his mother nearly gave him away to a neighbour, a coachman, for there was nothing to feed the children; on black bread, and little of it, the boy hired himself to rich market-gardeners to dig their beds. At thirteen he was sent to the diocesan school at the archbishop’s house in Novgorod, and in 1740 was taken at state expense into the new seminary, where he did so well that on finishing in 1754 he was kept on to teach — Greek, then rhetoric and philosophy.

He was tonsured Tikhon in 1758 and made prefect; in 1759 he went to Tver as archimandrite of the Zheltikov monastery, rector of the Tver seminary and superior of the Otroch monastery; and on 13 May 1761 he was consecrated bishop of Keksholm and Ladoga, vicar of Novgorod — providentially, his life says, for at the drawing of lots in Petersburg at Pascha his name came out three times of eight, and the same day the bishop of Tver, without meaning to, commemorated him as bishop at the Cherubic hymn.

In 1763 he was moved to the see of Voronezh, which he governed four and a half years, instructing it by his life, by countless pastoral letters and by books: on the seven mysteries, on the priestly office, on repentance, on marriage; he required every priest to own and read the New Testament daily, called the clergy in a circular letter to reverence in the sacraments and to brotherly love, uprooted the old pagan festival of Yarilo, opened a mission to the Old Believers in the Don Cossack lands, and in 1765 turned the Slavonic-Latin school into a seminary with teachers from Kyiv and Kharkiv.

Worn out, often sleepless, he gave up the diocese for his health in 1767 and withdrew to the Tolshevo monastery, and in 1769 to the monastery of the Mother of God at Zadonsk, where he became a great teacher of the Christian life: he wrote the Rules of monastic life and the Instruction to those turned from the vain world, went to church almost daily, sang and read on the kliros and later, out of humility, only stood in the altar; he knew the Psalter by heart and read or sang it on the road, and his cell reading was the lives of the saints and the Fathers.

He meant once to go back to the Novgorod diocese, and the elder Aaron said, what are you raving about — the Mother of God does not let him leave; then I will not go, Tikhon said, and tore up the petition. The fruit of his life on retirement were his books, A Spiritual Treasure Gathered from the World (1770) and On True Christianity (1776); he lived in the simplest surroundings, and died at Zadonsk in 1783, where his relics lie. He was canonised in 1861 — first, the Prologue says, by the people, then by the Church — and by his wisdom, holiness and ascetic life, it adds, can be set beside the great Fathers of old.

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 13 august, and the life it prints adds the night that turned him. While he was still a teacher at the Novgorod seminary he had the sense of God's unceasing presence and loved to read or think through whole nights; during one such vigil, as he was thinking on eternal blessedness, heaven opened of a sudden and an indescribable light shone before him, and it kindled in his heart a burning desire for the monastic life and for prayer. It adds his end as well: after a vision he was struck with paralysis down his left side and kept to his bed, and three days before he died he called those near him and his benefactors, showed them the cross and said, I shall pray to the Lord for all of you. Those were his last words. In his testament he wrote, as John Chrysostom did, Glory to God for all things.
