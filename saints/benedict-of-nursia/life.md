# Benedict of Nursia

Benedict rose like the morning star out of Nursia, a city of the Romans. He was devout and right-believing and adorned with the gift of God, shining from childhood in innocence of heart and in temperance of body; while still young he hated the heat and the impulse of the flesh, the indulgences and the desires of the world, and reckoned this world with its flower to be a dry tree. His parents put him to school; but seeing that many went after their own appetites, he withdrew like a wise man and went no more, lest he fall into that same soul-destroying pit.

So, leaving school, his father's house and the great wealth he had inherited — for his parents had died — and wishing to please God alone, he loved the monastic habit and became a monk. The writer of the Romanian life says plainly that he did not see the saint's deeds with his own eyes but heard them from four of his disciples: from Constantine, a most reverent man who succeeded him; from Valentine, who governed the holy house in the Lateran for many years; from Simplicius, who shepherded third after Synodius; and from Honoratus, who lives today in the cell where the venerable father used to sit.

He went to live in the wilderness, and nobody followed him but the woman who had brought him up, who loved him greatly. At a place called Effide many reverent men, drawn by his spiritual love, came and stayed with him at the church of Saint Peter. There his first wonder was worked: the woman had borrowed a shallow tray from a neighbour to clean some wheat, and the devil threw it down and broke it in two; and when she wept over it, Benedict took the pieces, fell to prayer with tears, and rose to find the vessel whole. The people of that place hung it at the church gate for all to see.

Loving God and hating glory, he gave himself to greater labours, thinking it better to rejoice in toil than in the praises of men; and he slipped away from his nurse and went to desert places, coming to a place called the Lake, forty miles from Rome, where many waters spring and make a river. A monk named Romanus, from a monastery near by, saw him, learned where he had gone and what he intended, told nobody, helped him with what he needed, clothed him in the holy habit of the monks, and served his bodily wants as he could.

In that cave, shining with divine virtues and with the signs God worked through him, he drew many to that place to be shepherded by the grace given him; and there, by the power of Christ, he set up twelve monasteries, appointing twenty monks to each, and keeping a few with him under his own teaching. Men of good family and learning began to come from Rome with their sons to be tonsured; among them came Euthicius and Maurus, and the patrician Tertullus gave his son Placidus. Maurus, the younger, grew in good habits and began to work as his teacher did, and Placidus reined in his youth with the labours of the desert.

Moving to other places, the venerable man changed his dwelling but the enemy did not let him be, and he suffered greater and heavier wars from the devil. Leaving his first place he went into the land of Campania, to the borders of the city called Cassino, where on a high mountain he found a very old Greek temple in which the god Apollo was honoured, with a crowd of altars and trees about it, where the unbelieving still went up to sacrifice. Benedict broke the idol of Apollo, threw down the altars and burned the trees; in the middle of the temple he built a church of Saint Martin, and where Apollo's altar had stood a church of John the Baptist; and the people who lived thereabouts, taught by him, turned to God and all became Christians.

The life is very long, and tells many wonders and prophecies: how Totila, king of the Goths, hearing of his gift of prophecy, sent one of his guardsmen named Rigo dressed in the royal clothes to test him; and how his sister Scholastica, hallowed to God from her youth, used to come once a year to a house near the monastery gate, where they would spend the day in holy words and in the stories of the God-inspired Scriptures.

Besides the wonders by which he was glorified in this world, the life says, this man of God was glorified and shone not a little by the teaching of his word: for he set out the canons and rules of monks with a chosen clearness and in bright words, and whoever wishes to know his manners and his life exactly will understand them by reading the composition of his rule, that is, his typikon; for as he lived, so he taught, and as he lived he taught others to live.

In the year he was to depart he told his disciples, those with him and those far away, the day of his holy end; and he had his tomb opened six days before. A terrible fever came on him, and on the sixth day he was carried into the church, where he received the Master's Body and Blood. Standing among his disciples and held up by them, being wholly weak, he turned to the east, raised his hands to heaven and prayed; and with the prayer his hallowed and most pure soul went up to heaven and was given to the Lord.

That same day two of the brothers, one keeping silence in his cell and one living far off, saw the same revelation: a road from the saint's cell reaching up to heaven, spread all over with silks, and eastward countless lamps burning, ten thousands of them in rank upon rank; and a man clothed in white with a shining face standing above, who asked them whether they knew whose road it was that they looked at in wonder, and said, “This is the way by which Benedict, the beloved of God, goes up to heaven.” So they understood the saint's end. His holy body was buried in the church of John the Baptist which he had built on the place of Apollo's altar.

In the cave by the stream where the venerable man had lived before, those who draw near in faith have a great gift of wonders to this day. The life ends with one of them: a woman went mad and wandered by day and night through mountains, ravines and fields, sleeping wherever sleep overtook her; and one day, wandering here and there in the wilderness, she came without knowing it, being led by God, into the venerable man's cave, and stayed the night there. When day came she went out so sound that all who saw her said she had never been mad, and she stayed well to the end of her life. doxologia gives no year anywhere in the life.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and puts years to the life. saint.gr heads him 481 to 543 and has him born at Nursia in Italy of noble and wealthy parents who were devoted children of the Church and brought him up, in the words of the Apostle which the page quotes, in the nurture and admonition of the Lord. When he was grown he withdrew to a ravine in the Apennine mountains and gave himself to prayer and study; and the holiness of that life brought him such fame among the mountain people round about that in the space of twenty years a hundred and sixty-four monks had come to him.

In the year 540, the page says, he founded a monastery on mount Cassino, and he was the founder of the monastic order of the Benedictines, which exists in the western Church to this day. He died peacefully in 543, at the age of sixty-two. The page keeps three verses for him, one of which counts the day — «Οὔλυμπον Βενέδικτος ἔβη δεκάτῃ γε τετάρτῃ», on the fourteenth Benedict went up to heaven — and prints an apolytikion and a kontakion, which are given here with the folder's Romanian tropar.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same menologion day and tells the life at length. It has him born in the region of Nursia in Italy in 480, of rich and notable parents — a year earlier than the one saint.gr gives, and the folder keeps saint.gr's. He did not stay long at school, seeing for himself that book-learning might cost him the great reason of his soul, and came out of it an unlearned sage and a reasonable ignorant man. He fled to a monastery, where a monk named Romanus tonsured him, and then withdrew to a steep mountain and stayed over three years in a cave at great labour over his soul, while Romanus brought bread and lowered it to him on a rope down the crag.

When word of him spread through the country round about he left that cave, to escape glory from men. He was very hard on himself: when the unclean demon of bodily lust fell upon him he stripped himself naked and rolled in nettles and thorns until he had driven off every thought of a woman. God gave him many gifts of the Spirit — he saw what was hidden, healed, drove out evil spirits, raised the dead, and appeared to others both waking and at a distance in sleep. Once he saw that a cup of wine set before him was poisoned; he made the sign of the cross over it and the cup burst. He founded twelve monasteries, each with twelve monks in it at the beginning.

Six days before his death he had his grave, prepared beforehand, opened, for he saw that his end was near. He gathered all the monks, counselled them, and gave up his spirit to the Lord whom he had served faithfully in poverty and purity. His own sister Scholastica lived in a convent of women and, looking to her brother, laboured much herself and came to great spiritual perfection. When Benedict breathed out his soul, two monks — one on the road, the other at prayer in a distant cell — saw the same vision at the same moment: a road from earth to heaven spread with costly cloths and lit along its sides by rows of men, and at the top of it a man of indescribable beauty and light, who told them the road was prepared for Benedict, the beloved of God. He reposed in peace in 543.
