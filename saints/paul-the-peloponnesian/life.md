# Paul of the Peloponnese

saint.gr says he was born in the village of Sopoto in the district of Kalavryta and brought up as a Christian by parents who were poor but virtuous Orthodox people. His first name was Panagiotis. While still young he came to Patras, learned the sandal-maker's trade, and stayed there working honestly for fourteen years; then he left Patras for Kalavryta, where he rented a workshop for his trade.

The owners of the workshop demanded more rent than they had agreed and had him shut in prison, and there, under pressure and in his anger, he said the words that undid him: «Τούρκος να γίνω αν δώσω περισσότερα» — may I become a Turk if I give more. In the end he paid what they asked, and when he came out he left Kalavryta for Tripoli, where he amused himself in the outskirts with two companions, saying that he was a Turk.

But his conscience convicted him, and he went away to the Holy Mountain. At the Lavra of Saint Athanasius he found a wise Peloponnesian elder named Timothy, confessed to him and was comforted, and afterwards was made a monk with the name Paul. With his elder he moved to a Russian coenobium on the Mountain and stayed three years, and there the longing for martyrdom was kindled in him. At twenty-five he went to the skete of Saint Anna and submitted to the hieromonk Ananias, to whom he confessed that longing; he was tested forty days, took the fathers' blessing and set out.

He came to the monastery of the Great Cave and struggled there forty whole days with fasting and prayer, then went by Kalavryta to Tripoli. Hearing that a cousin of his who had denied the faith was at Nauplion, he went there to set him right, took him along as a travelling companion, returned to Tripoli and presented himself to the mufti of the city, from whom he got a written order to appear before the judge on the day of a great gathering of Christian notables and bishops.

At that gathering he preached the Godhead of Christ before them all and reproached the Muslim religion in the sharpest terms. The judge, faced with a mind that would not be moved, sentenced him to be burned alive; but some of the Turks said the Christians might take the ashes and the remains of the martyr, so he changed the sentence, and they beheaded him at Tripoli on 22 May 1818, aged twenty-eight. His body was thrown into the cesspit of a Turkish governor's house, and the Christians took it away secretly, cleansed it at the monastery of Saint Nicholas of Varsoi and buried it. His martyrdom was written down by the hieromonk Jacob Vertsagias of Zakynthos, of the Russian coenobium on Athos.
