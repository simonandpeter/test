# The Hundred Thousand Martyrs of Tbilisi

In 1227 the sultan Jalal al-Din of Khwarazm and his army attacked Georgia. On the first day of fighting the Georgian army drove the invaders back as they came up to Tbilisi; but that night a group of Persians living in the city secretly opened the gates and called the enemy army in. A river of blood ran through the city: children were abused, mothers violated and stabbed, and the river and the streets filled with the dead.

Jalal al-Din, now master of the whole city, ordered the dome of the Sioni church pulled down and his own tent set in its place, and climbed up there to look with pleasure on the ruins of Tbilisi. But his victory was not complete, as he thought, unless he changed the faith of all his Christian captives. So he had the icons of the Lord and of the Mother of God carried out of the church and laid in the middle of a bridge joining the banks of the river Kura, set soldiers with drawn swords at both ends of it, and put a man with a loud voice to announce his decision to the prisoners gathered there.

Their great emperor commanded them to renounce their Christ and receive his faith; to prove the conversion they must spit on these icons and trample them, and they would be freed at once and given rich gifts, and if they would not they would be cut down by the sharp swords in the soldiers' hands and thrown into the river. He was sure they would obey rather than lose their lives, and he was wrong: he did not know that the faith of the Georgian people in Christ was so great that not even death could overcome it.

Hearing the herald, the hundred thousand Christian prisoners — men, women, old and young, bishops, priests and monks — with one mind and one voice resolved to offer their bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God. They rejoiced both to confess their faith before the barbarians, sealing the confession with their own blood, and to escape quickly from passing torments and gain by God's mercy the eternal goods of the heavenly kingdom.

Their answer to the shah was plain. By the grace of the Lord they were Christians; this holy faith they had learned and it had been handed down to them by their forefathers; they confessed before him that they believed in the Holy Trinity. Since they had transgressed his commandments and set his law at nothing, God had justly allowed the destruction of their country and their enslavement by unbelieving enemies. But whatever they suffered they would not deny their true God nor dishonour his holy icons; for the cleansing of the sins they had done in this earthly life they were ready to die as martyrs.

One young man, in fear, spat beside the icons rather than on them, when he was told that this would be enough to save his life; and the Persians at once took him aside, applauding and boasting, and began to embrace him and praise and congratulate him. Then, as if waking out of a faintness, he said: My God, what have I done? Can I not confess with my brothers? Can I deny you and follow the accursed Mohammed, and be the only one to escape death in so shameful a way? No — I will not be parted from the other Christians. I am coming to you, Lord.

With a sudden movement he broke out of the Persians' hands, ran to the holy icons, knelt before them and cried out, weeping bitterly, "Queen of heaven, take me, the prodigal son, under your protection." In that moment he gave up his soul into the hands of God. The executioners beheaded his dead body and threw it into the swift waters of the river, as they had done before with the body of an old man.

So not one of the hundred thousand Georgian prisoners denied the Lord: all were slaughtered willingly, like faithful sheep of their good Shepherd. A chronicler of that age wrote that a man could cross from one bank of the river to the other without wetting his feet, walking on the bodies of the holy martyrs, and that the waters of the river stayed red with the blood of the Christians for two weeks.

Jalal al-Din watched the martyrdom of the prisoners from the dome of the cathedral of Saint Sioni where he had pitched his tent; and when the last martyr had been killed, late in the evening, a heavenly light in the form of a pillar came down over the bridge and lit the whole region, while at the same moment a strong earthquake threw down the shah's tent from the top of the church. From that time the bridge has been called the Bridge of the Hundred Thousand Martyrs of Tbilisi, or the Bridge of the Martyrs. doxologia.ro gives this account from the Georgian Paterikon and prints no troparion for them.
