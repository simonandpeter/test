# Herman (Ryashentsev)

Herman, bishop of Vyazniki — Nicholas Stepanovich Ryashentsev in the
world — was born on 10 November 1883 in the city of Tambov, the son of the
merchant of the second guild Stepan Grigorievich Ryashentsev; four days
later, on 14 November, the feast of the apostle Philip and of Gregory
Palamas, before the Nativity fast, the infant was baptised in the Tambov
cathedral of the Nativity of Christ by the archpriest Alexis Petrovsky
and named Nicholas for the wonderworker. Already in early childhood
Nicholas felt the calling of God that decided the path of his life; in
1902, after the classical gymnasium, he entered the Kazan Academy, and in
his third year, on Great Saturday 1905, at twenty-one, was tonsured
Herman for Herman of Kazan. In 1906 he was ordained and finished the
Academy as a candidate of theology with a thesis on the moral views of
Symeon the New Theologian; on 17 August that year he was appointed to the
Pskov seminary to teach Holy Scripture, on 1 December 1907 became its
inspector, an honourable and troublesome post, and on 28 June 1912, as
archimandrite, rector of the Bethany seminary. On 27 September 1919, the
Exaltation of the Cross, he was consecrated bishop of Volokolamsk, vicar
of Moscow, and settled in the ancient Joseph-Volokolamsk monastery — but
governed the vicariate very briefly: on 10 December 1920 the Volokolamsk
district committee of the party resolved that Bishop Herman was an active
force demoralising all the clergy of the Volokolamsk, Ruza and Mozhaisk
districts round Patriarch Tikhon, and asked the Cheka to move him to a
concentration camp “until the full victory of the working people”, the
local investigator of deserters adding that with his higher education he
had led the district clergy along a definite path, darkening the people’s
minds to the utmost with his sermons. In the night of 19 February 1921 he
was arrested and put in the Butyrka, where Metropolitan Sergius
(Stragorodsky) was also held, and for a time, until they were put in
separate cells, the two served together in a cell with any prisoners who
wished present; the faithful protested against this outrage on the
Church, faith and conscience of the Orthodox Russian people, wrote that
their bishop had always and everywhere called for obedience to the
authorities and patient endurance of the ruin, and begged unceasingly for
his release. Freed on 22 April 1921 on an undertaking not to leave Moscow
before trial, he lived at the Danilov monastery until the amnesty of the
revolution’s fourth anniversary closed the case and ordered him back to
Volokolamsk. In July 1922 he was arrested again at his Moscow flat in the
first Krestovsky lane, spent some months in the Butyrka and was exiled by
administrative order to the Tobolsk district for three years, sent under
guard in July 1923; the exile ended officially on 12 July 1925, and in
August he sailed to Tobolsk and returned to Moscow — for four months of
freedom, in which he saw his family and friends, until the search and
arrest in the night of 30 November 1925, the inner prison of the OGPU and
the Butyrka, and on 21 May a sentence of three years’ exile, served from
September at Turtkul in Karakalpakia. On 14 January 1928 he was allowed to
leave and came back to Moscow, and on 26 June was appointed to Vyazniki,
where he held the see four months: arrested at Vyazniki on 14 December
1928 and questioned at Vladimir the next day, he was sentenced on 17 May
1929, as the “ideological inspirer of the grouping” who had sufficiently
shown his true reactionary face, to three years in the camps. At the
beginning of 1930 he reached Solovki and lived in the hut in the forest
between the sea and the lake where Archbishop Hilarion had lived until
December 1929, and within a month caught typhus, which in two and a half
months left him an invalid; at the end of 1930 he was moved to the
mainland with the old, the sick and the crippled, and lived as an exile in
the north, at times in the harshest conditions and without a roof, in more
than twelve places, until February 1933. Allowed on 15 January 1933, the
feast of Seraphim, to leave, he chose Arzamas from the towns offered, saw
Metropolitan Sergius in Moscow on the way but, not being wholly free, got
no new appointment, and had no illusions that Arzamas was more than a
short respite — many, very many of my brethren, he wrote, and especially
where the Lord led me out from, have already gone to their eternal rest.
He met his arrest in early March 1934 calmly: the charge of 4 March said
that with Bishop Serapion he had actively fought to raise the authority of
religion and to unite the clergy of the Arzamas bishopric; ten others were
arrested, neither bishop admitted guilt, and the timid false testimony of
a few broken co-accused sufficed — on 15 April Serapion got five years,
Herman and four others three years’ exile in the Northern Region, five
more the camps. In May 1934 he reached the station of Oparino on the
Northern Kotlas line, and on 10 August was ordered to Syktyvkar, to the
suburban village of Kochpon, where he lived until his last arrest, as
choir-master of the Kazan church, whose singers were mostly exiled monks —
joined at Christmas 1937 by the priest Paul Florensky, on his way back from
imprisonment. His old openness and cheerfulness never left him whatever
the trials, and the light of Christ shone from his letters as the warmth
of the Russian stove and the joy of the coming feast filled the village
hut. His exile was to end on 2 March 1937, and he was talking over with
friends where to settle for a new respite when, on 24 February 1937, he
was arrested for the fifth and last time, with twelve others, on a
denunciation, under article 58-10. Among more than twenty letters seized
was one he had not managed to destroy, from Archbishop Nicholas
(Klementyev) of Veliky Ustyug in October 1935, answering a request for a
blessing on a secret monastic tonsure — the words “secret tonsure” alone
suggested some secret subversion to the Bolsheviks — though the pretext
mattered little beside the power’s settled aim of destroying dissent. The
indictment ran to four counts: organiser and leader of a
counter-revolutionary group of churchmen of fascist stamp called the
“Sacred Company”; counter-revolutionary pronouncements at its illegal
gatherings; counter-revolutionary agitation among the people and defence
of the Trotskyite enemies of the people; and the organising of material
help for the group’s members and the exiled clergy. He admitted guilt, in
the investigator’s phrase, “in part” — he did not deny helping exiles as
he could — and none of the other charges can be counted admitted from the
records, though the investigators tried to read some answers as
confessions: questioned from the day after his arrest about his
acquaintances and correspondence he answered calmly, stressing the
harmless character of his ties — clergy, exiles, plain believers who tried
to help — and firmly denied ever giving anyone counter-revolutionary
tasks; all they got was an admission of anti-Soviet sentiments, his own
and his friends’, voiced in talk about the party’s policy on religion and
the clergy, and after a pause of some days, strengthened in spirit, he
rejected every charge with unshakable firmness. The last interrogation,
on 29 May, was hardly needed — the indictment had been ready since the
24th. The troika of the Komi NKVD gave its sentence on 13 September, the
same for all: to be shot; and on 15 September 1937 — 2 September of the
old style, the day the Russian calendar keeps him — the hieromartyr
Herman, bishop of Vyazniki, and his fellow-prisoners were shot near
Syktyvkar, where an airfield now stands. On 2 September 2001 the synodal
commission for canonisation, examining the material submitted by the St
Tikhon Orthodox Theological Institute, found no obstacle to his
glorification, and on 6 October the Holy Synod entered his name in the
synaxis of the new martyrs and confessors of Russia; the Russian calendar
keeps him also in the synaxes of the saints of Komi, Vladimir, Solovki,
Moscow and Arkhangelsk.
