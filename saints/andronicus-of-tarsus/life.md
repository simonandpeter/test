# Andronicus of Tarsus

In the reign of Diocletian and Maximian, when Numerius Maximus the proconsul judged at Tarsus in Cilicia, three Christians were seized in the city of Pompeiopolis — [Tarachus](/saints/tarachus-of-tarsus), [Probus](/saints/probus-of-tarsus) and Andronicus — and brought before him. The third to be brought in answered the question of his name with «Sunt creștin»; told that the two before him had got no good of that name and that he should answer otherwise, he said that his common name among men was Andronic.

He was of honourable stock, he said, the son of one of the foremost citizens of Ephesus. Maximus bade him spare himself and listen as to a father, and honour the gods, who were their fathers. Andronicus answered that he had named them well, for their father was Satan and they were sons of the devil and did his will. Threatened with terrible torments, he said he was not so foolish as to fall short of those who had suffered before him, and was ready for them all.

The centurion Demetrius told him to obey before his flesh fell from his bones, and he answered that it was better his body should perish than that they should do to his soul what they wished. Another centurion, Anxius, urged him as a father would, and he told the old man he had no wisdom if he counselled a man to bow to stones and sacrifice to devils. His body was wounded and he was pierced between the ribs, and he said his flesh had been strengthened by the blows and that he did not fear the judge’s threats.

At the later hearings bundles of rushes were soaked in scalding oil, laid on his belly and set alight, and he answered that though they burned him wholly they would not overcome him, for the Lord whom he worshipped helped him. When the proconsul spoke slightingly of Christ he told him to be silent, for it was not for him to speak ill of Him. The meat of the sacrifice was forced into his mouth and wine poured in, and he cried, «Doamne, Doamne, vezi nevoia ce rabd» — Lord, Lord, see the distress I endure.

The rest, the page says, was written by three faithful men — Macarie, Felix and Verie — who stood hidden at the games a thousand paces outside the city. When the martyrs were carried in, for their wounds would not let them walk, the crowd turned its face away and wept and began to cry out against Maximus, and many left. The beasts loosed on them would not touch them: a bear that had killed three men that day came up and licked Andronicus’s wounds, and a lioness sent from Antioch by Herod ran about seeking to escape, then came to the martyrs, bent her knees and lay down before Tarachus, and when he pushed her off and provoked her she stayed beside him like a gentle sheep. So, the page says, these holy martyrs made their end.

Maximus left soldiers to guard the bodies, ordering that they be mixed with the bodies of the condemned so that Christians should not know them and steal them. The three men prayed, and at once the earth shook with thunder and lightning and a storm of rain, and when they came near the fire was out and every soldier had fled. The night was very dark, and three lights like stars appeared above the relics; they took them up in secret and followed the lights over the mountain, and laid them in a cave cut in the rock, securing the entrance. Going back into the city they found the guards put to death by Maximus.

The Greek calendar keeps the three of them on the same day, 12 October, and saint.gr adds where each came from and how they answered. Probus was from Pamphylia, Andronicus from Ephesus and Tarachus from Illyria; all three were soldiers, it says, truly most devout and thoroughly grounded in Holy Scripture, and they were arrested by Maxentius the eparch of Tarsus while they were in the desert, in the persecution under Diocletian and under Flavian governor of Cilicia. Brought before him, Andronicus answered last and bravely: use what tortures you like; all my blood may go, but endurance will not be wanting to Andronicus, even were he cut into ten thousand pieces. Struck down by the three answers, the page says, Maxentius had the skin flayed from [Tarachus](/saints/tarachus-of-tarsus)'s head and the eyes of the other two put out, and at last, unable to bear such humbling, beheaded all three.

The Russian calendar keeps the three of them on 12 October too, and gives the year the other two do not: 304, at Tarsus in Cilicia. Its life for the three is four sentences and says nothing of him by himself: Tarachus the old soldier answers for all of them, Probus speaks the one saying it quotes, and he is named only in the heading and in the first sentence. The bodies are cut in pieces and Christians take the remains away in secret and bury them — which is as much as the Russian has to set against the bear that licked his wounds in the Romanian life.
