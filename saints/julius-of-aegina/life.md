# Julius of Aegina

As his synaxarion has it, saint.gr says, Julius was born in the year 330 on Aegina, of well-off and devout parents who brought him up in the teaching and admonition of the Lord. He learned his general letters on Aegina and then studied at Athens, together with [Basil](/saints/basil-the-great), whom the calendar keeps on 1 January, and [Gregory](/saints/gregory-the-theologian), whom it keeps on 25 January.

Having come back to Aegina he resolved, along with the deacon Julian, to imitate Paul the apostle of the nations and to preach Christ. So the two of them took apostolic staves and gave themselves up to the Lord; and the bishop of Athens ordained Julius presbyter. Adorned with the grace of the priesthood he set out with the deacon Julian to preach the Gospel and to baptise many of the gentiles.

At the end of his life he withdrew to Kousion on Lake Orta, where after asceticism and prayer he fell asleep in peace. The page's heading dates his repose to 407 and its own text to 401 at the age of seventy-one; neither sits easily with the birth in 330 that it also gives, and all three readings are set down here as the page has them rather than reconciled. It prints no apolytikion and no kontakion.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 21 June, and it is the first of the three calendars to keep the two of them on one day rather than on two. It calls them brothers by birth, which the Greek page does not: brought up in Christianity from childhood, with a vow to live always in virginity and to serve the church, Julius a presbyter and [Julian](/saints/julian-the-deacon-of-aegina) a deacon. It has them obtain from the emperor it names a charter allowing them to pull down the idols throughout the empire and to build Christian churches — which cannot be squared with the years the Greek page gives either of them, and is written here as this book has it and reconciled with nothing. That emperor is named in the attestation note and not here, his name being a corpus folder’s own, and an emperor who grants a charter being no relation.

Like two apostles, it says, the two brothers turned unbelievers into Christians through the East and the West, and built churches; and they built a hundred churches in their lifetime. They fell asleep in peace in the Lord near Mediolanum — the city that the two lakes of the Greek account stand near. And the Prologue adds one line that belongs to him alone, in the present tense: the people of Mediolanum call on Saint Julius for help against wolves.
