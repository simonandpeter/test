# Ananias of Lacedaemonia

saint.gr prints its fullest 15 April life for this eighteenth-century metropolitan of Sparta. Ananias was born at the beginning of the eighteenth century of noble and well-to-do parents: his father Theophiles was a kinsman of the Lampardaioi, and his mother was the daughter of Syntychos of Vyziki in Gortynia. He studied at the school of Dimitsana and was afterwards made bishop of his own country; in February 1750 he was raised to be metropolitan of Lacedaemonia, and in 1755 he wrote a treatise of his own upon Sparta and its metropolitans.

The page sets him at the head of the affairs of the Morea. He was marked, it says, by his learning, his high knowledge, the steadiness of his churchly character, and his goodness and love of men; to the Greeks he was an object of unexampled reverence, and to the Turks of terror. He built an aqueduct at Dimitsana, settled the yearly dues of the famous school of the monastery of Philosophou, raised a great metropolitan church at Mystras, and had a large building put up for the school of Dimitsana to house the poor scholars who came to it from all over the Peloponnese. Two powder mills were founded there at his care and cost.

In 1762 he was sent to Constantinople to stand before the grand vizier and set out the miseries of the Peloponnese. When Papazolis came into the Mani in 1766 to raise the Peloponnese first, Ananias was among the first called to the famous assembly at Kalamata, and the Peloponnesians' bond, the «συνυποσχετικόν», was drawn up with his blessing; afterwards he came to an understanding with the captains and armatoloi of the Peloponnese and the Mani, and with the notables and hierarchs of the provinces and of the three islands of Hydra, Spetses and Psara. The movement was betrayed, and he was denounced to the Mora-Valesi as being in league with Russia.

Many urged him then to armed resistance, but he answered that he alone should fall, so that the Turks might not learn what was planned. He received the immaculate Mysteries and let the soldiers in at once; and he knelt down and with unbelievable readiness called on the executioner to strike. The blood that leapt from his wounded heart anointed the door-post of the little western door of the metropolis of Mystras. The Turks dragged his body through the streets and left it three days unburied; when they had gone the Christians took it up and buried it with great honour, and the people made a folk-song of his end.
