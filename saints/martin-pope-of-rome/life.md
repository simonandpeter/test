# Martin

Martin, bishop of Rome, was born at Todi in Umbria, in central Italy, and
became Pope at the time when the heresy of the Monothelites was troubling
the Church — into whose nets the Church of Constantinople too had fallen,
the Patriarch Paul II being a champion of Monothelitism together with the
emperor Constans II. Martin, a champion of Orthodoxy, tried by letter and by
clergy sent for the purpose to bring the Patriarch back to the Orthodox
dogma, in vain: Paul, under the emperor’s influence, held to Monothelitism
and exiled Martin’s envoys to various islands, and Constans sent and had
Martin himself seized by guile, brought a prisoner to Constantinople, and
exiled to Cherson, where he died on 16 September 655, having governed his
church six years — and, what matters most, death found him fighting on the
ramparts of Orthodoxy, rightly dividing the word of truth, teaching it
without error.

Two bishops exiled to Cherson with him died there after many hardships. The
synaxarion’s verse has him who rejoiced to eat the Saviour’s flesh rejoicing
to be stripped of his own. The Greek synaxarion keeps him on 13 April, and
some synaxaria repeat his memory on 20 September with [Maximus the
Confessor](/saints/maximus-the-confessor), which is the line this entry
stands under.

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 14 April, and the Lives it prints add the shape of the quarrel and the end of it. Martin took the see of Rome by the spoken choice of all after the death of Pope Theodore, at a time when the emperors at Constantinople still held old Rome; the Monothelite heresy — that there is only one will in Christ our Lord — had grown out of the earlier heresy of Eutyches, was begun by Cyrus of Alexandria and Sergius of Constantinople with the agreement of the emperor Heraclius, and was carried on by the patriarchs Pyrrhus and Paul, at whose urging the emperor Constans wrote the booklet full of it that he called the *Typos*.

Against it the Orthodox confession holds that in our incarnate Lord and God there are two natures, and likewise two wills and two operations, each nature distinct, in one single person of Christ — for Christ God is not in two separated persons, but is known in the duality of natures without confusion. When the notary came to take Martin away to exile, those in the prison wept, and the saint said to them with a glad face: «Nu plângeți, ci mai bine bucurați-vă împreună cu mine, care mă bucur; căci mă duc în surghiun pentru dreapta credință!» — do not weep, but rather rejoice with me, for I am going into exile for the right faith.

He was put aboard ship and sent into exile at Cherson, where he was tormented with hunger, with hardship and with every kind of want, and after two years went to the Lord. His body was buried outside the city of Cherson, in the church of the Most Holy Theotokos that was called of Blachernae, and his grave was glorious, the Romanian Lives say, for the many healings given there to the sick.

saint.gr keeps him on 13 April as well, where this folder's Greek row records the 20 Σεπτεμβρίου on which the same site keeps him with Maximus the Confessor, and that page adds the reason for his exile and a note about the days. It says Martin, bishop of Rome, was born in central Italy, at Todi in Umbria, and became pope at the time when the heresy of the Monothelites was troubling the Church, when the Church of Constantinople too had fallen into its nets, the patriarch Paul II being a champion of Monothelitism together with the emperor Constans II. Martin, a champion of Orthodoxy, tried by a letter of his and by special clerics sent for the purpose to bring the patriarch Paul back to the Orthodox teaching, and in vain: the patriarch, under the emperor's influence, held to Monothelitism and exiled Martin's envoys to various islands. Constans II then sent men who seized Martin himself by a trick, and having brought him captive to Constantinople they exiled him to Cherson, where he died on 16 September 655 after governing his church six years — death finding him, the page says, still fighting on the ramparts of Orthodoxy, rightly dividing the word of truth. Two more bishops were exiled with him to Cherson, and died there after many hardships; the page names neither of them.

About the days it is explicit and hedged: «Η μνήμη του, από ορισμένους Συναξαριστές, επαναλαμβάνεται στις 20 Σεπτεμβρίου» — his memory is repeated, by certain synaxaristes, on 20 September. So the 20 Σεπτεμβρίου this folder's Greek row already holds and this 13 Απριλίου are the same man on two Greek days, and the row was left where it stands.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Romanian calendar's day rather than the Greek one, and tells the whole of his pontificate. He became pope on 5 July 649, it says, just in the time of the furious quarrel of the Orthodox with the Monothelite heretics. The emperor then was Constans II, Heraclius's grandson, and the patriarch of Constantinople was Павле. To restore peace in the Church the emperor himself wrote a little book, the Typos, which pleased the heretics greatly; and Pope Martin called a council of a hundred and five bishops, at which that book of the emperor's was condemned. At the same time he wrote to the patriarch, asking him to hold to the pure Orthodox faith and to counsel the emperor to leave off his heretical subtleties — and that letter angered the patriarch and the emperor both.

The emperor sent a commander, Olympius, to Rome, to bring the pope to Constantinople in chains. The commander did not dare to bind him, but talked a soldier into killing him with a sword in the church; and when the soldier came into the church with the sword hidden, he went blind on the spot. So by God's providence Martin escaped death. The Saracens were attacking Sicily in those days, and Olympius went to Sicily and died there. Then, by the scheming of the heretic patriarch, the emperor sent a second commander, Теодор, to bind the pope and bring him, under the charge that he was in league with the Saracens and did not honour the most pure Mother of God.

When the commander reached Rome and read out the charge, the pope answered that it was slander, that he had no fellowship of any kind with the Saracens, the enemies of Christianity — and that as for the most pure Mother of God, if anyone does not honour her and does not confess her and does not bow to her, let him be accursed in this age and in the age to come. It did not alter the commander's orders. The pope was bound and brought to Constantinople, where he lay long in prison, sick past bearing and tormented with narrowness and hunger, until he was sentenced at last to exile at Cherson; and there he lived two years and gave up his soul to the Lord, for whose sake he had suffered so much, in 655 — the year this folder already holds.

The patriarch died two years before him. When the emperor visited him on his deathbed, the entry says, he turned his head to the wall and wept, confessing that he had sinned much against Pope Martin, and begging the emperor to set him free. The patriarch and the two commanders are named here in the entry's own forms and are identified with nobody.

**The Russian calendar keeps him on the same day the Romanian and Serbian ones do, prints a troparion for him, and gives the day of his death, which no source read for this folder had stated.** Its line is «Свт. Мартина исп., папы Римского (655).» and its life makes him a native of Tuscany in Italy, well educated, who entered the clergy of the Roman Church and was chosen for the throne as a presbyter on his predecessor's death. The peace of the Church was broken at the time by the monothelite heresy, which had spread widely, and the emperor and the patriarch of Constantinople both held with it.

The emperor issued a heretical Pattern of the Faith, the Typos, binding on the whole population and forbidding all further dispute. It reached Rome in 649. Martin, a firm champion of Orthodoxy, called a local council at Rome which condemned the heresy, and at the same time sent the patriarch a letter urging him back to the Orthodox confession. The angry emperor ordered a commander to bring him to trial; but the man, come to Rome, was afraid of the clergy and the people gathered for the council, and sent a soldier to kill the pope secretly. The assassin went blind as he came near him, and the commander hurried away to Sicily and was soon killed in battle.

In 654 a second commander was sent on the same errand, with heavy charges laid: secret dealings with the empire's enemies the Saracens, blasphemy against the Mother of God, and an uncanonical accession to the papal throne. The clergy and laity of Rome brought proof of his complete innocence; the commander seized him at night with a detachment of soldiers all the same and sent him to Naxos, one of the Cyclades, where he spent a whole year on a nearly empty island, bearing want and the insults of the guard. Then the exhausted confessor was sent for trial to Constantinople.

The sick old man was carried in on a litter, and the judges ordered him roughly to stand up and answer on his feet; soldiers held him up through the questioning while false witnesses accused him of treasonous dealings with the Saracens, and the judges would not hear his defence at all. The Lord knows, he said in deep grief, what a great kindness you will do me if you put me quickly to death. He was shown in torn clothes to a crowd made to shout anathema on him, and those who knew he was suffering innocently went away in tears. The sentence, read out by the emperor's officer, was to strip him of his rank and put him to death; half naked and in chains he was dragged to a prison and shut up with robbers, who were kinder to him than the heretics had been.

The emperor went meanwhile to the dying patriarch and told him of the trial, and the patriarch turned away from him — woe is me, one more deed to my condemnation — and asked that the torments be stopped. A notary and others were sent to the prison for a further questioning, and the confessor answered that even if they broke him in pieces he would not be in communion with the Church of Constantinople while it remained in false belief. His torturers were struck by his courage and changed the death sentence to exile in the Tauric Chersonese. There he died, worn out by illness, want, hunger and privation, on 16 September 655, and was buried outside the city in a church of the Most Holy Mother of God at Blachernae. The heresy was condemned at the Sixth Ecumenical Council in 680, and his relics were carried to Constantinople and afterwards to Rome.
