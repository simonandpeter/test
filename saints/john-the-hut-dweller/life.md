# John the Hut-dweller

In the reign of the right-believing emperor Leo the Great (457-474) there lived in Constantinople, which is called the new Rome, a man of the rank of stratelates, rich and honoured, named Eutropius, whose wife was Theodora. He had three sons. Two he put into the imperial service and got honourable offices for; the third and youngest, John, he gave over to letters, to rhetoric and to the philosophers. At twelve the boy was sharper than all his fellows, so that his teachers marvelled at his understanding; and he was a chosen pupil not only of the outward philosophy but of the spiritual, for the Holy Spirit worked in him and he was gentle, without malice and humble. What time he had over from his lessons he spent, not in the games boys play, but in reading the God-inspired books and running eagerly to the churches to pray.

A monk came one day into the house where he was studying, and John asked him where he was from and where he was going. He was from the Monastery of the Unsleeping Ones — the house of the Akoimetoi, in Bithynia, built by the blessed Marcellus — and was going to venerate the holy places at Jerusalem. John asked him about the monastic life, about prayer and fasting and the unsleeping singing, and the answers were sweeter to him than honey. Then he took the monk aside by the right hand and begged him: his parents loved him more than his elder brothers and meant him for a great office and a marriage, but he had understood, hearing the word of God in church and reading the books himself, that everything in the world is vanity but one thing. Let the monk take him away to that monastery when he came back. The monk knew a call from God in it and swore that he would.

While the monk was at Jerusalem, John asked his parents to have a Gospel book written for him, that he might learn the words of Christ and do what pleased him. They were delighted at such zeal, found a good scribe, had it written beautifully, bound it in gold and set precious stones in it, so that the outside might draw him as the sweetness of the words inside did. When the monk came back they went down to the harbour and found a ship; but the captain wanted a hundred gold pieces, a full ship's fare. So John went home and told his mother that his fellow students had asked him to their tables many times and he had never repaid the honour, and was ashamed now to go to school at all; and his father, who loved him, gave him the hundred pieces, and set a trusted servant beside him so that a boy should not waste them. John sent the servant off, went aboard with the monk, and sailed with the Gospel book under his arm; and his parents searched Constantinople and the country round it for many days and did not find him.

He laboured in that house six years. Then the enemy, who hates the good, set snares for his feet and gave him such a longing for his parents that his heart was caught with grief, remembering how his father and mother had loved him. He came back to Constantinople in rags, a beggar at his father's gate, and no one knew him. His mother, coming out one day and seeing him rolling in the rubbish, was disgusted and had the servants take him away. But the steward of the house, at his asking, quickly built him a small hut there, and in it he stayed, praying without ceasing. His father sent him food every day; he took it with thanks and shared it among the other poor who came to him, and lived himself in hunger and thirst, so dried out by fasting that the frame of his bones could be counted.

After three years the Lord appeared to him and told him that he had been made like the beloved disciple, John the virgin, since he had left all things and followed him in pure virginity, and that in three days his long labours would be over. He sent the steward, who had been kind to him from the first, to ask his mistress to come, and begged her to have him buried in the place where his hut stood and covered with no other clothes than the rags he had on, since he was unworthy of a better place or a better garment. Then he drew the Gospel book out of his breast and gave it to her, to be her comfort in this life and a good journey in the age to come, for her and for her husband. She turned it over and said that it was like the Gospel her husband had bound in gold for their son; and running, she showed it to him, and he knew it.

They came together and adjured him by the Holy and life-giving Trinity to tell them where he had got the book and where their son John was; and knowing that it was the last hour of his going out, and because of the oath, he judged it right to confess the hidden thing, and told them, sighing from the bottom of his heart and weeping. He was buried, as he had asked, in his own hut. His parents then built a church on that place, over his grave, and endowed it with half of everything they had and gave the other half to the poor, that the tree might match its fruit; and when they died they were buried in it themselves.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and calls him two things in the one line: «Όσιος Ιωάννης ο Καλυβίτης ο δια Χριστόν πτωχός», John of the hut, the poor man for Christ's sake. Its verses turn on the hut itself — that the boy who renounced the world left his hut on earth and pitched a new hut in the heavens, and that on the fifteenth John moved his hut across. saint.gr's own telling of his life is long, and this paragraph does not retell it; what the Greek day adds to the Romanian is that second epithet, the voluntary poverty, and the reading of the hut as a thing he changed rather than a thing he left.
