# Acacius the Centurion

When the devil raised a persecution against the servants of Christ for the third time, Maximian reigning in the east, Acacius entered on the fair struggle of suffering for Christ. He was a Cappadocian by descent, a soldier by his order and a centurion by his honour, under the tribune Firmus, commander of the company called Martisia. The pagan emperor had given orders that a search be made not only through the cities but among the soldiers, for anyone believing in Christ or refusing the pagan gods; such a man was to be put to trial and to torments, and if he resisted the imperial order altogether he was to be punished with death.

So Firmus called his soldiers and asked each of them what he believed; and whenever he found one of the Christian faith he coaxed him with promises and frightened him with torments and pressed him to worship the idols, and many of the small-souled, the weak in faith and the fearful he bent to his own impiety. But when he called the centurion Acacius, Acacius cried out with a great voice that he had been born among Christians, that he was a Christian and meant to remain one, and that the Lord Jesus Christ was his witness that he was a Christian not only from his father and his mother but from his grandfathers and his ancestors.

Firmus tried him with many kinds of deceit and temptation, and not once but many times, coaxing and threatening by turns so that he might obey the imperial order. Then, seeing him immovable, he put him in chains and sent him to the commander Vivianus; and when the commander sat in judgement and questioned the confessors of Christ, the gaoler Antoninus told him that Firmus, tribune of the company of Martisia, had sent a man to him bound the evening before. The long middle of the life, with the examinations and the torments, was not read through here.

At the end the martyr blessed God for counting him, unworthy as he was of such good things, worthy of the honour of being called a martyr of Christ — an honour given him not for any work of his own but out of God's own goodness and unspeakable love for men. Saying this he bowed his head under the sword and it was struck off. Devout people buried his body with honour in the place called Stavrion. All this, the life ends, was in the reign of Maximian; the page prints no year.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and fills in a life the Romanian line did not give. He came of Cappadocia, saint.gr says, and lived at the end of the third century, and he shone among the numberless heroes of the Christian faith in the persecution against the Church under Galerius Maximian, whose reign the page sets at 286 to 305. He was serving in the military ranks and had for his commander the centurion Firmus; and one day Firmus, under orders from above, examined his soldiers to find out how many and which of them were Christians. Among those who confessed Christ was Acacius. Firmus tried to change his mind, and failed.

Firmus then sent him to the proconsul Bibianos, who tried in many and various ways, gentle and harsh, to make Acacius change his faith, and failed too; and after flogging him without pity he sent him to the darkest prison in Byzantium, and still got nothing. After that the proconsul Phalakianos, or Phylakianos as the page also spells him, took Acacius over. He imprisoned him first, and when he saw how unbending he was he ordered him beheaded. Acacius was twenty-five years old. His synaxis used to be kept in the quarter of Heptaskalon, where the emperor Justinian had built a church.

The Serbian church keeps him on this same menologion day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović gives the man behind two bare calendar lines. He was a Roman officer, it says, in the time of the emperor Maximian. Answering at his trial for his faith in Christ, he said that he had inherited the pious faith from his own parents, and that he had been confirmed in it by seeing many wonderful healings worked by the relics of Christian saints.

After great torments borne bravely in the Thracian city of Perinthus he was taken over into Byzantium, where he endured fresh torments, until at the last he was beheaded with the sword. He suffered honourably, the Prologue says, and passed over into the kingdom of eternal joy **in the year 303**.

**That year is the first date this folder has carried.** Neither of the other two calendars prints a century, a country or an emperor for him: doxologia names him and gives no life, and saint.gr gives the name and the rank and nothing else. Nothing narrower and nothing wider was taken, and no place was recorded as a coordinate — he was tortured in one city and died in another, and neither is given as his home.

The Russian church calendar keeps him on the same day and gives him a life of its own, which names the men who tried him. He was born in Cappadocia and was a centurion of the Martisian regiment under a commander named Фирм. When the persecution of Christians began, Фирм questioned his soldiers about their faith one by one; this man confessed himself a Christian firmly and openly, and seeing that he would not bend his commander sent him up to a higher officer, Вивиан, who put him to cruel torments and then had him loaded with heavy irons and shut in prison.

A little later he was marched with other prisoners to Byzantium to be brought before the governor. The soldiers who escorted them went fast and showed the prisoners no mercy, and he grew faint on the road from his wounds, the irons, hunger and thirst; but when at last they halted for the night he gave thanks to God for being counted worthy to suffer for his holy Name, and while he prayed he heard a voice from heaven — «Мужайся, Акакий, и крепись!» The other prisoners heard it too, and many of them believed in Christ and asked him to instruct them and establish them in the faith.

At Byzantium he was kept in harsh confinement while the others were given easier terms, and in the night the prisoners saw shining young men come to him and serve him, washing his wounds and bringing him food. After seven days Вивиан called him up again and was astonished at how well he looked; suspecting that the gaoler had been bribed to feed him and spare him, he had the man beaten, until the prisoner answered for him: «Крепость и сила мне даны от Господа Иисуса Христа, Который исцелил мои раны». In a mad rage Вивиан had him struck in the face and his teeth broken for speaking unasked, and then, meaning to prolong the torture further, sent him on with a letter to the governor Флаккин. But the governor, reading the letter, was indignant that a soldier holding the honourable rank of centurion had been tortured so long and so cruelly, and ordered him beheaded without delay. At the place of execution he lifted his eyes to heaven, gave thanks to God for being counted worthy of a martyr's end, and bowed his head under the sword with a quiet gladness. Under «Константин Великий» his relics lay at Constantinople in a church built in his honour, and were afterwards carried to Calabria, to the city of Squillace.
