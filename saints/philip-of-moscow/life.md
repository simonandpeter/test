# Philip of Moscow

Philip was born Theodore in 1507, the first child of the boyar Stephen Ivanovich of the Kolychev family and of his wife Barbara, who ended her life a nun under the name Barsanuphia. She planted a sincere faith in him and a deep reverence, and he studied the Scriptures and the writings of the Fathers with diligence. The Grand Prince Vasily III, father of Ivan the Terrible, brought the young Theodore to his court, where he came to know the prince's son; but court life did not hold him.

Hearing at the Liturgy the Saviour's words that no man can serve two masters, he went to take leave of his kin and left Moscow in peasant clothes. For a time he lived hidden from the world in the village of Khizna by Lake Onega, earning his bread as a shepherd. Then he came to the Solovetsky monastery, where he was given the hardest obediences: cutting and carrying wood, digging in the garden, working at the mill, going out to fish. After a year and a half the abbot Alexis tonsured him with the name Philip and gave him into the care of the elder Jonah Samin, who corresponded by letter with [Alexander of Svir](/saints/alexander-of-svir).

At the monastery forge he joined unceasing prayer to his work; he was always first into the church and last out of it. Moved afterwards to the bakery, he received there a sign from heaven, and the icon of the Mother of God "of the Bread", before which the humble baker used to pray, can still be seen in the monastery church. In 1546 Archbishop Theodosius of Novgorod raised him to the rank of abbot of Solovki. Chosen against his will, he set to work at once.

He governed the monastery eighteen years and is justly reckoned its second founder, after Zosimas and [Sabbatius](/saints/sabbatius-of-solovki), whose psalter and mantle he found. He gave the brothers a rule of life down to what they should wear, built the church of the Dormition, consecrated in 1557, and in 1558 laid the foundation of the great church of the Transfiguration, working beside the masons himself. In its north wall he cut his own crypt next to his elder Jonah's. Two versts from the monastery is the place still called Philip's desert, and for those who loved solitude he founded a skete on the island of Zaiatskoe.

Ivan the Terrible remembered the Solovki ascetic he had grown up beside and hoped to find in him a true companion and counsellor. Philip long refused the burden of the primacy, and agreed at last only on condition that the oprichnina be dissolved; the hierarchs persuaded him to accept without it, advising him not to meddle in the affairs of the court. He was consecrated Metropolitan of Moscow on 25 June 1566. The city grew quieter for a time, but from the middle of 1567 the oprichnina began again to plunder and kill, and the Metropolitan went and confronted the Tsar.

The final rupture came in Lent 1568. On the third Sunday, 2 March, the Tsar came into the cathedral surrounded by oprichniki dressed as monks and waited for a blessing; Philip stood still, looking at the icon of the Saviour, and then denounced their crimes aloud in the middle of the service. Ivan answered that he had been too gentle with him. The Tsar gathered and tortured officials to make a complaint against the Metropolitan, and when nothing could be found, false witnesses were brought from Solovki — the abbot Paisius, promised a bishopric, the monk Zosima and others who had resented Philip's strictness. Sorcery was among the charges.

At the trial Philip said he did not fear death and would rather be martyred for the truth than stay silent on the Metropolitan's throne, and took off his white cowl and mantle in sign of resignation. The Tsar stopped him and made him keep his rank and serve the Liturgy on 8 November 1568. That day the oprichniki burst in as the service began, read out the sentence, went into the sanctuary and tore the vestments from him, dressed him in rags and dragged him out on a peasant's sledge to the Monastery of the Theophany, with curses and blows.

There he was held in a dark damp cell, in heavy chains and a neck-block, and left without food. Ivan sent him the severed head of a beloved nephew, with word that his sorceries had not helped his kinsman; Philip stood, blessed the head, kissed it on the forehead and asked that the gift be given back to the Tsar. Ivan ordered him executed at once, and the bishops begged for his life. He was moved to the Nikolsky monastery and then, because the people were speaking of his miracles, to a monastery in Tver. Almost a year passed. On 23 December 1569 Maliuta Skuratov smothered him with a pillow, and he was buried behind the monastery church.

The Greek calendar keeps him on 9 January, and saint.gr says plainly why the day is not the day of his death: «Η μνήμη του Αγίου Φιλίππου, εορταζόταν στις 23 Δεκεμβρίου την ημέρα του θανάτου του αλλά το 1660 μ.Χ. μεταφέρθηκε στις 9 Ιανουαρίου» — his memory was kept on 23 December, the day he was killed, until it was moved to 9 January in 1660. The page dates him 1507 to 1569, gives his name in the world as Theodore Stepanovich Kolychev and his parents as Stephen and Barbara, who later became the nun Barsanuphia, and traces him from Solovki, where he was a monk and then abbot, to the metropolitan throne of Moscow in 1566; it adds that his incorrupt relics were laid in the Dormition cathedral of the Kremlin in 1652 by the tsar Alexis Mikhailovich.
