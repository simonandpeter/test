# Mary, called Marinos

The venerable Mary is, saint.gr says, perhaps the most widely spread example of the «sacred legend» of the woman who gives herself to monastic ascesis under a man's disguise. She stands in the Synaxarion of Constantinople, and the page reports two readings of when and where she lived: the fifth century, chiefly on the Maronite tradition, which puts the place of her ascesis in the cave of Qanubin in the Qadisha valley of northern Lebanon; and the seventh century, in Egypt.

The synaxaristes relate that, wanting the monastic life, she disguised herself as a man and took refuge in a monastery together with her father [Eugenius](/saints/eugenius-father-of-mary-called-marinos), who had been left a widower. Once, when she was lodging at an inn with other monks, she was accused of having corrupted the innkeeper's daughter — who had in fact been corrupted by a soldier.

She not only bore the reproach, the insults and the persecutions in silence and patience, but received the child that was born as her own and fed it outside the monastery for three years, the monks having put her out of the house as a sinner. When, after her holy repose, it was made plain that she was a woman, the monks who a little before had called her a wretch blessed her for her virtue and her spiritual manliness and held her a saint.

The distich over her turns on the two names and the two moments that revealed each: «Στολὴ Μαρῖνον μαρτυρεῖ τὴν Μαρίαν, Ταφὴ Μαρίαν δεικνύει τὸν Μαρῖνον» — the habit witnesses Mary to be Marinos, the burial shows Marinos to be Mary.

The Serbian church keeps her on the same day. The Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović opens her entry by calling her a girl of a man's courage. When her mother died her father wished to be tonsured, and Mary would not be parted from him, so by agreement the two of them set out for a men's monastery — she with her hair cut off and in men's clothes, as a youth. Her father died; she was tonsured as a man and given the name Marin.

There was an inn near the monastery, and the innkeeper's daughter took a liking to the supposed monk Marin; having got nowhere, and having conceived by another man and borne a son, she accused Marin of unlawful relations with her. Mary did not defend herself and was driven out of the monastery in disgrace. With another woman's child in her arms she lived three years in the monastery's woods, bearing hunger and frost and every hardship and want. In that time the innkeeper's daughter went mad, and soon after Mary died; only after her death was it learned that the monk Marin had been a woman. The mad girl was healed the moment she touched Mary's relics, and then confessed her dreadful sin. The Prologue dates her repose 508.
