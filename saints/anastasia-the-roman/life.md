# Anastasia the Roman

In the days of the emperors Decius and Valerian and of the governor Probus there was a small monastery of virgins in a hidden place near the city of Rome, with a few sisters of proved life and, over them, the abbess Sophia, old and perfect in good works. In that house lived the blessed virgin Anastasia, of the country about Rome, who had been left an orphan at three years old and was taken and raised by Sophia, and was taught every good work until she surpassed all the other virgins, not in beauty only but in fasting and labour.

When she was near twenty, citizens who had heard of her beauty pressed hard to take her in marriage; but she counted all of it vanity, made herself the bride of Christ, and kept her virginity, watching day and night in prayer. The devil laboured against her with the war of the flesh and with unclean thoughts and could gain nothing, and so rose against her openly: in that time of great persecution he stirred up men who hated Christians to denounce her to Probus.

They told the governor that no fairer virgin was to be found in all Rome; that she lived apart among poor women who had no husbands and would not marry, who mocked the life of the pagans, believed in the Crucified and laughed at their gods. Probus sent his servants at once, and they could not open the monastery until they had cut the doors with axes. The other sisters were terrified and fled by another door, but Sophia would not leave Anastasia: the hour of the contest had come, she said, and the Bridegroom was about to crown her.

Sophia went out to the soldiers and asked two hours to adorn the girl, so that she might be pleasing to their master's eyes. They supposed she meant dress and jewels and waited; she led Anastasia into the church, set her before the altar and wept over her. The Bridegroom's chamber was open, she said, the place of everlasting rest prepared, the crown woven, the Lamb calling her to the marriage. She begged her not to be deceived by a tongue sharp as a razor, nor by gifts and the glory of a vain world, nor frightened by torments that purchase everlasting life.

Anastasia confessed Christ before Probus, and the life tells her torments at length. She was beaten and stretched; the nails were pulled from her fingers; her hands and her feet were cut off and all her teeth broken, and her mouth ran with so much blood that she begged for water. A man named Cyril, who was indeed a Christian, gave her a little to drink; and the governor, seeing it and knowing him for what he was, had him beheaded at once, so that he went to receive his wage for the cup of cold water.

Refreshed, she breathed a little and prayed, "Do not forsake me, God my Saviour." Probus ordered her tongue cut out, and she answered that even so her heart would not stop crying to the Lord, who hears best those who pray in silence; a servant drew the tongue out with pincers and cut it. The whole people cried out in horror and reproached the governor for a torture so wholly without humanity, and he, angry with them, had her taken outside the city and beheaded with the sword. So she finished her martyrdom.

Her body was left unburied for the beasts and the birds, and was kept untouched by God's covering. In the night an angel appeared to Sophia and told her to go and take it up. She went out with a clean cloth, not knowing which way to go, and prayed and was led to the place, and kissed the body and wet it with tears, asking her daughter's prayers now not as a daughter's but as a mother's and a mistress's. Too old and weak to carry it, she was helped by strangers, honourable to look at and Christians by faith, who gathered the severed hands and feet, laid the head in a place apart and honoured, sang over the grave, and buried that treasure of great price.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 29 October, and gives her words of her own. saint.gr sets her in the years of Decius — or by others of Diocletian — and of Valerian, and says she came of rich parents at Rome, whose fortune she gave to the poor before withdrawing into a monastery. When the governor Probus arrested her, about the year 256, he reminded her of her flowering youth, for which she ought to deny Christ. Her answer, the page says, was forceful: one beauty and one youth she knew, the kind Christ gives to faithful and brave souls who choose death for him rather than any good of this world; riches she had had in plenty and had not wanted them, but her Christ she did want, and no power would be able to part her from him — and if he doubted it, he might try. Enraged, Probus whipped her face, spread her on burning coals, hung her up and tore her body, cut off her breasts, pulled out her nails, and at last beheaded her. The page records her shins with the skin and her right hand at the monastery of Gregoriou on the Holy Mountain, and a fragment at the Lavra of Alexander Nevsky in Saint Petersburg.

The Russian church calendar of the Сретенский monastery keeps her on this same menologion day and carries two lives of her, which do not agree with each other. The first has her orphaned at three years old and brought up in a monastery near Rome, twenty-one when the persecution of Decius began, refusing the noble Romans who asked for her; and it is plain about what the governor Probus had done to her — her nails torn from her fingers, her hands and her feet cut off, her teeth knocked out, and when she grew faint from the bleeding and asked for water, a bystander, a certain [Cyril](/saints/cyril-martyr-29-october), pitied her and gave her a drink. The torturers took him for a secret Christian and killed him too. Then they cut out the tongue with which she had not stopped praising God, and at last beheaded her.

The second life tells it otherwise. There it is the abbess Sophia who takes the orphan in and brings her up in faith and in the fear of God, and who blesses her for the contest when Probus sends armed men for her; Probus, seeing how young and how beautiful she was, tries flattery first — why ruin your years, bow to our gods and you shall have a noble husband and live in honour — and she answers that her husband, her riches, her life and her gladness are the Lord Jesus Christ, and that he will not part her from the Lord by the fear of torments. They cut out her tongue; the people, seeing the cruelty, grew angry, and the rulers of the city had to break off the torture and behead her. Her body was thrown outside the city to the wild beasts, and God did not let it be dishonoured: Sophia, told of it by the Lord, found it and buried it with two Christians to help her.

Nothing is chosen between the two. The Russian also dates the persecution to Decius, 249 to 251, and prints the span «ок. 249-259» on its line, where the Greek page gave this folder about 256; the interval now holds both readings. Its name link calls her of Thessalonica as well as of Rome, which nothing else read repeats, so that is left out of the folder and said only here. The day page prints a troparion and a kontakion for her, the first the common of a woman martyr with her name set in it, the second her own.
