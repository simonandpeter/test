# Daniel of Moscow

Daniel was born at Vladimir in 1261, the fourth son of [Alexander
Nevsky](/saints/alexander-nevsky) and the righteous Vassa, and lost his
father at two; his mother’s death is not recorded, but she was buried in the
Nativity church of the Princesses’ convent at Vladimir and honoured by the
people round as righteous. In 1272 he received by the division of the
inheritance the town of Moscow with its lands, a small and unenviable
principality, and built on the bank of the Moskva a church, and a monastery
with it, to his namesake Daniel the Stylite. He strengthened and enlarged
his principality not by wrong and force but by mercy and peace: Rus’ was
never quiet, the princes’ feuds were constant, and often it was his tireless
striving for unity and peace that kept blood from being shed. When in 1293
his brother the grand prince Andrew came with Tatars from the Horde under
Dyuden and laid waste Murom, Suzdal, Kolomna, Dmitrov, Mozhaisk and Tver,
Daniel, having no force to resist, let them into Moscow to save the people
from destruction and bore the ruin and the plundering with his people; in
1295, defending his rights, he had to go out against his brother near
Yuryevo Tolchishche, and there too his love of peace won and no blood was
shed. In 1300, when the prince of Ryazan, Constantine Romanovich, was
secretly preparing a surprise attack on the Moscow lands with Tatar help,
Daniel marched to Ryazan, defeated him, took Constantine prisoner and
destroyed many Tatars — the first victory over the Tatars, quiet but
remarkable, a first stirring toward freedom — and took neither land nor
spoil, as the custom of the time allowed, but showed true freedom from greed
and brotherly love; he never took up arms to seize another’s land, never
took another prince’s property by force or guile, and for that the Lord
enlarged his borders: his nephew John Dmitrievich of Pereyaslavl-Zalessky,
meek, devout and a benefactor of the poor, loved his uncle and, dying
childless in 1302, left him his principality, the first after Rostov in
people and in the strength of its chief town, and Daniel stayed loyal to
Moscow and did not move his seat to the stronger Pereyaslavl. That accession
put Moscow among the principalities that mattered, and there the gathering
of the Russian land into one strong state began; so the honour of the first
princedom and of the first hierarchy began to draw near to the God-loving
city, and in all his days no one harmed his realm and he never tried to take
another’s, thanking God for the blessed inheritance allotted him and, fenced
by the fear of God, growing in brotherly love. In 1303 he fell gravely ill,
took the great schema, and charged that he be buried in his Danilov
monastery — out of deep humility not in the church but in the common
monastery graveyard — and died on 4 March, in his forty-second year. Within
thirty years the house he founded was moved into the Kremlin, its church
made a parish church and its graveyard a lay one; under Ivan III he reminded
his forgetful descendants of himself, appearing to a youth of the grand
prince’s suite — do not fear me; I am a Christian and the lord of this
place; my name is Daniel, prince of Moscow, and by God’s will I am laid
here; tell the grand prince Ivan from me, you comfort yourself and have
forgotten me, but God has not forgotten me — and from then the grand prince
had memorial services sung for his kin; under Ivan the Terrible a dying son
of a Kolomna merchant was healed at his grave, and the tsar, struck by the
wonder, restored the old Danilov monastery and ordered the metropolitan to
go there every year in procession with his council and sing the panikhida.
In 1652 his relics were found incorrupt, and on 30 August they were carried
into the church of the Fathers of the Seven Ecumenical Councils and laid in
a shrine for the glory of the Holy Trinity and the healing of the sick; they
rest now in the Danilov monastery. Metropolitan Platon of Moscow, in the
life he wrote, says that this first founder laid the beginning of Moscow’s
present greatness by a small path trodden with quiet steps — as a building
raised with care rather than haste stands firm, and a tree that begins as a
twig thickens over centuries and spreads its branches far, so this city had
to grow from small, firm beginnings, its first shining too faint to darken
envious eyes, and its founder left the greater glory of its rise to his son,
the grand prince Ivan Danilovich, called Kalita. The Russian calendar keeps
him on 4 March, the day of his death, on 30 August of the old style, 12
September, for the finding of his relics, which is the day this entry stands
under, and in the synaxis of the Moscow saints.

*After the life printed by the Russian church calendar at azbyka.ru — [the entry](https://azbyka.ru/days/sv-daniil-moskovskij), read 6 September 2026; until then this entry had only the Sretensky calendar’s note for the day of the relics.*
