# Serapion (21 March)

doxologia.ro's calendar names him and gives no life. The line for 21 martie reads «Sfântul Cuvios Serapion», and the page it links to carries neither a Viață tab nor a Tropar tab: there is the heading, the day, and nothing more. What the line says is that he was a monastic — «Cuvios» — and that the Romanian church keeps him on this day; that is the whole of what has been read here.

No year, no country, no account of his life and no hymn has been taken from anywhere else, and none has been supplied from memory. The corpus already holds three men of this name, kept on other days and in other calendars, and nothing on this page identifies him with any of them. If a life for him is printed elsewhere, it has not been read, and this page will say only what doxologia says until it has been.

The Greek calendar gives him what the Romanian line did not: an epithet, a country and a life. saint.gr calls him ο Σιδώνιος, of Sidon, says he came from Sidon and lived mostly in Egypt in the fourth century, and makes him foremost in works of kindness and almsgiving — distributing not only his own considerable property to the poor but whatever the faithful who visited him gave him for himself. He gave himself to spreading the word of God in many and various ways.

Two of those ways the page tells. When he once fell into the hands of robbers, the persuasiveness that marked him turned the affair about, and he managed to make the robbers servants of God. And he managed as well to bring over to Orthodoxy a Manichean notable of Lacedaemon, to whom he had been sold as a slave. In the end he went back to his beloved desert, where he fell asleep in peace in extreme old age, having become an example to the monks. Over him the page prints a couplet: having given everything into the hands of the needy, Serapion gives his spirit too at the last to the Lord.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same menologion day and is the first page read for this folder to say anything about him. He was a companion of [Anthony the Great](/saints/anthony-the-great). In the Nitrian desert he was superior of what were called the Arsinoite monasteries, in which there were about eleven thousand monks. Palladius and Sozomen, the Prologue says, call him the Great. He reposed about the year 366 — the only year any page read for this folder gives him.

It also quotes something he wrote, which is paraphrased here rather than copied: that one should not think sickness the heavy thing, for only sin is heavy, since sickness goes with us no further than the grave while sin follows the sinner past it. The Greek calendar calls him the Sidonian, where the Prologue puts him in Egypt and at the head of the Arsinoite houses; the two by-names are recorded as the two pages leave them and are not reconciled here.
