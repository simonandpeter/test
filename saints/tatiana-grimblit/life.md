# Tatiana (Grimblit)

Tatiana was born on 14 December 1903 at Tomsk, the daughter of Nicholas
Grimblit, a clerk of the excise office, and finished the Tomsk gymnasium in
1920, the year her father died; she went to work as a teacher at the
children’s colony at Klyuchi. Brought up in a deeply Christian spirit,
wanting a feat and seeking perfection in keeping the Lord’s commandments,
she gave her life, straight out of school, to the help of her neighbour. In
1920 the civil war ended in Siberia and the repressions began, and Siberia
itself soon became the place of prisons and exile; and Tatiana made it her
rule to turn almost everything she earned, and whatever she could gather in
the churches of Tomsk, into food and things for the Tomsk prison, asking at
the gate which prisoners received no parcels and giving to those. In 1923
she took parcels to the prison at Irkutsk and was arrested there for
counter-revolution, which consisted in charity to prisoners, and freed after
four months; in 1925 the OGPU arrested her again and freed her after seven
days, and she went on as before, by now knowing many of the bishops and
priests of the Russian Church who lay in the prisons of Siberia. The report
drawn up for her arrest called her connected with the counter-revolutionary
clergy of the Narym region, Archangel and the Tomsk and Irkutsk prisons,
collecting and sending by post and by hand, with acquaintances in every
Tikhonite parish through whom the collections were made. Questioned on 6 May
1925, she said that since 1920 she had helped exiled clergy and exiles
generally — in the Alexandrovsk central prison, the Irkutsk prison, Tomsk
and Narym — collecting in the churches and the town, money, things and food,
and sending them by post and with travellers: two-pood parcels to Bishop
Barsanuphius (Vikhvelin) in the Narym exile, help to Bishop Victor
(Bogoyavlensky) in the Irkutsk prison, to the priests Popov and Kopylov and
to Bishops Euthymius, Anthony, Joannicius and Agathangel in Narym, to the
imprisoned clergy and laity of Tomsk, and to prisoners generally without
knowing why they were held; asked whether she had asked the clergy to help
her collect, she said she had and had been refused — so as to draw no one in
— and that she knew no one else who collected. The OGPU, finding no material
for an open trial but her guilt “nevertheless established”, sent the case to
the Special Board in Moscow as that of the inspirer of the Tikhonite
movement in the province, and on 26 March 1926 she was exiled three years to
the Zyryan region, reaching Ust-Sysolsk on 1 July; on 15 July 1927 the Board
sent her across the whole country to Kazakhstan for the rest of her term,
she reached Turkestan on 15 December, was ordered released on 19 December
with leave to live where she pleased, was told of it only on 10 March 1928,
and on 16 March left for Moscow.

There she settled near St Nicholas in Pyzhi, where Archimandrite Gabriel
(Igoshkin), whom she knew well, served, and sang in its choir; and she
helped the exiled and the imprisoned, many of whom she now knew in person,
more actively than before — visiting prisoners and helping them became her
feat and her service to Christ, and bishops who afterwards won the martyr’s
crown said she had become for them a new Philaret the Merciful; in the
breadth of that help and its refusal of no one she had no equal, and in a
heart that held Christ no one was crowded. In the new wave of persecution at
the start of the thirties she was arrested in Moscow on 14 April 1931, told
the interrogator that she had indeed helped exiles and prisoners, and at
first all of them without asking whether they were church people or not, or
political or criminal — it was enough that they were in need and had no one
— and on 30 April was sentenced to three years in the Vishera camp in the
Perm region, where she learned medicine and worked as a medical assistant,
the fittest work for the road she had chosen. Released in 1932 with a ban on
twelve cities, she chose Yuriev-Polsky, then in 1933 Alexandrov, where she
worked as a medical assistant in the hospital, and in 1936 moved to
Konstantinovo in the Moscow region as a laboratory assistant in the district
hospital; working there, and often far beyond her duties, she gave almost
everything she had and everything believers gave her to the imprisoned
clergy and laity, and wrote to all of them, so that to some she was for a
time the only correspondent and helper. Bishop John (Pashin) wrote to her
from the camp that her letter breathed such warmth, love and courage that
the day it came was one of his happy ones — he had read it three times
running and then to his friends; you, weak and poor, warm the destitute with
your goodness like the sun; how wisely and mercifully the Lord arranged
that, having borne a heavy illness — the word for prison in the letters of
those years — you learned medicine, and now, healing the sick, will earn the
little you need for yourself and for helping others; remember the great
martyr Panteleimon with his box of medicines, and let every powder and every
bottle be guarded with the sign of the Cross. Archbishop Averky (Kedrov),
exiled at Birsk in Bashkiria, wrote that her letters were still full of
courage and light, of firm faith and hope in the providing right hand of the
Most High; short as her road had been, how many storms had passed over her
head and through her heart, and had not shaken it from the rock on which it
rested, Christ the Saviour. Of all earthly places she loved Diveyevo best,
where her spiritual father, the archpriest Paul Peruansky, served, and on 5
September 1937, worried by the news of arrests coming in from everywhere,
she wrote to Averky that she had spent a month there and at Sarov on leave —
no, it is not sweeter in paradise, because it is impossible to love more;
answer, dear sun, I am anxious that nothing bad has happened to you; remind
me of the geography, is Birsk far from Ufa?

That evening she was arrested, stopped in the middle of another letter to a
priest in exile. Going to the prison she left a note for a friend, calm even
then: put everything away, sew the bedding and clothes into clean whole
sacks, and ten days after they take me from here send it all to mother,
having first written to her of my arrest; I kiss you all, I thank everyone
for everything, forgive me; I knew, putting on the cross I wear, that I
would go again — for God I would go with joy not only to prison but to the
grave. Asked about the mass of addresses in her seized letters, she said six
were clergy who had been in prison and on the transports and were now in
prison or under restriction, and the rest her relations. Her colleagues at
the hospital, questioned as witnesses, said that she had sat with a dying
patient and crossed him openly when he died, that she gave out medicines
with the words “with the Lord God” and put crosses on the necks of the weak,
that a patient she had visited dreamed all night of monasteries and monks,
that she had not voted for the state loan, that asked why she lived so
meanly she said, you spend your money on wine and the cinema and I on
prisoners and the church, and that of the cross on her neck she said, for
this cross I will give my head, and while I live no one will take it off me;
whoever tries will take it off only with my head, for it is put on for ever;
that she had gladly given up her room to a prisoner of the Dmitlag held
under article 58, saying she was always ready to help such people with
anything; that she said the people had become like cattle and the time would
come when the Lord would punish and ask for everything; and that she had
taught a nine-year-old to cross herself and given her sweets for it. To the
interrogators she said she had belonged to no sect and no organisation,
helped from her own earnings, and, as a religious person, helped only the
religious prisoners she had met on the transports and in prison and written
to them afterwards; before the authorities and those around her she had
tried to show herself an honest and conscientious worker, to prove that a
religious person too can be a needed and useful member of society, and had
never hidden her faith; when people pitied her and said she should dress and
eat better rather than send money to someone, she had answered that they
might spend on fine clothes and a sweet morsel, and she preferred to dress
more simply, eat more plainly and send what was left to those who needed it.
Held in the prison at Zagorsk, charged on 21 September also with wrecking
and deliberately killing patients in the Konstantinovo hospital, she denied
it all, signed the record as taken down correctly from her words, and on 22
September was sentenced to death by the troika; the next day she was taken
to a Moscow prison, photographed for the executioner, shot on 23 September
1937 and buried in an unknown common grave at the Butovo range outside
Moscow. The life is by the igumen Damascene (Orlovsky) from the case files
of the Tomsk and central FSB archives; the Church keeps her on 10 September
of the old style, 23 September, and in the synaxes of the new martyrs of
Butovo, of Moscow, of Vladimir and of Radonezh.
