# Ignatius (Lebedev)

Ignatius — Alexander Alexandrovich Lebedev in the world — was born on 28
May 1884 in the town of Chukhloma in Kostroma province, into the devout
family of Alexander Konstantinovich and Maria Filosofovna Lebedev; his
mother was the daughter of the secretary of the zemstvo court, his father
secretary of the congress of justices of the peace of Chukhloma, raised
for zealous and blameless service to court councillor and decorated with
three orders. He finished the Soligalich church school and the Kostroma
seminary, and in 1903 entered the Kazan veterinary institute, where beside
his studies he went regularly to the services of the Saviour monastery,
whose superior, the archimandrite Barsanuphius, became his first spiritual
guide; there too he met the schema-archimandrite Gabriel (Zyryanov), told
him of his wish to be a monk, and on 25 April 1905 received his blessing —
your desire for monasticism is God’s calling. Reading the Fathers he
copied out what touched him most, above all what they said of the last
times, which spiritually sensitive people already felt these to be: the
vision of Pachomius, who wept over the negligence, laziness and darkening
of the monks of the last days and was told by the Lord that his spiritual
seed would not fail, that those who came after, without guides to lead
them out of the dark pit, would leap out of the darkness by their own
will, walk the bright path of the commandments, be saved by trials and
misfortunes and equal the great saints. In the summer of 1905 his parents
blessed him for the monastic way, and he asked the igumen Herman (Gomzin)
of the Smolensk Zosimova hermitage near the Trinity-Sergius Lavra to
receive him; Herman blessed him to finish the institute first, and in
obedience he went back to Kazan, shaping his life there as a preparation
for the cloister — the rudiments of monasticism and the very idea of the
spiritual life, he wrote later, I received in Kazan: Fr Gabriel, Mother
Apollinaria, Fr Barsanuphius were my earthly guides, and my heavenly ones
the Queen of Heaven in her Kazan icon, the holy hierarch Barsanuphius, at
whose relics I lived the best hours of my life, and the holy hierarch
Gurias. Having sat his last examinations, without waiting for the diploma
or calling on his parents, he went to Zosimova and entered it on 4 May
1908 — you kept your obedience and finished the institute, said Herman,
and we keep our word and receive you. The hermitage was famed for its
unabridged, unhurried, well-sung services, the centre of its life, in a
spirit of great simplicity and penitent tenderness; his first obedience
was to herd the cattle and doctor all the monastery’s animals, and he
passed through every obedience — choir, pharmacy, bookshop, fields,
prosphora bakery, stables, of which Herman said to a bishop, what gold I
have hidden in my stable! Clothed in the cassock in August 1908 and
tonsured rasophore on 17 March 1910, he gave much of his time by the
elder’s blessing to Symeon the New Theologian and Isaac the Syrian in
Paisius Velichkovsky’s translation, and above all to Ignatius
Brianchaninov, in whom he found an ascetic close to his own views and
struggles. On Great Wednesday, 18 March 1915, he was tonsured with the
name Agathon for Agathon the Egyptian ascetic — parents, he wrote that
day, you have a son and have none; he is dead and he lives — under four
commandments from Herman: not to go to the station, not to go out of the
gate without business, not to read newspapers, not to talk idly. Soon
after the tonsure he caught influenza complicated by encephalitis, whose
parkinsonism stayed with him for life. Bishop Theodore (Pozdeevsky)
ordained him hierodeacon at the Danilov monastery on 2 (15) December 1918,
and Patriarch Tikhon hieromonk at the Trinity patriarchal metochion on 9
October 1920. Whatever his obediences he never broke off the inner work,
bringing himself up in poverty of spirit and at first keeping away from
visitors with the briefest answers. Herman died on 30 January 1923 and the
hermitage was soon closed; by the blessing of the hieroschemamonk Alexis
(Solovyov) he moved to Moscow, to a family of Alexis’s spiritual children
in Troitskaya street, who for all their many children gave him a little
one-windowed room looking on the garden of the old patriarchal metochion,
his cell for some years, with its icon-case, its poor bed under a strip of
striped cotton, its portraits of the elders and its relics — of holy
martyrs, a piece of the Mother of God’s girdle, a medallion of Sergius,
and the big copper cross that was his blessing from Kazan. In October 1923
Bishop Bartholomew (Remov) drew him and other Zosimova brethren into the
brotherhood he was forming at the Vysoko-Petrovsky monastery, and on 18
May 1924 made him archimandrite and its superior; his chief work there was
confession, of the monks and of the pilgrims, at the roomy left choir of
the church of Sergius of Radonezh, where two or three waited for him at
first and in time the largest flock in the monastery, though some said he
had begun eldership too early and there were sorrows from the brethren
too. In the middle of 1924 the monastery was closed and the monks took
refuge at the Antiochian metochion, until the bishop obtained the great
cold Bogolyubsky cathedral by the monastery gate, where they laboured
several years; he served the Liturgy only on Sundays and the other days
confessed, sitting in a little armchair, listening closely, eyes sometimes
briefly closed, saying very little — sometimes only the absolution,
sometimes a word that pierced the soul through. The young women who came
to him he prepared for monasticism, some breaking with the world at once,
others living on in their families, studying and working, without giving
up the intention. His guidance, a spiritual daughter recalled, was never
hasty or outward but like life itself, gradual, simple and daily; its
foundation was a sincere, complete, unconcealed disclosure of every deed
and thought and the beginnings of thoughts, and only after that did he
take a soul and lead it. He wanted them to watch their feelings, thoughts
and deeds from the moment they rose, to call things by their names however
ugly the thought and however hard to confess — the harder, the more
serious he grew; I can sit a whole day, he told one, listening while you
get to the point; you have shown the outside — the nut must be cracked
and what is inside shown, not only the shell. When they could not tell
him their day they wrote it, briefly or at length — how they rose, prayed,
went, where they were angry, where they shouted — and after each fault the
word “forgive”, which he loved best: write it oftener, it is the most
useful. In 1926 he judged the time come to begin monasticism, and by the
bishop’s blessing began secret tonsures into the rasophore, never more —
in the rasophore you will slip through the temptations of the world, you
live in the world; in the mantle you will get tangled — choosing very
selectively, preparing each one, picking the name with care and always
changing it, so that without monastery walls or dress they should have a
new intercessor, a new fear and a new joy. Do you never tire of the
people, father? — never; and to whether he feared the scholars, engineers
and artists who came with their questions he only smiled: before receiving
anyone, he said later, I always pray to speak not my own words but what
pleases God and may save them, and then I am always at peace. When
disclosure came hard he would give his last strength — I am ready to sit
all night, only tell me everything to the end; he demanded zeal in church
obediences and even in secular jobs, which he counted as holy obedience,
so that the same outward life was filled to the brim with a new content,
everything done in God’s name and for God’s sake, nothing great or small,
conscience kept in all things, and a love of order. All those years he
still walked home to Troitskaya, now with a companion to hold him up, for
he fell forward, sometimes forced to take a cab; and the burden of the
superior’s business and of the ever-growing crowd drove him in summer to
seek a little quiet outside Moscow — in the summer of 1927 at Zagoryanskoye
on the Northern railway, in a small house above the river that he and the
bishop used by turns, where the thought came to him to found a skete in
Moscow among the senior sisters and so begin his city monastery: its first
nuns were Mother Eupraxia and Mother Xenia under the senior nun
Euphrosyne, blessed with the icon of the Mother of God “of the Sign” that
had hallowed the rooms at Zagoryanskoye, and so named the Znamensky skete —
a shelter and rest of soul and body for all the sisters who took the
monastic path, seven already with those who lived there. He loved it,
though his illness made the climb hard: it was the attic of a tall
seven-storey house on a high hill in Pechatnikov lane on Sretenka, near
the Petrovsky monastery, and explaining the way he said, first a wide
staircase — that is the world — and then a narrow path to the attic — that
is the monastic way, and there is the skete’s door. In the summer of 1929
the Bogolyubsky church was closed and the brethren moved to the church of
Sergius on Bolshaya Dmitrovka; his illness worsened, and a poor widow
named Alexandra, a yard-keeper who lived near the church, made him a
little alcove in her room to lie down between the morning and evening
services and served his spiritual children at table — he later tonsured
her rasophore for John the Much-Suffering, and she died in hospital, not
yet old, before his arrest. In the Nativity fast of 1929 his health broke
sharply, and he petitioned Archbishop Philip (Gumilevsky) for the schema:
on 30 January, the day of Herman’s memory, in the Vladimir chapel of the
church of Sergius, the archimandrite Agathon was tonsured into the great
schema as Ignatius, for the hieromartyr Ignatius the God-bearer and in
memory of Ignatius Brianchaninov, whose works he had long and deeply
loved. In mid-December 1931 he was arrested and held ten days; his hosts
then insisted he stop receiving his spiritual children at home, and he
moved to Nikonovsky lane, where the nun Athanasia (Davydova) and Vera
Vishnyakova had exchanged their flats for one of three rooms, one of which
became his. In October 1933 the church of Sergius on Dmitrovka was closed
and the monks moved to the Nativity of the Mother of God in Putinki; in
1934, before the Protection, the church authorities under NKVD pressure
forbade the schema-archimandrite Ignatius to receive people in church and
retired him, and since all his spiritual children were equal to him, he
said, he would receive no one more for confession — submitting to the
church’s decision, yet bearing the loss painfully, gravely ill for a time
with violent headaches, and going deep into the Fathers for answer and
support. On 10 April 1935 he was arrested and put in the Butyrka, with the
hieromonk Cosmas (Magda), the nuns Athanasia (Davydova) and Agathona
(Komarova), Anastasia Pechnikova and Agrippina Dvoretskaya, a spiritual
daughter of the archpriest [Roman Medved](/saints/roman-medved). The NKVD
wrote that the archimandrite Agathon, playing the fool — “artificially
shaking his hands” — had the authority of a seer and a blessed one among
believers, a great number of followers under his complete influence who
came systematically to his flat, where he worked on them in a religious
and counter-revolutionary spirit, telling them to go to church
systematically and, if work in Soviet institutions prevented it, to leave
their jobs; that the secret nun Agapia (Dvoretskaya) received believers
at her flat at his direction, foretold the future and gave counsel with
anti-Soviet agitation; and that followers back from exile without a
Moscow passport had settled round Moscow at his direction and agitated
there — the nun Anastasia Pechnikova at Mozhaisk spreading false rumours
of war and the imminent fall of the Soviet power. The nun Agathona, found
at his flat in the night of the arrest, was held with him as a secret nun
who helped and served him and often stayed there unregistered — enough
for a charge of counter-revolutionary activity. Questioned the day after
his arrest he said that serving at the Petrovsky monastery, at St
Sergius’s and at the Nativity in Putinki he had had about two hundred
spiritual children who kept close ties with him, coming systematically for
confession, counsel and blessing, and that at Putinki a considerable
number of believers had come wishing to speak with him; and shortly before
the end of the inquiry: I do not admit myself guilty under article 58-10;
my acquaintances did visit me daily, sharing their troubles and asking
advice, our talks were usually on religious subjects, and they brought me
food and money since I had had no means of living since my retirement in
October 1934 — and asked what he had said of the persecution of religion:
the churches are the property of the state, and the Soviet power may
dispose of them as it likes; believers are unhappy when churches are
closed, but must submit even so, and I called those who voiced discontent
to patience and pointed out that one can still pray in the churches not
yet closed. On 4 June 1935 the doctors of the Butyrka hospital found him
suffering from an organic lesion of the central nervous system in the form
of encephalitis — stiffness, sharply retarded movement, a mask-like face
and difficulty of speech — and unfit for labour; on 8 June the special
board of the NKVD gave him five years in a labour camp, and on 16 October
he was sent to the Sarov camp. At last, he wrote to his children after the
long journey, I am in the place the Lord showed us: I am at Sarov, within
the walls of the former monastery! Glory to God for all that has happened
— that alone we can say; with him it is good everywhere, on Tabor and on
Golgotha. In the spring of 1936 he was moved to the camp at
Sukhobezvodnaya near Nizhny Novgorod and soon to an invalids’ camp near
Alatyr, where on 17 July, the eve of St Sergius, the nun Eupraxia had a
meeting with him: greatly weakened, much changed and thinner, still
moving, trying to tell of himself, weeping quietly a great deal, asking
after the vineyard he had raised, and hearing that all were united and
living as they had lived, charging them in tears: the Lord must be loved
with the whole heart, the Lord must be first, the faith never denied; the
Lord is fairer, sweeter, dearer than all, salvation is in your hands — use
it while you can; he is the one sweetness, the one joy. Are you applying to
the Cross, he wrote on 18 April 1937 — the Red Cross, through which his
children were trying to get him freed as an invalid — and wherever else is
needed? it is useful to do it often: the kingdom of God is taken by force.
His health collapsed with the first signs of pellagra, the camp food being
bad and the parcels partly stolen and partly given away, little reaching
him; on 30 May 1938 he begged Eupraxia to write to the colony commander
for a meeting — nine months unseen, with my numberless infirmities; at
times my voice fails from weakness of the heart, and my hands hardly obey
— and later wrote of hands burned by the sun on 13 June that would not
heal for weeks, of dizziness, of falling backward when he stood and
forward after thirty steps, of a burning mouth, of heart and bowels out of
order — all disorders of nourishment, come upon me at last in the fourth
year of imprisonment; remember my sicknesses; forgive me. On 5 September
1938 he dictated his last letter, having no strength to write: I am alive,
but my health is poor; I suffer in the bowels, in general decline of
nourishment; how it will end I do not know, but he who sets right the ways
of men knows all; remember me in sorrows, needs and sickness, and pray;
forgive me. He died in the prison infirmary at dawn on Sunday 11 September
1938, at half past three, on the Beheading of John the Forerunner — 29
August of the old style; the Russian calendar keeps him on 30 August — and
some days later Eupraxia came to Alatyr and was shown a small mound in the
camp cemetery under which he lay. The life was compiled by the igumen
Damascene (Orlovsky); the Russian calendar keeps him also in the synaxes of
the saints of Vladimir and Moscow and of the new martyrs of Vladimir and
Radonezh.
