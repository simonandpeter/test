# Gothazat, companion of Simeon of Persia

saint.gr gives him the one story it tells at any length. There was a Christian community in Persia, it says, whose heart was their bishop [Simeon](/saints/simeon-of-persia), head of the churches of Ctesiphon and of Salik in Seleucia; the Persians slandered the Christians to king Shapur II as having revolutionary designs, and though Simeon assured the king that his religion makes law-abiding citizens and not foolish rebels, the king was so prejudiced that he had him imprisoned.

In the prison Simeon came to know Gothazat, or Gouzthadat as the page also spells him, a Christian deserter who had changed his faith to save his head. Simeon found the right way to move his heart towards Christ again; and when Shapur learnt of it he was angry and beheaded Simeon and Gothazat, and with them another 1150 Christians of the church there, together with the presbyter [Audelas](/saints/audelas-companion-of-simeon), and last of all [Phousik](/saints/phousik-companion-of-simeon), his own kouropalates.

His couplet has him choosing: «Ἐπιτραπέντος τοῦ θύειν ἢ τεθνάναι, Θανεῖν Γοθαζὰτ εἵλετο τμηθεὶς ξίφει» — being given the choice of sacrificing or dying, Gothazat chose to die, cut down by the sword. The page gives him no year, no rank and no country of his own beyond Persia.

**The Russian calendar tells this company's story at far greater length than the Greek page these folders were written from, and it is a different story in its shape.** The Greek has the bishop meet an apostate in the prison and bring him back; the Russian has the apostate meet him on the road to the prison and be shamed into repentance in the street. Shapur II, whose reign it dates 310 to 381, issued a decree in 344 laying a heavy tax on the Christians, and when some refused to pay it — which was presented as a revolt — the persecution began. The bishop was brought to judgement in iron fetters with two presbyters, and would not bow to the king: he had bowed to his rank before, he said, but now, brought there to deny his God, it was not fitting.

Pressed to worship the sun and threatened with the wiping out of Christianity in the country, he was taken to prison; and on the road the king's own former tutor, a eunuch who had worshipped the sun out of fear, stood up and bowed to him, and was turned away from and reproached. The eunuch repented, put off his costly clothes for coarse ones and wept at the palace doors, and when the king asked what grieved him he said openly that he would worship the sun no more. He was condemned, and asked only that heralds should proclaim through the city that he died as a Christian and not for any crime against the king; and the king granted it.

Called before the king a second time the bishop refused again, and the king had every Christian then in the prison beheaded before his eyes; they laid their own heads under the sword as he blessed them. So the first of the two presbyters died. When the turn came to the second he began to tremble, and one of the nobles, a secret Christian, cried out to him not to fear but to close his eyes and he would see at once the divine Light of our Lord Jesus Christ — and with that cry gave himself away. His tongue was torn out and his skin flayed from him, and his daughter was tortured to death with him. Last of all the bishop laid his head on the block with a prayer, on 13 April 344, and the killing went on through the whole of Pascha week to the twenty-third of April.

On him the two sources are the same man told twice, and they are worth setting side by side. The Greek page has the bishop come to know him in the prison, a Christian deserter who had changed his faith to save his head, and has the king beheaded him with the bishop when he learned of the change of heart. This calendar has him a eunuch and the king's own former tutor, who had bowed to the sun out of fear; it has him meet the bishop on the road to the prison, bow to him, be turned away from and reproached, and then repent in the street — putting off his costly clothes for coarse ones and weeping at the palace doors, where the king found him and could not coax him back. It gives him the one request the Greek does not: that heralds should proclaim through the city that he died as a Christian and not for a crime against the king. Where the meeting happened the two differ, and neither reading has been chosen here. This calendar also gives him its second spelling, «Усфазан», which is the one the Greek page's alternative form answers to letter for letter.
