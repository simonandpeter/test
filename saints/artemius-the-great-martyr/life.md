# Artemius

Artemius was an Egyptian by birth and a soldier under Constantine the Great and his son Constantius. The Romanian life says he was a commander in Constantine's army, valiant in war, and that he saw with his own eyes the sign of the Cross in the sky before the battle against Maxentius; from that hour, it says, he was confirmed in the faith and conquered by the power of the Cross. When Constantine died and the empire was divided among his three sons, Artemius served Constantius, who held the East, Constantinople and Egypt.

Under Julian the Apostate he suffered. The life gives a long account of Julian's descent — his secret sacrifices, the grudge he bore his cousins, his claim on the throne — and then brings Artemius before him at Antioch. Julian offered him the high priesthood of the gods and a place second to himself in the empire if he would sacrifice to Apollo of Daphne. Artemius answered that he would not serve the idols, and defended the memory of Constantine against the emperor's charges point by point.

He was stripped and his sides burned with heated spits and his back torn with iron, and he was sent to prison to be starved. In that prison, the life says, Christ appeared to him and healed his wounds, and from the day he was thrown in he ate nothing earthly until his death. Meanwhile Julian went to Daphne to ask Apollo about the Persian war and got no answer, because the relics of [Babylas](/saints/babylas-of-antioch) and of the three youths who suffered with him lay near; and when the relics were removed at his order, fire fell from heaven and burnt the temple with its idol.

Brought out again, Artemius laughed at a god who could not save himself from fire, and told Julian that his own destruction was near. Julian had a great stone split, laid the saint between the halves and let the upper half fall. A day and a night he lay so, his bones crushed and his eyes forced out; and when the stone was lifted he was found alive and walking, flattened like a board, but still able to speak.

He was led out to be beheaded, prayed facing east, heard a voice bidding him enter in with the saints, and was struck down by a soldier's sword on the twentieth day of October, on a Friday. A deaconess of the church of Antioch named Arista asked his body of the judges, anointed it with costly spices and sent it to Constantinople for burial, where many healings followed. Julian died in Persia soon after, as Artemius had told him to his face he would.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 20 October, and saint.gr fills in his office. He was a distinguished statesman of Byzantium and a most pious Christian, it says, and Constantine the Great, valuing his moral and political gifts, gave him the rank of patrikios and appointed him Dux and Augustalis of Alexandria. In 357 the emperor Constantius sent him to Patras to receive the relics of the Apostle Andrew and bring them to the newly built church of the Holy Apostles at Constantinople, which he did on 3 March of that year; while he was at Patras he oversaw the building of an aqueduct, and camped near what is now the Gerokomeio he gave alms to crowds of the needy and above all to old men — which the page offers as the reason for the place's name. When in 363 he heard that Julian the Apostate was torturing the Christians at Antioch, he went there at once and reproached him to his face; Julian had him scourged, his bones broken with stones, and then beheaded, and a deaconess named Ariste took up his body and carried it to Constantinople. The page names Mykonos as the city he patronises, and puts half his jaw at the monastery of Konstamonitou on the Holy Mountain.

The Russian calendar keeps him on the same day and differs on two things. Its year is 362 where the Greek gives 363, so the folder now holds both; and the title on its name link gives him the office the Greek and the Romanian lines leave out, «Артемий Антиохийский, военачальник» — a military commander, and of Antioch. The day page carries a troparion and a kontakion for him, which are set down here with the Greek and the Romanian ones.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 20 October and gives his whole career. He was an Egyptian by birth, and the first dux of Constantine the Great. When the victorious cross appeared to Constantine, ringed about with stars, Artemius saw it too, believed in Christ the Lord and was baptised.

Later, under the emperor Constantius, he was sent into Greece to carry the relics of [Andrew the First-Called](/saints/andrew-the-first-called) from Patras and of [Luke the Evangelist](/saints/luke-the-evangelist) from Thebes to Constantinople, and the dux Artemius did it with joy. He was afterwards made augustalis and imperial viceroy in Egypt, and held that office under Constantius and for a time under Julian the Apostate as well.

When that God-forsaking emperor set out to war against the Persians he halted at Antioch and ordered Artemius to come there with his army. Artemius came — and found the emperor putting two Christian priests to the torture. He was shaken to the heart at the sight of it, went out before the emperor and said: why, O emperor, do you torture so inhumanly men who are innocent and consecrated to God, and why do you force them to renounce the Orthodox faith? And he foretold the emperor his end: your destruction is near.

The enraged emperor sent the two priests into exile in Arabia, where they soon died, and stripped the dux Artemius of his military rank and had him scourged and torn. All wounded and bloodied he was thrown into prison, where the Lord Christ himself appeared to him, healed him and comforted him. Then the emperor had him laid out on one stone and pressed down with another heavy one, so that his whole body was crushed and flattened like a plank; and at the last they cut off his head. And Julian went against the Persians and perished shamefully, as Artemius had foretold him.
