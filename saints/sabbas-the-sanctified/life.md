# Sabbas the Sanctified

Sabbas was born in the parts of Cappadocia, in the metropolitan province of Caesarea, in a village called Mutalasca, while Theodosius the Younger held the sceptre of the Greek empire, in the twenty-seventh year of his reign. His parents were called John and Sophia, of good family and right-believing; and when the child was five they went to Alexandria on imperial service, for John was a soldier, leaving Sabbas and their property with Hermias, his mother's brother. Hermias had a bad-tempered and quarrelsome wife, so the child went to Gregory, his father's brother, in another village called Scandos, and the two uncles fell out over him.

While the parents stayed long in Alexandria the uncles quarrelled, each wanting to have him — not so much out of love for the child as to have a share of his father's goods. The blessed boy, having an old man's understanding and seeing their strife, renounced the whole of his parents' property and went to the monastery of Flavian, some twenty stadia from Mutalasca, and took the angelic habit, being then barely eight years old. Living there he quickly learned the psalter and the other holy scriptures and grew in the virtues, keeping the monastic rule with care. His uncles, once reconciled, came to the monastery and tried to unsettle him, counselling him to leave that holy enclosure and take a wife.

Hearing of [Euthymius the Great](/saints/euthymius-the-great), who shone with virtues and with wonders in the wilderness east of Jerusalem, he went to Palestine; and at the monastery of Saint Passarion, in winter, the archimandrite Elpidius received him and gave him to a Cappadocian elder. On the Saturday he saw Euthymius going to church and fell before him, begging to be received into his lavra; and Euthymius sent him to the house lower in the valley, which was for beginners, to the blessed [Theoctistus](/saints/theoctistus-of-palestine), bidding him take care of the young monk, and prophesied of him.

Under Theoctistus he served and travelled at obedience, and while still young among the young he came to the measure of the fathers grown old in virtue, for which Euthymius called him a young old man: young in body, he had an elder's spiritual wisdom and a pure life. Once on a journey through a great wilderness Sabbas failed and fell for thirst, and Euthymius, pitying him, withdrew a stone's throw and prayed, and water was given. Later he was allowed to shut himself in the cave south of the monastery, and Longinus made it known by letter to Euthymius, whose ears were already full of the accounts of Sabbas's blameless life, of his fasting, his prayers and his meekness.

When Euthymius departed to the Lord, and when other old fathers of that lavra had died and Sabbas saw the monastery's rule changing, he went out into the deeper wilderness. There he found a hermit who said to him: "How did you come to us? Who showed you this place? For by God's grace I have lived here thirty-eight years and have seen no man"; and Sabbas answered that God, who had shown the place to him, had shown it also to another.

So the Great Lavra was gathered about him. While the coenobium was being built and food ran short, the angel of the Lord appeared in a vision to Marcian, archimandrite of Bethlehem, reproaching him for sitting at ease with all he needed while God's servant went without. After the death of the archimandrite Marcian and of the most holy patriarch Sallustius, all the monks of the lavras and monasteries gathered to Sabbas, and the headship of all the monastic assemblies was entrusted to him.

He was sent twice to the emperors on behalf of Palestine. When [Theodosius](/saints/theodosius-the-cenobiarch), his fellow father, went to the Lord on the eleventh day of January, Sabbas was ninety-one years old; and in that time the Samaritans of Palestine rose in revolt. He went to the emperor and turned the anger that was directed against the Palestinians upon the Samaritans instead, and obtained the decrees he asked for. When the emperor, wishing to show his favour, bade him ask for whatever he needed and take as much gold as he wished for his monasteries, the venerable man asked nothing for himself, and while the emperor was ordering everything he had asked, with his counsellors and treasurers, Sabbas withdrew a little aside and read the psalms of David, keeping the third hour.

On his way home he saw a small child named Cyril — the man who afterwards wrote his life — and called him his disciple, prophesying that he would be a monk and a dweller in his lavra. A few days after his return he had a divine vision telling him of his own departure, and he told it to the patriarch and asked leave to go to his lavra. So he died on the fifth day of December, having lived ninety-four years, and passed to the life that does not grow old, with the angels of God and the holy martyrs escorting him; and word of his falling asleep was heard through all the borders of Jerusalem, so that a great multitude of monks gathered from all the lavras and monasteries, and the patriarch came with the bishops and the officials.

His lavra was guarded after him as in his lifetime. Once the Origenists gathered from many places, with one Leontius at their head, meaning to fall on the great lavra without warning, to scatter the right-believing flock and to pull the whole place down to its foundations; and having got ready picks and iron tools and many weapons, they set out in great anger. It was about the second hour of the day when a mist and darkness fell on them on the road, so that they laboured the whole day and did not find the lavra, but wandered in untrodden places until night came on them, and only the next day found themselves beside the monastery of Saint Marcian; and understanding that they could do nothing, they scattered in shame, each to his own.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 5 December, and its page adds the years and the end of the story of his body. saint.gr gives him as born in 440 and dying in 534 at the age of ninety-four, and says that so great was his faith that he once went into a furnace of fire and came out unhurt by the help of God; that for all his seriousness and his gravity of manners, young as he was, [Euthymius](/saints/euthymius-the-great) the Great called him the boy-elder, παιδαριογέροντας, when he sent him to the cenobium that [Theoktistos](/saints/theoctistus-of-palestine) directed; and that for the holiness of his life and his great fame the patriarch of Jerusalem sent him twice as ambassador to Constantinople, first to the emperor Anastasius and then to Justinian.

In 584, it says, his relic was found incorrupt when his grave was opened to bury the abbot Cassian; it was kept at his monastery, then taken to Constantinople in the Arab raids, and in 1965, after the efforts of the patriarch Benediktos, the Roman Catholic church returned it from Venice to the patriarchate of Jerusalem, where it has lain in his monastery since.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same menologion day, gives the same ninety-four years and puts his repose two years earlier, in 532; neither year is chosen here. It begins at the obscure village of Mutalaska in the region of Cappadocia, which this great lamp of the Orthodox Church made famous, and names his father John and his mother Sophia. In his eighth year he left his parents' house and was tonsured in a nearby community called the Flavian; after ten years he crossed into the monasteries of Palestine, and stayed longest with [Euthymius the Great](/saints/euthymius-the-great) and [Theoctistus](/saints/theoctistus-of-palestine). Euthymius, who saw into things, foretold of him that he would be a famous monk and a teacher of monks, and would found a lavra greater than all the lavras of that time.

After Euthymius died he withdrew into the desert, and spent five years as a hermit in a cave an angel of God showed him. When he had become a perfect monk, men who longed for the spiritual life began to gather about him by God's providence, and so many of them came that he had to build a church and many cells. Some Armenians came to him too, and he appointed them a cave in which to serve in the Armenian tongue. When his father died his aged mother came to him; he tonsured her and gave her a cell further off from his monastery, where she struggled to the end of her life.

Many troubles he bore from those near him, from heretics and from demons, and he overcame them all — those near him by mildness and by giving way, the heretics by an unshakeable Orthodox confession, and the demons by the sign of the cross and by calling on God for help. His hardest battle with them was on Mount Castellium, where he founded the second of his monasteries; he founded seven in all. He and his neighbour [Theodosius the Cenobiarch](/saints/theodosius-the-cenobiarch), whom this book calls the Great, were held the two greatest lamps and pillars of Orthodoxy in the East: they corrected emperors and patriarchs in the faith, and served all men by the example of a holy man's humility and of the wondrous power of God. Of his many wonders and good works the book names one only — that he was the first to order the rule of divine service in the monasteries that is known as the rule of the Church of Jerusalem.
