# Socrates of Tiberiopolis

The Fifteen Hieromartyrs are the patrons and city-keepers of Kilkis, and saint.gr tells their story out of the martyrdom written for them by Saint Theophylact, archbishop of Ohrid and of all Bulgaria, in the eleventh or twelfth century. They lived in the fourth century, it says, when the emperor at Constantinople was Julian the Transgressor, whose reign it dates 361 to 363; their descent remains unknown. Julian reopened the temples of the idols, gave them back their properties, and set about public sacrifices, while he put the Christians — «των Γαλιλαίων», of the Galileans, as he called them in contempt — out of public office and out of the schools. Socrates is one of the fifteen, one of the six monks it names.

When his first failure made him savage, the page says, Julian ordered the governors of the cities to arrest Christians and, if they would not deny Christ, to torture them to death. The governor of Nicaea put that order to the Christians of his district, and they answered together that they could not deny Christ the true God and sacrifice to deaf and voiceless idols. Some of them he tortured and killed; many fled to the mountains and the deserts, and others scattered into other places. Among these last were Timothy, Komasios, Eusebios and Theodore, who took refuge first at Thessalonica and then, the persecution following them, went north to the city of Strumitsa, which was then called Tiberiopolis.

Their holy life and their burning teaching drew to them eleven more priests and monks of Tiberiopolis, making up what the page calls the third company of five. It then gives the names: Timothy and Theodore the bishops; Peter, John, Sergios, Theodore and Nikephoros the priests; Basil and Thomas the deacons; [Hierotheos](/saints/hierotheos-of-tiberiopolis), [Daniel](/saints/daniel-of-tiberiopolis), [Chariton](/saints/chariton-of-tiberiopolis), Socrates, [Komasios](/saints/komasios-of-tiberiopolis) and [Eusebios](/saints/eusebios-of-tiberiopolis) the monks.

Word of them reached Thessalonica, where Valles and Philip were governors, fanatical idolaters and blind instruments of Julian. The two hurried to Tiberiopolis, arrested them and brought them to a public trial, charging them with despising the emperor's decrees and believing as God one crucified among robbers. The saints confessed their faith without hesitation, crying the apostle's words that no one shall separate us from the love of Christ, and their arguments were so convincing that the two tyrants cut them short: do you confess the immortal gods or not? «Ποτέ» — never, the martyrs said, we will not sacrifice to demons and their idols. At that the governors sentenced them all to death by the sword, and they set out for the place of execution with joy.

After the governors had gone back to Thessalonica the faithful of Tiberiopolis took the holy relics and laid them in coffins, writing on each the name, the life and the rank of the man within; it was the twenty-eighth of November in the year 362. Later they raised a great church over the fifteen coffins, and many of the wonders worked there are kept by their biographer. The relics were lost afterwards in barbarian sackings, and only the right hand of Peter the presbyter survived, which the Greeks of Strumitsa kept through the Turkish years and carried with them to Kilkis in 1913, with an old icon of the fifteen; and since a royal decree of 19 July 1967 the city keeps their day, 28 November, as its holiday.

Socrates is named among the six monks of the company, and he is one of the five the kontakion names — «καὶ ὁ θεῖος Σωκράτης», and the divine Socrates. Nothing further is said of him singly; the page's note on his name is about the Athenian philosopher and not about him.
