# Eleazar (Spiridonov)

**A Crimean Greek who sang on the kliros as a child, gave his land away in the revolution, and was ruined by a letter to a consul.** The Сретенский monastery’s calendar closes the day with «Сщмч. Елеазара пресвитера и мч. Александра (1937).» and carries no life for either; the life here is what azbyka.ru prints for the civil 6 декабря out of pstgu.ru. Елеазар Спиридонов was born in 1874 in the Crimea, at Евпатория, in a family of Crimean Greeks; his father was a clerk in the salt works. From childhood he sang on the kliros of the Greek church at Евпатория, and as a youth the Greek community made him a present: the parishioners collected money so that he could make a pilgrimage to Jerusalem. His spiritual father was a priest of the town who had built the St Nicholas cathedral there at his own charges, and who married him to Ксения Ивановна Янкова, who also suffered for Christ afterwards. In 1899 he was ordained priest, served in the Таврическая province and taught in the parish school at Евпатория.

**He had saved twelve years for fifteen desyatinas of land and he handed it back.** On the savings of twelve years’ service he had bought the land in the province and kept two cows; but the revolution came and the redistribution of land and other property was declared, and the priest himself offered the local peasants his cows and his plot. Having lost them he moved into the town, where he served in the church of the prophet Elias, built on the money of the Greek community and of a shipowner who directed the building. In 1920 there was a great famine and many of his relatives died.

**The parishioners loved him for the things the authorities could not use.** The life says they loved him for his kind and gentle temper, his meekness and his taking of no money, and that his heartfelt kindness and sincere disposition to everybody made an atmosphere of love and mutual understanding about him. A convinced teetotaller, he drank no wine and kept none in the house, giving his flock the example. Straitened as he was he received all who were in need: two nuns of a ruined monastery lived in his family, and when the Bolsheviks quartered two soldiers on him he took them in without a murmur and fed them at his own cost. At the end of the twenties he became close friends with a spiritual writer whose views and spirit were his own, and who was arrested at the end of 1934 or the beginning of 1935 and died in one of the camps near Moscow.

**He refused to inform and he refused to take off the cassock.** Life changed fast and frighteningly after the revolution. It happened that young men put up to it by the authorities broke the windows during the services. The priest was called in for questioning continually; they demanded that he work with the authorities, threatened him with reprisals if he did not renounce his orders, and required him not to appear in the town in a cassock. He refused to inform on his parishioners and to take the cassock off: «Я священник и буду ходить в подобающей моему сану одежде» — I am a priest and I shall go in the dress that befits my order. It was taken off him only after his arrest. After such summonses he would sit silently at the piano and sink into the Slavonic chants he loved and played masterfully. He knew the arrest was not to be escaped, and his things were already packed for it.

**They took him for a letter to the Greek consul, and sentenced him for books and a memorial.** He was arrested straight after the plundering of the Elias church; the occasion was a letter from the Greek community of that church to the Greek consul, sent with his blessing, telling of the plundering and of the pressure on the community. The other members, as Greek subjects, they did not venture to arrest, but took undertakings not to leave; every parishioner was questioned many times in the hope of getting testimony against their priest, and the community held out bravely, admitting no guilt and giving no testimony against him in spite of the investigator’s threats.

**He was sentenced for books and for a memorial service, and died in the Kolyma mines.** The ground of the sentence was the books taken from the church: «Подвергнув проверке литературу, изъятую при обыске в греческой церкви г.Евпатории, нашли в ней элементы монархической пропаганды». When the patriarch of Constantinople died in 1936 a memorial service was of course sung in the Elias church, and on that ground he was accused of having «служил панихиды за иностранных подданных и о врагах Советского Союза, проживающих за границей». He bore himself with dignity under questioning. Held almost a year in the Simferopol prison under investigation and worn down by frequent and tormenting interrogations, the authorities never got the testimony they wanted: «В предъявленном мне обвинении я себя виновным не признаю, так как я никогда никакой агитации против советской власти не проводил и идей фашизма не проповедовал». Others of the community were sentenced with him, for refusing to testify against their priest. In July 1937 he was sentenced to five years in a corrective labour camp on the Kolyma; he worked in the Kolyma mines less than five months and died on 6 December 1937, aged sixty-four — the civil day of this Julian 23 November, the same day as [Alexander (Uksusov)](/saints/alexander-uksusov), whom the calendar names beside him.
