# Aglaia of Rome

Aglaia was a rich and honoured woman of Rome, daughter of a man named Acacius who had been proconsul of the city. doxologia.ro keeps the same life under her name and under that of [Boniface](/saints/boniface-of-tarsus), who was the servant of her house and of all her wealth, and with whom she lived in impurity. The life says so openly, and says why: that the earlier sins of the saints are not hushed up, so that no sinner should despair of becoming holy by true repentance.

A longing came into her heart to have the relics of martyrs in her house, and she judged Boniface the most faithful of her servants for the errand. She gave him much gold, part of it to be given away to the poor, and he prepared spices and clean linen. When he asked her, in jest, what she would do if his own martyred body were brought back instead, she rebuked him for joking on the road and sent him off with a blessing. He was beheaded at Tarsus in Cilicia.

As the servants came near Rome with the body, an angel of the Lord appeared to her in a dream and told her to receive the man who had once been her servant, and was now their brother and fellow servant, as her own lord and the guardian of her soul. She went out with clerics to meet him, remembered his prophecy, and built a bright church in his name in a village of hers fifty stadia from Rome, where she laid his relics and many miracles were done.

Afterwards she divided her goods among the poor and the destitute, renounced the world, and lived fifteen years in great repentance. She fell asleep in the Lord and was laid beside the martyr's grave. The life closes on the pair of them: one washed his sins away in his own blood and was crowned as a martyr, the other cleansed her life with tears and hard living, and both were shown forth blameless before God.

The Greek calendar keeps the two of them on the same day, and saint.gr says less of her father and more of her standing. Aglaia and [Boniface](/saints/boniface-of-tarsus) lived, it says, in the third century; Aglaia belonged to the class of the noble and rich Roman women and was always ready with alms and various good works, and Boniface was the secretary of her property and the overseer of her estates — like his mistress compassionate and kind to men, managing her property with much honesty and most courteous towards the servants. Of a proconsul father the page says nothing at all.

But unchecked good living choked the spirituality of the two of them, the page says; it set their inflammable youth alight and they were carried away by guilty pleasures of the flesh. Happily the check of their consciences was what prevailed in the end. They sinned; they both wept bitterly; and the page sets against the question whether God would receive them again as living members of his Church his own word, that there is joy before the angels of God over one sinner who repents.

So with much contrition the two penitents confessed their moral fall to a spiritual priest, and their moral return and rebirth was then a fact. Afterwards Boniface died a martyr's death for the faith at Tarsus of Cilicia; and Aglaia, having sold her possessions, devoted her life to the relief of the poor and of the suffering. The page gives her no year of birth and none of death, and the apolytikion it prints names her as the modest one to whom Boniface gave his body as a wealth that is never spent.
