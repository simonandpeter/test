# Senoch of Tours

saint.gr's calendar for 24 Οκτωβρίου names Senoch of Tours, the healer, and gives him a long life that begins with a warning rather than a birth. Vanity of vanities, it quotes from Ecclesiastes, and says that even saints of God whom no passion burns and no defilement touches sometimes come to think themselves wholly righteous and fall through the pride of that conviction: men whom the sword of great sins could not strike down have been easily choked by the little smoke of vainglory. Something of the kind happened, the page says, to the man it is about to describe, who for all his many virtues would certainly have fallen into that pit if faithful brothers had not hurried to admonish him.

Senoch was a Theiphalos by descent, born in the district of Pictavium that was called Theiphalia. He believed in Christ, became a cleric and founded a monastery; in the country of Tours he found ancient ruins and built new buildings upon them, and found there a church in which, it is said, Martin had prayed, which he repaired with great care. Having set in it a holy table with a place prepared for relics, he called the bishop to consecrate it; the blessed bishop Euphronios came, and after the consecration ordained him deacon. When they came to set the reliquary in its place they found it too long to fit, and the deacon knelt and prayed with the bishop, and the space was widened and the reliquary shortened until it sat there easily.

There, with three monks, he served God willingly, and at first lived with great abstinence in food and water; in Lent his labour increased, and he ate only barley bread and drank only water, not more than four hundred grams of each in a day. He bore the cold of winter unshod and wore iron chains on his hands, his feet and his neck, and later, for a stricter solitude, he withdrew from the sight of the brothers and shut himself in a small cell, where he prayed day and night. What the faithful gave him out of reverence he preferred to put into the purses of the poor rather than into hiding places, remembering that where a man's treasure is there his heart will be; and in the course of his life he freed more than two hundred wretched people from the yoke of slavery and the weight of their debts.

He had the gift of healing the sick, and as he advanced in holiness vainglory began little by little to come in: leaving his cell he went with much haughtiness to visit his parents in the district of Pictavium, and came back swollen with pride and looking only to please himself. But when the other monks rebuked him and he heard what they said of the vainglorious, that they are driven out of the kingdom of God, he was cleansed of it entirely and humbled himself so far that not the least root of pride was left in him, which he confessed by saying that he now felt the truth of the Apostle's words, let him who boasts boast in the Lord.

He wished after that to stay enclosed so that no man should see him, and the monks advised him not to submit to such enclosure permanently but only from the repose of Saint Martin to the feast of the Nativity and through the Lent before Pascha, holding himself the rest of the time at the disposal of the sick; and he obeyed them willingly and without argument. The page then tells the healings: a blind man called Popousitos who saw again when the priest's hand made the sign of the cross on his eyes, a young man of Pictavium with the same affliction, two children whose fingers straightened when he took their hands in his, a woman called Benaia who came with her eyes shut and went away with them lit, men swollen from the bite of a serpent whose poison lost its force at his prayer, and others tormented by demons who recovered their minds when he laid his hands on them. Those he healed he fed and clothed if they were poor; and his care for the poor went so far that he undertook to build them bridges over the rivers, so that no one should mourn a drowning when the waters were in flood.
