# John (Ilyinsky)

John Euthymievich Ilyinsky was born in 1846, the son of a sacristan, and
entered the Simbirsk seminary in 1864 — his father, a plain church servant
who had spent his life in the sanctuary without orders, straining his small
means to give the son he saw chosen for the Church a proper education. He
finished in 1870 with a second-class certificate, and in no hurry either for
orders or for a wife, taught three years in the zemstvo schools, winning
three awards from the Ministry of Education for it. In 1873 he married
Alexandra, eighteen, with whom he lived forty-five years in love and
concord, and the same year Bishop Eugene (Sakharov-Platonov) of Simbirsk
ordained him deacon and soon after priest for Tomylovo in the Sengiley
district, a large village of two hundred and eighty-five households and over
three thousand souls by 1913, with a zemstvo school and a ministry school;
it was his first and only parish, and he served it forty-five years to his
martyrdom. From the first day he taught the law of God in the village
school, and was thanked by the diocese in 1880 for it; he sat on the deanery
council from 1895 to 1899, was the deanery’s missionary for fourteen years
from 1886 and from 1911 for the Simbirsk and Buinsk districts — no light
post in a country full of priestless Old Believers and of pagan Chuvash and
Mordvins — taught four years in the Tomylovo missionary school preparing his
parishioners to answer the schism, and brought fifty people back from it. He
was given the nabedrennik in 1884, the skufia in 1887 and the kamilavka in
1894, the thanks of the Kazan educational district and of the Simbirsk
missionary society, the Synod’s blessing in 1901 for helping his
parishioners in the famine year, the order of St Anne in 1903, the pectoral
cross in 1906, and in 1913, for forty years of priesthood, the rank of
archpriest. He and Alexandra had six children: three sons at the front in
the First World War — Vladimir a surgeon in the field hospitals, Alexander
an ensign, Nicholas out of the Kiev ensigns’ school in 1915 — and three
daughters, two of them village teachers and one a priest’s wife.

In 1918 the fighting between the Czech legion and the Red Army reached the
Sengiley district. The priest of the neighbouring Kivat, Heraclius
Zhemchuzhnikov, recorded it in his parish chronicle: the Reds’ arrival on 16
August, his own narrow escape from being shot as a “kulak” priest until the
parishioners denied it to a man, the Czechs’ attack down the Tomylovo hill
on the 18th and the Reds’ retreat, and the return of quiet only on 12
September, when the Czechs fell back toward Syzran. Their staff had been
quartered in the archpriest’s house at Tomylovo; as they left, the Red
soldiers came in. On 13 September, after the vigil of the Exaltation of the
Cross, he was called to a dying parishioner and went out of the church in
stole and cuffs with the pyx about his neck; at the gate three Red soldiers
stopped him, one tore the pyx with the Holy Gifts from the old man’s neck
and flung it away — enough of your deceiving the people; you are under
arrest — and without letting him take off his vestments or say goodbye to
his family they marched him to Bezvodovka station, questioned him all night
in the staff carriage, and at about ten in the morning shunted the carriage
into a siding, led him a quarter of a verst to the edge of the wood, and
shot him, throwing the body into a shallow pit under a little earth — most
likely for harbouring the Czech staff. So on the feast of the Cross a
zealous servant of the altar went up his Golgotha. His family knew nothing
for a long time; in October his wife learned of his death and where the body
lay, and on 25 October 1918 asked the Simbirsk provincial executive
committee for leave to bury him with Christian rites, and got no answer; on
9 September 1919 his son Alexander wrote again that the body still lay under
barely half a metre of earth after almost a year of “consideration”, and
that the chairman of the investigating commission had told him his father
had nothing to do with counter-revolution. No document of a permission
survives, but it may be presumed that he was at last laid in the parish
cemetery of Tomylovo. His property was partly looted and partly confiscated
by the committee of the poor, one cow led off and the other slaughtered, and
his widow and daughter were left without means. On 13 September 1919 the
Simbirsk revolutionary tribunal, hearing “the case of the priest John
Ilyinsky, accused of counter-revolution”, found there was no sufficient
material for the charge, that he had been shot by unknown persons whose
names could not be established, dismissed the case and ordered the property
returned: the killers’ own court could find no political ground for the
killing, and covered the killers. His whole guilt was forty-five years at
one altar. The life is by the archpriest Alexis Skala; the Church keeps him
on 15 September of the old style, 28 September, and in the synaxis of the
Simbirsk saints.
