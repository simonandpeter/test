# Anthimos the New, the blind ascetic of Cephalonia

Anthimos, the blind missionary of the Aegean, was born at Lixouri in
Cephalonia in 1727 — a contemporary, the synaxarion says, of that other
apostle and martyr of the nation, Kosmas of Aetolia, whose work and offering
his resembled. Athanasius at baptism, he lost his sight at seven to smallpox
and got it back by God’s hand and the unsleeping prayers of his mother
Atzouleta and his own: they had joined a forty-liturgy for his healing, and
at the fortieth, after mother and son had communicated, the child cried
out, I can see — the priest is wearing a red phelonion. Quick and retentive,
he was schooled probably by the abbot Anthimos of the monastery of St
Paraskevi at Lepeda, his first teacher and confessor; at twenty he went to
sea in his father’s trade, and his father died of plague at Constantinople
on one of their voyages. In 1750 his captain, taken with his character,
offered him his daughter, and he accepted. On the morning of the wedding he
woke wholly blind. Now I understand, he said, that I am blinded by God — and
theotyphlos, God-blinded, was what he was called for the rest of his life.
He took it as the Lord’s will, left the world, and went to the Holy Mountain,
where he was tonsured Anthimos, probably at Iveron, lived in great strictness
and took part in the Kollyvades movement; and when his elder died he decided
to go out and preach, to save the enslaved Greeks from ignorance and from
turning Turk.

His first journey took him to Chios, where he ate a little bread a day and
drank water only after sunset, then to Siphnos, then towards Paros — the ship
caught in a storm that stopped at his tearful prayer to the Mother of God,
a miracle he begged his fellow passengers not to spread and which they spread
everywhere — and, at the bishop’s invitation, to Naxos, where among those who
heard him was the boy Nicholas Kallivourtsis, the future Nicodemus of the
Holy Mountain; then Ios, and the Holy Places, where he wept at the Sepulchre
with sightless eyes. Coming back, about 1759, he made for Kastellorizo to
found his first monastery: the island was in drought, he prayed, the sky
opened, and the people built St George on the highest hill and its cells and
walls around it, made stavropegial in 1761. From there he went to Astypalaia
and stayed nine years. Told to build a monastery of the Panagia Portaitissa
there, and told there were no building materials, he had a landowner named
Chousoulinis open his fields and showed the workmen where to dig: they found
the stones of an old settlement, and a jar of gold coins, which he had them
say nothing of and which turned, at his prayer, into a heap of charcoal, so
the Turks would have nothing to seize. He went round the Greek lands
collecting money and vessels for it, and at Iveron pressed an old iconographer
to copy the Portaitissa: the two prayed, laid a board under the wonderworking
icon, kept vigil, and in the morning found the Mother of God’s likeness
printed on the board, which he kissed and could not see. On the island he
rid the north-east of a great snake that had kept it uninhabited — it came
out to the woodpile he had built and coiled on it, and they burned it there,
and grass is said not to grow on the spot — so that he has been the terror of
snakes ever since. Slandered at last over the nuns of his monastery, and
cleared at a public trial, he chose to leave for his own island and quiet.

He reached Lixouri at the end of 1768, found St Paraskevi at Lepeda in ruins
from the earthquakes of 1766 and 1767, and rebuilt it from the foundations
with the seven nuns who had followed him from the Dodecanese as its first
sisterhood, and gave it a rule that is still called his Testament; Kosmas of
Aetolia, the tradition says, visited him there in 1777. From Lepeda he went
out to Kythera, the Peloponnese, Crete and Siphnos — at Sphakia in 1770
three sorcerers who meant to kill him as he preached saw flames come from
his mouth and gave it up; there he healed a blind woman and brought rain in
dry years — founded a monastery of the Forerunner’s Nativity on Kythera in
1773 with the money of a captain saved from a storm, and on 10 July 1775
restored the convent of the Chrysopigi on Sikinos, the sixth and last house
he raised or restored without ever seeing one of them. On his way to the
Mani he was told his end was near, ordered the sailors back to Lepeda, fell
ill with jaundice, blessed those who came, and gave his soul to Christ on
4 September 1782. His relics were taken up in 1800, saffron-coloured and
fragrant; only his right forearm survives, at the Portaitissa on Astypalaia,
whose patron he is. The church proclaimed his sanctity on 30 July 1974.
