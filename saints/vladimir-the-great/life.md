# Vladimir the Great

Vladimir was prince of Kiev and sole ruler of all Rus, the son of Svyatoslav, grandson of Igor and of Saint Olga, and great-grandson of Rurik, who had been called from the Germans to rule in Rus and whose seat was at great Novgorod. Igor, Rurik's son, moved the seat to Kiev after his father's death, and Olga bore him Svyatoslav; and Svyatoslav had three sons, not by one woman — Yaropolk, Oleg and Vladimir.

Svyatoslav divided the great rule of Rus among the three: Kiev to Yaropolk the eldest, the Drevlians to Oleg, and great Novgorod to Vladimir, the youngest. After their father's death Yaropolk, sitting on the paternal throne at Kiev, made war on Oleg and killed him and took his rule; and Vladimir, fearing the same from his eldest brother, fled overseas to the Germans, so that Yaropolk held Novgorod and the Drevlians too.

Not long after, Vladimir gathered a great force from the Germans and from other parts of the land of Rus, marched against Yaropolk, killed him, and took the rule of Kiev and his brother's wife, a Greek woman, and became master of the whole land of Rus. He set idols in the high places of Kiev and worshipped them with sacrifices and urged all the people to worship them, and ordered those who would not to be put to death. Then the blessed Theodore, a Christian, and his son John were killed, because he would not share in the worship of idols nor give his son to a devilish sacrifice.

Of the Christians whom Saint Olga had enlightened, and for whom she had built a church at Askold's mound, those who were still alive went unknown for fear: some fled, some kept the holy faith in secret, some turned back to their old paganism, and any Christian church that stood anywhere was pulled down by the hands of the idolaters. Among the idols Vladimir set up in Kiev the page names first Perun, whom he held to be the god of thunder and lightning and the rain clouds.

The Romanian life is a very long one and the middle of it is left here unsummarised. Its end says that the great prince Vladimir, who at his holy baptism was named Basil, having moved from great Novgorod, spent thirty-five years in the great rule of Kiev — eight years before his baptism and twenty-seven and some months after it — and died in the twenty-eighth, in the year 1015 from the incarnation of God the Word.

Svyatopolk, who loved power, rejoiced at his father's death, meaning to seize the throne of the great rule, and first hid the death; then, no longer able to hide it, he had the body brought into the church of the Most Pure Mother of God, the Desyatinnaya. There all Kiev and the country round, clergy and laity, gathered to his honoured body, weeping and lamenting for a father and for the one who had won their salvation, and gave him a glorious burial, laying him in a marble tomb in the church he had built. Then they appointed that his memory be kept as that of a saint and equal to the apostles, because he had enlightened the whole land of Rus with holy baptism.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 15 July, and saint.gr sets out his descent. It says he was the son of Svyatoslav, prince of Kiev, that he was born in 958, and that his mother was called Malousha and had first been a servant of his father's mother — that is, of the holy queen [Olga](/saints/olga-of-kyiv), who was the first in Russia to become a Christian and led many to piety. When Vladimir took the throne of Kiev he was at first a pagan and acted like one, taking many wives and fathering twelve sons and eleven daughters.

Then, through the fervent prayers of his holy grandmother and a divine illumination — many signs are told of his coming over to Christianity — he believed in Christ and was baptised at Kiev. He married Anna, sister of the Byzantine emperor Basil II the Bulgar-slayer, pulled down every idol in the land, baptised most of the Russians and built two fine churches at Kiev; his life changed root and branch, and he died peacefully on 15 July 1015.

The Serbian church keeps him on the same 15 July. The Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović says he was named Basil at his baptism, and that he was the son of prince Svyatoslav and the grandson of Igor and of [Olga of Kyiv](/saints/olga-of-kyiv); and that at first he was wholly a pagan, in belief and in life. Learning that other faiths existed, he began carefully to enquire which of them was best, and sent an embassy to Constantinople among others. When the envoys came back they told the prince that they had been at the service in the Orthodox church, Holy Wisdom, and had been «beside themselves, not knowing whether they were on earth or in heaven». That moved him to be baptised and to baptise his people.

The chief idol, Perun, was pulled down from the hill of Kyiv and thrown into the river Dnieper. Having received the Christian faith, the Prologue says, he changed his life perfectly and put all his labour into keeping every precept of it exactly. He ordered churches built everywhere in his realm in place of the idols that had been torn down, and himself raised a beautiful church to the Most Holy Theotokos at Kyiv — on the very place, the book says, where a father and son had earlier suffered martyrdom for Christ, and it sends the reader to its own entry for the 12 July for them. **With the same irresistible force with which he had once defended idolatry**, it ends, he now spread Christianity; and he reposed in the Lord in 1015.
