# Paraskeva of Diveyevo

Paraskeva was born Irina, into a peasant family of the province of Tambov. Her parents married her off, and she was married fifteen years without children; after her husband’s death she suffered a great deal, and after a journey to Kyiv she resolved to give herself to God, was tonsured there and took the name Paraskeva. For thirty years she lived in the forest of Sarov, in a cave she had dug with her own hands, already counted a fool for Christ, and because she had the gift of foresight many came for her help and her prayers. Robbers beat her almost to death, the trial, the Greek calendar says, that Saint Seraphim had borne. In her last years she lived for long stretches at the Diveyevo convent.

She kept children’s dolls and looked after them like her children. Unlike other fools for Christ she was always very clean, in clean light-coloured clothes, and kept her cell in perfect order; her bed was decked with fat pillows and dolls and she hardly used it, sleeping little and never lying down. She knitted socks while she said the Prayer, and made many prostrations while cutting grass with a sickle. The spiritual world was open to her, so that she saw the souls and thoughts of her visitors; she counselled and guided another fool for Christ, [Maria Ivanovna](/saints/maria-ivanovna-of-diveyevo). When she foresaw something bad for a visitor she would put a great deal of sugar in their tea. In 1903, at the uncovering of the relics of Seraphim of Sarov, she foretold to the Tsar Nicholas and the Tsaritsa that they would have an heir, and the bloodshed of 1917; she filled the Tsar’s cup with sugar until it overflowed, and those who saw his face as he left her cell saw that he was plainly shaken. Before her death she made prostrations before his portrait, foreseeing his sanctity.

She died in peace on 22 September 1915, at a hundred and twenty, the day the Russian Church keeps her, and her relics are at the Diveyevo monastery.

The Russian calendar keeps her on 22 September old style, with 1915, and prints no life; azbyka.ru’s life, read in its opening part, tells her early years more fully and puts her birth at the beginning of the nineteenth century where the Greek page has 1795. She was born Irina in the village of Nikolskoye in the Spassk district of Tambov province, to Ivan and Daria, serfs of the Bulygins, who at seventeen married her to a peasant, Fyodor; after fifteen years the Bulygins sold them to German landowners, the Schmidts, and five years later her husband died of consumption. She refused to marry again. Falsely accused by the servants of stealing two lengths of linen, she was savagely beaten by soldiers at the district officer’s order, left, and went on pilgrimage to Kyiv, where the holy places and the elders changed her; twice the police found her there and sent her back under escort, and the second time the Schmidts would not take her in and turned her out. Tonsured at Kyiv with the name Paraskeva, she wandered her village for five years as a madwoman, then lived more than twenty years in a cave she had dug herself in the forest of Sarov, working at the Sarov mill; once, when she came out of the forest to rebuke Tatars who had just robbed a church, they beat her half to death and broke her head. azbyka.ru prints a troparion and a kontakion to Pelagia, Paraskeva and Maria of Diveyevo together; being to the three, they are not written here.
