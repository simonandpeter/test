# Julitta, mother of Cyricus

Julitta was a young woman of Iconium in Lycaonia, of good family — the Romanian life says she came of the line of the older emperors of Rome — and a Christian in her faith. She lived a short while with a lawful husband, bore him a son, and was left a widow; the child she enlightened with holy baptism and named [Cyricus](/saints/cyricus-son-of-julitta).

When Diocletian's persecution reached Lycaonia under a governor named Domitian, a man who rejoiced in the shedding of Christian blood, she judged that her faith could not stay hidden and feared that under torture she might deny Christ. So she left her goods, her house, her kindred, her servants and all the beauty of this world for the love of Christ, and went out of Iconium by night with her three-year-old son and two of her more faithful maidservants, remembering that here we have no abiding city.

She went to Seleucia as a stranger and hid her birth, and found the persecution there too under the governor Alexander; recalling the word to give place to wrath and to flee from one city to the next, she went on to Tarsus in Cilicia and lived among the poor. Alexander came to Tarsus in his turn, she was recognised and denounced, and soldiers took her with her child while her two maidservants fled and followed at a distance to see her end.

Set before the tribunal with Cyricus in her arms and asked her name, family and country, she answered that Christian was her name and her good family, and her country the heavenly kingdom of Christ. The governor had the child taken away and had her stripped, stretched on the ground and beaten with raw sinews; and under the blows she suffered as if in another's body and, feeling nothing, like a lifeless pillar, cried only that she was a Christian and would not sacrifice to their idols.

When they had stopped and raised her up she saw her son dead in his blood before the tribunal, the governor having thrown him down the stone steps, and she was filled with joy and thanked God that the boy had been granted to be finished as a martyr before her and to take the unfading crown. Then she was hung up and raked with iron combs and her wounds sprinkled with molten pitch, while the herald called on her to spare her youth and worship the gods; she answered that she would not bow to deaf and dumb idols but to her Lord Jesus Christ, and that she was hurrying to overtake her son.

Seeing her endurance unbroken, the governor condemned her to the sword. She went out to the place of execution rejoicing as to a wedding, asked a little time, knelt and thanked Christ for calling her son before her and asked to be numbered with the wise virgins; and the executioner struck her neck. Both bodies were left unburied for the dogs and the beasts, and in the night the two maidservants carried them far off and buried them. One of those women lived to the days of Constantine, the first Christian emperor, and showed the Christians the place; the relics were taken up whole and fragrant and gave healings, and the passion was written down.
