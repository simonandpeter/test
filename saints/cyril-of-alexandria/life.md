# Cyril of Alexandria

Cyril was born in 378 at Mahalla in Egypt, the son of a well-born and believing father; his mother came from Memphis, and her parents died leaving her and her adolescent brother Theophilus orphans. In 362 or 363 Theophilus took his younger sister and left Memphis for Alexandria, where Archbishop [Athanasius](/saints/athanasius-of-alexandria) baptised the two orphans and took them under his protection: the girl to a community of virgins until she was of age to marry Cyril's father, and Theophilus into his own house, where he finished his studies, entered the clergy, and served Athanasius as his secretary in his last three years.

Theophilus, by then patriarch, called his sister's son to Alexandria so that he might get on with his studies and learn the business of the Church, and Cyril came under his uncle's tutelage at twelve. He was given a liberal education — the classical literature in detail, with mathematics, music and astronomy, then rhetoric and philology, which his elaborate Attic Greek shows — and studied the Christian dogma at the school of Alexandria under the famous exegete Didymus the Blind. For five years he lived on Mount Nitria in asceticism, and the *Evergetinos* keeps a saying of his there: that a man should not fear dead thoughts, the old memories, but flee the living ones, the words at hand, and give himself the more to prayer. His uncle then had him ordained archdeacon.

When Theophilus died on 15 October 412, the clergy and the people, after three days of deliberation, judged Cyril worthy of the see, though he was only thirty-four and the administration of Byzantium was backing the archdeacon Timothy; he was consecrated patriarch on 18 October 412. He drove the Novatian schismatics out of Alexandria and confiscated their treasury; and at Menuthis, near Canopus, where his uncle had wished and failed to clear an old shrine of Isis, he was told by an angel in a vision to bring there the relics of the unmercenary physicians Cyrus and John, built them a church, and the place became a spring of healing. The life also tells at length of the violence between the Jewish community and the Christians of the city, and of his quarrel with the prefect Orestes.

In 428 the controversy broke out over Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople, who would not have the Virgin called Theotokos. Cyril wrote to him as a brother, then sharply, then to Pope Celestine; he drew up the Twelve Anathemas, and at the Third Ecumenical Council, which Theodosius the Younger called to Ephesus for Pentecost 431, he opened the session on 22 June before two hundred bishops and Nestorius was declared excluded from the episcopate. The emperor at first suspended both parties and Cyril was kept under arrest at Ephesus for weeks; John of Antioch held his own council against him; peace came only when Cyril and John agreed on one confession, in the letter the page quotes, which the life says agrees fully with the later formula of Chalcedon.

One fault the life will not pass over: his long anger against [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom), which he held not from malice but from a preconceived opinion, and which Isidore of Pelusium, his kinsman, rebuked him for plainly. He was healed of it by a dream in which he saw a beautiful church and the Mother of God in it, and John and those serving him drove him out until she interceded. At his death, the life says, the Mother of God herself stood beside him and visited her servant, who had served her faithfully and laboured much for her honour.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, with Athanasius the Great, and saint.gr sets down his course briefly. He lived under Theodosius the Younger and was born at Alexandria in 370, of well-off parents of the Greek society of the city; he was nephew to Theophilus, archbishop of Alexandria, took a great theological education, and afterwards succeeded his uncle on that throne. When the Third Ecumenical Council met at Ephesus in 431 he presided over it, and he worked the pulling down of the errors of Nestorius concerning the person of the most holy Theotokos. Having patriarched about thirty-two years, he gave up his spirit peacefully to the Lord on 27 June 444, and Anastasius of Sinai, the page adds, rightly gave him the name «σφραγίδα των Πατέρων», the seal of the Fathers. A portion of his relic, it says, is at the monastery of Mega Spilaio of Kalavryta.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 9 June, which is the day this page already argues for and neither of the other two calendars uses: both of those keep him on 18 January, and the Romanian life itself says the ninth of June is the day of his repose and the eighteenth of January is not. This book’s own Julian 18 January prints two other archbishops and does not name him. It sets his life out as three fights. He was of noble descent and a close kinsman of Theophilus, patriarch of Alexandria, whom this corpus does not keep, and he was consecrated patriarch after that man’s death.

The first fight was with the Novatians, who began at Rome and are named after the presbyter Novatian: they prided themselves on their own virtue, went about in white clothing, forbade a second marriage, and held that one must not pray for those who commit a mortal sin nor take back into the church those who once fall away from it, however bitterly they repent. Cyril beat them and drove them out of Alexandria together with their bishop. The second, and this book calls it much harder and bloodier, was with the Jews of Alexandria, who had grown powerful there from the founding of the city; after a long struggle he prevailed with the emperor that they be driven out.

The third was with Nestorius, and the Third Ecumenical Council at Ephesus settled it. Cyril presided at that Council himself, and at the same time represented [Celestine](/saints/celestine-of-rome), pope of Rome, at that pope’s own request, he being too old to come. Nestorius was condemned, anathematised and banished by the emperor to the eastern frontier of the empire, where he came to a horrible end, worms eating the tongue with which he had blasphemed the Most Holy Theotokos by calling her Christotokos. After the fighting was over Cyril lived in peace and shepherded the flock of Christ zealously, and stood before the Lord in 444, where the Romanian life’s note gives 446; the folder now holds both. And it is said of him, this book ends, that he composed the hymn that begins Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos — an attribution and not a text, and this source prints no hymn.

The Russian calendar keeps him twice and the day this folder now records is the June one. Its line for 9 июня is «Свт. Кирилла , архиеп. Александрийского (444).» and its name page lists «18 января , 9 июня», so of the two days that calendar keeps, the January one is the joint commemoration with the great Athanasius that the Greek calendar also keeps, and this June day is his own. The June day page prints a troparion and a kontakion over him, which are added here with their English typed: the troparion is the common of a hierarch with his name filled into it, and the kontakion is his own, with the abyss of theology drawn from the springs of the Saviour that drowns the heresies and saves the flock from the surge of the waves.
