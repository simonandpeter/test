# Constantine the Great

The life doxologia prints begins with his father. Constas, the emperor in Britain, was the grandson by a daughter of the earlier emperor Claudius, and Constantine was born to him of [Helen](/saints/helen-the-empress); of Helen was born the great Constantine alone, who was also the heir of his father's rule. Constas kept the old Roman religion in appearance but did not force it on anyone, hoping rather in the Most High God, and he taught his son to look for help to the providence above and not to the idols. He pitied the Christians whom the other emperors tortured and killed, and defended the Church from persecution, so that in the West the Christians had rest.

Wishing to know which of his men were steady in faith, Constas called his whole court together and said that anyone who wanted a place in his palace must sacrifice to his gods, and anyone who would not could go where he pleased. The court divided. Those who were truly servants of Christ stepped aside, left their offices and their high ranks, and began to leave the palace; those who loved this world and its glory more than Christ agreed and went to the idols. Then the emperor stopped the first and made them his counsellors and his friends, saying that men faithful to their God would be faithful to him, and drove the others out in shame.

Constantine took the empire on his father's death by the consent of the whole army. The Romans secretly sent to him in Britain, where he was living with his mother Helen, begging him to come and free them from the tyrant Maxentius. Seeing how great the demonic power on Maxentius's side was, he prayed to the true God for a sign of victory, and at midday there appeared to him the figure of the Cross drawn in stars brighter than the sun, with the words written above it: «Cu acesta vei birui» — by this you shall conquer. His soldiers saw it too, and their commander was that Artemius who was afterwards tortured for Christ by Julian.

Rising, he told his nobles the vision, called skilled craftsmen and had the honourable Cross made after the pattern of the sign, of gold and pearls and precious stones, and ordered the whole army to put the sign of the Cross on their weapons, their standards, their helmets and their shields. The Cross was carried before his ranks, and Maxentius was beaten and the multitude of his soldiers cut down. His second war was against Byzantium, then a small city; twice defeated and in great grief, he lifted his eyes at evening and saw a writing made of stars, and again a cross of stars with the same words about it, and so took the city.

Of his baptism the page tells a long story: the emperor was struck with leprosy, and the priests of the idols told him to bathe in the blood of infants. Children were gathered at the Capitol, and the weeping of their mothers turned him; he sent them home with gifts rather than kill them, and God repaid that mercy with health of body and soul. The apostles Peter and Paul appeared to him in sleep and sent him to [Sylvester](/saints/sylvester-of-rome), the bishop of Rome, who taught him the mysteries of the faith in the Holy Trinity and baptised him, and he came out of the font clean.

He forbade anyone thereafter to blaspheme Christ or to trouble the Christians, and built a church in his own palace courts; but when the people of Rome wanted to drive out everyone who would not be a Christian, he would not have it, and left each man to live in his own faith and by his own will. He meant to build a city of his own name at Ilium, where the Trojan war is said to have been fought, but was forbidden by a divine warning and told to build rather at Byzantium; so he raised there a great and glorious city, called it Constantinople, moved his throne to it from old Rome and had it called the New Rome.

When Arius troubled the Church with his heresy, Constantine set himself to examine the matter of the faith, and ordered the great council of the whole world to be held at Nicaea, where 318 holy Fathers set out the right dogmas and put Arius and his heresy under anathema. That council, the page says, was held in the year 325 and was the first of the whole world. He then sent his mother Helen to Jerusalem with much wealth to seek the honourable Cross.

He lived ten years and more after her death, and set out to war against the Persians; but in a village of Nicomedia he fell ill, and knowing his end near he made his will and divided the empire among his three sons. He gave up his soul into the hands of Christ, and was carried to Constantinople and buried with glory in the church of the Holy Apostles, in the thirty-second year of his reign and the sixty-fifth of his life. The page gives no year for his birth or his death.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and sets down what it does and does not know. saint.gr reports Tarsus of Cilicia and Drepanum of Bithynia as his birthplace and then says the prevailing view puts it at Naissus in Upper Moesia, the modern Niš; it says the exact year is unknown and is thought to fall between 272 and 288; and it makes his father Constantius, called Chlorus for the pallor of his face, a kinsman of the emperor Claudius, and his mother the daughter of an innkeeper of Drepanum. In 305 it finds him at Diocletian's court at Nicomedia with the rank of chiliarch. Before the battle with Maxentius, on Eusebius's account, he did not know which God to pray to, and while he prayed he saw in the sky about the sun the trophy of the Cross with «τούτῳ νίκα» written on it, and in the night the Lord appeared to him with the same sign and told him to have a copy of it made and to carry it as a protection in his wars.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 21 May together with his mother, and tells the beginning and the end that the other books leave out. His parents, it says, were the emperor Constantius Flor and the empress Helen; Flor had other children by another wife, but of Helen he had only this one son. Three great wars he had when he came to the throne: one against Maxentius, the tyrant at Rome, one against the Scythians on the Danube, and one against the Byzantines. Before the battle with Maxentius, when he was in great anxiety and in doubt of his own success, a most shining cross appeared to him by day in the sky, all hung about with stars, and on the cross stood written: by this conquer.

Astonished, the emperor had a great cross forged like the one that had appeared to him, to be carried before the army; and by the power of the cross he won a famous victory over an enemy far more numerous than his own, and Maxentius drowned in the river Tiber. Straight afterwards he issued the famous Edict at Milan, in 313, that the persecutions of the Christians should cease; and having conquered the Byzantines he built a fine capital city on the Bosphorus, which from then was called Constantinople.

But before that, the Prologue says, he fell into a heavy leprosy. The pagan priests and the physicians advised him to bathe in the blood of slaughtered children as a cure, and he refused it. Then the apostles Peter and Paul appeared to him and told him to seek out bishop [Sylvester](/saints/sylvester-of-rome), who would heal him of the dreadful sickness; and the bishop taught him the Christian faith and baptised him, and the leprosy went from the emperor’s body. When the discord arose in the Church through the troublesome heretic Arius, he called the First Ecumenical Council at Nicaea in 325, where the heresy was condemned and Orthodoxy confirmed.

He outlived his mother by ten years, the entry closes, and reposed in his sixty-fifth year, in the city of Nicomedia; and his body was buried in the church of the Holy Apostles at Constantinople. The book gives her death a year and gives his none, so none is set down here.
