# Sebaldus of Nuremberg

Doxologia.ro opens by calling Sebaldus a hermit who lived in the eighth century and spent most of his life in the woods round Nuremberg, reckoned for his preaching and his miracles the apostle of that city of northern Bavaria, whose great church in the centre of the town bears his name and holds his shrine, and was for centuries a place of pilgrimage. It then heads its account unclear origins, and says so plainly: the first documentary mention of the hermit who worked a miracle at Nuremberg is in the Annales Augustani for 1070, and in 1072 the monk Lambert of Hersfeld attests that he was venerated there and that many received his help. The later mentions, centuries apart, string together many incidents that often do not agree chronologically, and make an exact historical account particularly difficult.

The memory of his life was carried down the generations in local tellings, written up in various forms. The oldest biographies place his origin in England or Scotland and make him a contemporary of Willibald (†787) and Wunibald (†761), two great missionaries honoured in Bavaria. Konrad Sauer's thirteenth-century Ea war ain kunek opens by setting him in the days when Leo and Constantine were emperors and Pippin and Charles reigned in France; Sigmund Meisterlin's Legenda nova St. Sebaldi (1484–1488) and the Old Legend of Nuremberg, kept on two parchment rolls in his church, make him instead a son of the king of Denmark. His name is read as strong or brave at sea, or else as bold victory.

The fullest of these biographies says he was drawn to holy things from childhood, left Denmark at fifteen to study at Paris, and, married by force to a maiden his parents had chosen, fled on his wedding night as Alexis the Man of God had done, and lived sixteen years as a hermit. With Willibald, Wunibald and Walpurga he gave himself to asceticism and chose the life of a wandering monk. After a pilgrimage to Rome he and his disciple Dionysius met Pope Gregory II (715–731), who sent him to bring the pagan tribes of Germany to Christ.

He preached first in Lombardy, where the account says he saved his old friends Willibald and Wunibald from death by starvation with bread received from angels and a small cask of wine that never emptied. He stayed for a time in a hermitage near Vicenza, where people gathered to hear him, and there disputed with a heretic, perhaps an Arian, whom he answered both with arguments and with a wonder, the earth opening and swallowing his adversary until the saint prayed for his release.

Crossing the Alps into the German lands, he could find no way over the Danube near Regensburg and used his sheepskin coat as a boat. After several marvels in that city he went further north into Franconia and settled in a wood in the Nordgau, in the area of the present city of Nuremberg, beside the Pegnitz; the place long kept the name Sebaldiwald. There he lived withdrawn, in severe asceticism, prayer and vigil, but welcomed those who came to hear him or to ask his help. He once told a peasant who had lost his oxen at nightfall to go into the wood with his hands raised, and light came from his fingers as if it were day, and the animals were found.

He often came into the town and lodged at the inn of a local carter, where the chair in which he heard confessions was long kept, which the page takes to mean that he was a priest. One winter he asked his host for a fire, and when there was no wood, or none to be spared, he told the man's wife to put icicles on the hearth, and they burned and warmed the house. Another time a man who had been caught trading fish against the town's law and blinded by order of the authorities came at last to the hermit, who healed his blindness.

The biography says he spent a long time enlightening the local people in the right faith and foresaw his end. According to his wish, his body was laid after his death on an ox-cart, to be buried where the oxen should stop; they stopped before the chapel of Saint Peter at Poppenreuth, a church consecrated in 746 by Saint Boniface, the enlightener of Germany.

The townspeople venerated him from his lifetime. After the wooden chapel burned in a lightning strike his reliquary was moved for a time to the Scottish monastery of Saint Giles in the town, then to the church of Saint Peter near the imperial castle, which in 1230 was dedicated to Saint Sebaldus and was replaced by the present church built between 1361 and 1372. About 1250 an unknown author composed the office Os justi for him, and in 1256 Bishop Heinrich von Bilversheim of Bamberg granted an indulgence for visiting his tomb on three days of the year. In the eleventh century pilgrims came to his reliquary even from France, to pray for healing.

The silver reliquary made in 1397 holds two caskets with eighteen and ninety-one fragments of relics; a procession with it round the church is recorded in 1401. On 26 March 1425 Pope Martin V, at the townspeople's request, confirmed his official veneration, which was then kept in Nuremberg for eight days. The great monument made to hold the silver shrine was finished by Peter Vischer and his five sons in 1519 after thirteen years' work, its base carrying scenes from the saint's life. Though the Reformation forbade the cult in 1525, the shrine was kept intact in the church, which had by then become Protestant, because of the many miracles that took place there; and he was invoked particularly by women in childbirth, and by travellers to Rome and at sea. Doxologia.ro prints no troparion for him.
