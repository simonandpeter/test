# Ambrose (Polyansky)

In the world he was Alexander Alekseevich Polyansky, born on 12 November 1878 in the village of Petelino in the Yelatma district of Tambov province, into a priest's family; the Polyanskys had been priests for generations. He had his first schooling in the parish school of his own village, and at nine his father sent him to the ecclesiastical school at Shatsk. From there he went to the Tambov seminary, finished it in 1899 and entered the Kazan Theological Academy the same year. He was tonsured in 1901 with the name Ambrose and made hierodeacon, and ordained hieromonk in 1902.

In 1903 he left the academy a candidate of theology, was appointed a teacher at the Kyiv seminary and enrolled among the brotherhood of the Kyiv Caves Lavra; in 1906 he was made rector of that same seminary and raised to archimandrite. The revolutionary disorder of those years reached the seminary too, and his life prints the inspectors' own account of the riot of 29 March 1907 in full — the whistling and the shouting that went on for two hours in a corridor whose lights had been put out, and the deputation that came to the rector at midnight with four demands. He worked steadily at relief for students with no money behind them, and in 1915 was given the order of Saint Vladimir of the third class.

The question of making him a bishop was raised more than once, and each time the metropolitan of Kyiv, Flavian (Gorodetsky), answered «Он мне нужен» — I need him. He was consecrated bishop of Vinnitsa on 22 October 1918 and moved to the see of Kamenets-Podolsk and Bratslav in 1922, where he had little time to serve. The Renovationist archbishop Pimen (Pegov) invited him into the Renovationist organisation; he refused, the Renovationists reported the refusal to the OGPU, and he was arrested on a charge of sheltering former tsarist officers by ordaining them priests — men who had in fact left the army long before and were working as schoolmasters.

Exiled from Ukraine in 1923, he settled in Moscow, and at the gathering of twenty-seven bishops in the Donskoy monastery that September he was the one who asked aloud why an archbishop who had joined the schismatics was still being called a most eminent metropolitan. He was sentenced to three years and sent to the Solovki camp with Procopius (Titov), archbishop of Kherson; the two were told in November 1928 that three years' exile in the Urals would follow the camp, and they travelled it together through Yekaterinburg and Tobolsk. In the villages of the far north they went on serving the liturgy to a few people and writing to the imprisoned locum tenens, and that, his life says, was the real reason for the next arrest.

On 14 December 1931 the OGPU sentenced him and Procopius to three years' exile in Kazakhstan. He reached Turkestan at the beginning of September 1932, where exiled nuns of the Martha and Mary convent took him in; the OGPU then ordered him a hundred and twenty kilometres further, across the desert, to the small village of Suzak. One of the novices went to beg the chief of the OGPU to leave an elderly bishop in the town, and was refused. The sun burned him on the road and he barely reached the place; he went into hospital at once, and a week later he died. He was glorified at the Jubilee Council of Bishops in August 2000.
