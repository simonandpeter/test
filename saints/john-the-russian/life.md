# John the Russian

John was born in a village in Russia at the end of the seventeenth century, in the reign of Peter the Great. Doxologia gives 1690 as the most probable year, reasoning from his having served as a soldier in the war the tsar made against the Turks in 1711. Taken prisoner by the Tatars in the fighting for Azov, he was sold to a senior Turkish officer who was eparch in Prokopi — today Ürgüp in Cappadocia, near Caesarea — and was carried home with him.

Turkey was then full of Russian captives, and the life says that most of them, to lighten their suffering a little, denied Christ and became Muslims. John did not. Beaten, struck and spat upon, the target of the janissaries in the camp at Prokopi, he told his master: if you leave me free in my faith, I will do your orders diligently; if you force me to change my faith, I will hand you my head sooner than my faith. A Christian I was born and a Christian I shall die.

Set to look after the cattle, he laid his tired body down in a corner of the stable and thanked God for being counted worthy to follow the humility of Christ, who was born in a poor manger near Bethlehem. When in time a small room beside the stable was given him he refused it and went on sleeping among the animals. His nights were hours of prayer and prostrations, little sleep curled on the bare floor under an old cloth, long fasts on a little bread and water, and the psalms of David said in the Russian his mother had taught him.

He would go secretly at night to a church of Saint George built in a ravine not far from his master's house, keep vigil standing in its porch, and go there every Saturday to receive the Holy Mysteries. God gave his master much wealth, and the man knew where the blessing on his house had come from and told everyone of his servant's virtues.

When the agha went on pilgrimage to Mecca, his wife set a feast for her husband's kin and friends, and John served at it. She said aloud how glad her husband would have been to taste the pilaf he loved; John asked for a plateful to send him at Mecca, and the guests laughed. He took it to the stable, knelt, and asked God to send it by whatever means He willed, and the plate vanished from his sight. Days later the agha came home carrying the copper plate, and said that on the day of the feast he had found it full of steaming pilaf in his locked room, with his own name stamped on the rim as on all the vessels of his house.

After that nobody dared trouble him. His master and mistress pressed on him the little room he had refused, and he took it and kept the same manner of life, tending the animals by day and spending the nights in prayer and psalms. A few years later he fell ill, asked to be laid on straw in the stable he had hallowed, and knew the hour of his departure beforehand. The priest, fearing the fanaticism of the Turks, brought him the Mysteries hidden in a hollowed apple; he communicated and gave up his soul. This was on 27 May 1730. Christians, Muslims and Armenians together carried him to the Christian cemetery: the man who had been a slave was buried as a master.

In November 1733 the old priest who had communed him saw him in a dream and was told that his body lay whole and incorrupt; a heavenly light like a pillar of fire stood over the grave, and the relics were taken up and carried to the church of Saint George. In 1832 Osman Pasha, passing through Prokopi, plundered the church and threw the relics into the fire; seeing the body stir in the flames as though alive, the Turks fled, and the next day the Christians found it whole, blackened only by the embers, as doxologia says it remains. After the conflict of 1922 the Greeks of Asia Minor carried the relics away with them, and since the new church was finished in 1951 they have rested in the new Prokopi on Euboea.

The Russian church calendar keeps him on 27 мая ст. ст. and gives him two readings. He was born at the end of the seventeenth century in Little Russia, served as a soldier in the army of Peter I and took part in the Russo-Turkish war, and was taken prisoner. The Turks tried without success to turn him to the religion of Mahomet; and his master, seeing the prisoner’s fearlessness, left off tormenting him and forcing him to renounce Orthodoxy, and put him in charge of the cattle. His bed was set in the stall. Those about him often mocked him when they saw how hard he worked, and he not only went on working but helped his masters at their own work when the chance came and comforted them in trouble.

His good heart won the head of the house, who came to trust him and to respect his honesty so far that he offered to let him live as a free man; and the ascetic chose to stay in the stable, where nobody hindered him from praying to God every night. Sometimes in the night he came to pray at the church of the great-martyr George, and at the feasts he communicated there. Poor as he was, he always helped those in need. Once, while the master was travelling in far countries, his relatives grieved that he had no share in the festal meal of the family; and at the saint’s secret prayer a dish of pilaf was carried to the master by a wonder from the family table — the dish off the family service, and the pilaf still hot.

His righteous end came on 27 мая 1730, after he had partaken of the holy Mysteries of Christ. The master called the priests, and all the Christians living in Prokopi came to the burial. Three and a half years later his incorrupt relics were found and carried into the church of the great-martyr George; and the new saint came to be honoured not by the Orthodox alone but by the Armenians and the Turks. In 1881 a part of the relics was taken to the Russian Panteleimon monastery on Athos, and another part was placed in 1924 in the church of his own name on the island of Euboea, where it is to this day, and pilgrims come to it from all over Greece. Many, the calendar says, speak of him as a man of prayer and an intercessor for the Russian land.
