# Marcellus

Marcellus was born of noble parents in Cyprus. Well educated, he held high civil offices, astonishing everyone by the purity of his life, his meekness, mercy and eloquence, and around 375, leaving his wife and children, he gave himself to the solitary monastic life in Syria. The people of Apamea, bringing him to the city on the pretext of some useful business, elected him their bishop. He was zealous against paganism: Theodoret of Cyrus tells that, having leave from the emperor Theodosius the Great to destroy the massively built temple of Jupiter at Apamea, he did not know how to go about it, until a workman undermined three of its huge columns, propped them for the time with olive wood and then set the wood alight — and it would not burn; Marcellus, told of this, performed the lesser blessing of water in church at the holy altar and had the wood sprinkled with it in faith, and it burned quickly, the columns fell and brought the whole temple down with them.

When soldiers were destroying another temple near Aulon in the Apamean country, the pagans seized the bishop, who was watching from a distance, and threw him into the fire, about 389. The murderers were found, and his sons wished to avenge him; the local council forbade it, judging it wrong to avenge a death for which God should be thanked. The Prologue, which tells it for the Serbian calendar, finds it instructive that his life records the blessing of water and the use of the water blessed.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same menologion day and gives his life before the see. saint.gr says the hieromartyr Marcellus lived in the time of the emperor Theodosius I, the Great; that he came of Cyprus, where he received a civil dignity, the governance of the island being entrusted to him; and that in the discharge of those worldly duties he tried always to be just, which astonished everyone on the island. Later he became a priest, and after that bishop of Apamea in Syria. As a shepherd, the page says, he was correct and just, and tried to keep continually near the troubles of his flock.

He showed himself besides a warm zealot for the faith in Christianity, raising many churches and at the same time pulling down many idol temples. One of these would not come down by any means, so he threw holy water upon it; and at once that temple, which was dedicated to Zeus, caught fire and disappeared. On the occasion of that event they arrested him and threw him into the furnace, and in that way Saint Marcellus gave up his spirit to the Lord. The couplet printed above the notice puts the two fires together: «Κνίσσαις νοηταῖς τὸν Θεὸν καθηδύνας, / Καὶ σαρκὸς αὐτὸν ἡδύνεις κνίσσῃ Πάτερ» — having pleased God with spiritual savours, you please him, father, with the savour of your flesh as well. The page prints no hymn to him.
