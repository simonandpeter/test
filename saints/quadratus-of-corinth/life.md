# Quadratus of Corinth

In the days of Decius (249–251) and of Valerian (253–259) there was a great persecution of Christians, and many of the faithful, fearing torments past bearing, left their cities, their houses and their goods and hid in the wilderness, in the mountains and in the clefts of the earth, choosing to live among the beasts rather than among the idolaters, so that they might keep their faith in Christ unspotted. In those hard times a believing woman of Corinth named Rufina fled the city; and being with child when she fled, her days were fulfilled and she bore a son there in the wilderness, and died a few days after.

God did not overlook the orphan. The life says he commanded his clouds from above, and they came down and poured sweet dew into the child's mouth, feeding him as with milk or honey until he could feed himself on the herbs of the wilderness. The boy lived in the desert as John the Baptist did, kept by God and taught by the Holy Spirit; and when he was still a child some believing men found him and took him into the city, where he learned letters and the art of medicine, and healed every sickness — not by earthly medicine but by the gift given him from above.

Afterwards he went back from the company of men and their quarrels to the silence he had been used to from infancy, and spent most of his years in the mountains and the wilderness, coming into the city now and then for men's needs: with medicine he healed the ills of the body, and with the word of God the weaknesses of the soul. Those who loved him in Christ came out to him in the desert; such a one was Cyprian, and with him Dionysius, Anectus, Paul and Crescens, who suffered with him for Christ.

From Decius, the pagan emperor of Rome, there came to Corinth a governor named Jason, to torment and kill the Christians. Brought before him, Quadratus was asked why he should give himself up of his own will to such torments; and he answered that no man of natural understanding rejects this sweet life, but that since God gave it the Giver must be loved the more, and that the honour due to God must not be given to idols for fear of losing it.

Pressed to say what they held about Christ, he answered that the beginning of the world's making was by the will of the Father, accomplished by his Word, who is the Son, and established by the power of the Holy Spirit; that man, set in paradise, fell through the envy of the devil; and that the Word was pleased in the fullness of time to dwell by incarnation in the most pure womb of the Virgin Mary, the Theotokos, and to be born God in the flesh. This, he said, is the Christ we preach, who overthrew the ordinances of death and brought our forefathers out of hell.

The governor called it a lie, and Quadratus said that the mysteries of the right faith are not for unbelievers to probe, and that neither cunning devices nor angry threats would draw them away from Christ. He was beaten naked with staves, then hung head downward, his body torn with iron claws and a fire kindled beneath him; and he bore it all and was not overcome.

Jason turned then to Cyprian, who, though still young in years, made ready for the torments without fear; and Quadratus spoke to him and to the others who were stripping off their clothes for the scourges, telling them what good things were laid up for them from the Lord, that the time of the contest was now at hand, that they should hold one faith and one confession as men who must stand before God at the dread judgement, and that they should not spare the flower of their youth, being about to pass over to a life that does not grow old.

At that the governor had Cyprian hung up naked and tormented as Quadratus had been, with beating, with tearing and with fire, and after him Dionysius, and after him Anectus, then Paul, and last of all Crescens. Beaten by them and put to shame, he condemned them first to the beasts; and when the beasts would not touch them he gave them to the sword, having them dragged bound by the feet through the city, where a crowd beyond number, and children most of all, beat them with sticks and with stones.

Outside the city they asked a little time, prayed earnestly to the Lord, and bowed their holy heads under the sword on the tenth day of March; and in the place where their heads were struck off, where the ground was reddened with their blood, a spring of clear water ran, for an unforgotten memorial of the martyrs of Corinth. The life goes on to name many other Christians of that city killed afterwards under the governors Tertius and Venustus, and among them another Dionysius, stabbed with a knife.

The six who suffered together that day are Quadratus, [Cyprian](/saints/cyprian-companion-of-quadratus), [Dionysius](/saints/dionysius-companion-of-quadratus), Anectus, Paul and Crescens; doxologia's calendar for 10 martie prints three of them as lines of its own, and this folder is Quadratus, whom the life calls their guide and teacher.
