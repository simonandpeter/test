# Eudocia of Heliopolis

In the days of the emperor Trajan there lived at Heliopolis in Coele-Syria, in the dominion of Phoenicia of Lebanon, a Samaritan woman named Eudocia, whose beauty no painter could set down whole. Young men of good family and rulers from other cities came to Heliopolis as though on business, but in truth to see her; and by that beauty she drew many to ruin, and gathered the wealth of their countries into treasuries the life calls unjust.

A monk named Germanus lodged in the house beside hers, and rose in the night to chant the psalms and to read of the judgement to come. Eudocia woke and listened through the wall until the reading was finished, and could not sleep for the rest of the night. In the morning she sent for him; he told her that nobody goes into a bath to wash and then puts his filth back on, and that she must first cast off her uncleanness. A priest of the city was called to her, and taught her, and she prayed and fasted seven days.

She was baptized by the bishop, set her slaves free, and gave away her gold and her lands to the Church and the poor; then she entered the women's monastery that Germanus served. After thirteen months its abbess Charitina died. Philostratus, one of her former lovers, came to the gate in a monk's habit to call her back to her old life; she breathed in his face and he fell down dead, and afterwards, at her prayer, she raised him and sent him home in peace.

The emperor whom the life calls Aurelian — not, it says expressly, the Caesar of Rome of those years — sent soldiers for her gold, and for three days they could see the walls of the monastery and could not come near them; his son went with a greater force and died, and in the end that emperor believed in Christ and was baptized by the bishop of the city. Later Diogen, governor of Heliopolis, seized her and tortured her; the tribune who hung her up was burned by a fire out of heaven, and she raised him and others from the dead, among them Firmina the wife of Diodor, and a young man whom a serpent had killed.

She lived about fifty-six years after her baptism. Diogen died a Christian, and the governor who came in his place, Vichentie, an angry man and an enemy of Christians, heard of her and sent soldiers, who cut off her head. So, the life says, she ended by the sword in the month of March, on the first day.
