# Eudocia of Heliopolis

In the days of the emperor Trajan there lived at Heliopolis in Coele-Syria, in the dominion of Phoenicia of Lebanon, a Samaritan woman named Eudocia, whose beauty no painter could set down whole. Young men of good family and rulers from other cities came to Heliopolis as though on business, but in truth to see her; and by that beauty she drew many to ruin, and gathered the wealth of their countries into treasuries the life calls unjust.

A monk named Germanus lodged in the house beside hers, and rose in the night to chant the psalms and to read of the judgement to come. Eudocia woke and listened through the wall until the reading was finished, and could not sleep for the rest of the night. In the morning she sent for him; he told her that nobody goes into a bath to wash and then puts his filth back on, and that she must first cast off her uncleanness. A priest of the city was called to her, and taught her, and she prayed and fasted seven days.

She was baptized by the bishop, set her slaves free, and gave away her gold and her lands to the Church and the poor; then she entered the women's monastery that Germanus served. After thirteen months its abbess Charitina died. Philostratus, one of her former lovers, came to the gate in a monk's habit to call her back to her old life; she breathed in his face and he fell down dead, and afterwards, at her prayer, she raised him and sent him home in peace.

The emperor whom the life calls Aurelian — not, it says expressly, the Caesar of Rome of those years — sent soldiers for her gold, and for three days they could see the walls of the monastery and could not come near them; his son went with a greater force and died, and in the end that emperor believed in Christ and was baptized by the bishop of the city. Later Diogen, governor of Heliopolis, seized her and tortured her; the tribune who hung her up was burned by a fire out of heaven, and she raised him and others from the dead, among them Firmina the wife of Diodor, and a young man whom a serpent had killed.

She lived about fifty-six years after her baptism. Diogen died a Christian, and the governor who came in his place, Vichentie, an angry man and an enemy of Christians, heard of her and sent soldiers, who cut off her head. So, the life says, she ended by the sword in the month of March, on the first day.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and tells the conversion at more length. saint.gr puts her birth at Heliopolis in the province of Libanesia, in Phoenicia, in the time of Trajan, and says she spent her first years in sin, drawing many men after her by a dazzling beauty and gathering much money by it. After a grave illness she left the city, and came back a year later wanting to stay unknown, and settled at its edge; there she met a monk named Germanus, and his counsels brought her to repentance. A vision followed — an angel of God leading her up towards heaven and other angels rejoicing with her, while a black figure howled that it was greatly wronged because Eudocia had become a Christian — and she went to the bishop Theodotus and was baptised. She gave the whole of her property to the charitable work of the local church and entered a monastery, until her former lovers seized her and brought her before Aurelian to be tried. There, the page says, she raised the king's dead child by her prayer and drew him too to Christianity; later she was brought before the governor Diogenes, who set her free after a second wonder; and in the end she was beheaded by Vincentius.

The Serbian church keeps her on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović sets her at Heliopolis in Phoenicia in the reign of Trajan: first a great debauchee, then a penitent, then an ascetic and at the last a martyr, who had gathered an enormous fortune by her debauchery. The turn in her life was made, by God's providence, by an old monk named Germanus, and made unintentionally. He came into the city on business and lodged with a Christian whose house touched hers; and when he began at night, as monks do, to read the Psalter and a book about the Dread Judgment, she heard him and listened attentively to his words to the end. Fear and horror took hold of her, so that she stayed awake until dawn.

At first light she sent her servants to beg the monk to come to her, and when he came a long conversation began between them about what the old man had been reading in the night, and about faith and salvation in general. What came of those conversations was that she asked the local bishop to baptise her. After the baptism she gave her whole estate to the church to be shared out among the poor, dismissed her servants and her slaves, and withdrew to a women's monastery.

She gave herself so decisively to the monastic life — to obedience, patience, vigil, prayer and fasting — that after thirteen months she was chosen abbess. She lived fifty-six years in that monastery, the Prologue says, and was granted such grace before God that she even raised the dead; and when a persecution of Christians came from a prince named Vincentius she was beheaded with the sword. It draws the lesson itself: here is a wonderful example of how a vessel of uncleanness can be cleansed, hallowed and filled with the precious fragrance of heaven.

The Сретенский monastery’s calendar heads the Julian 1 March with her and prints the day’s troparion and kontakion under her name, which this folder now carries: «Прмц. Евдокии (160-170).» It serves two lives of her, and both make her a Samaritan by birth who lived at Heliopolis in Phoenicia and sold her body there, and both turn on her overhearing Scripture read aloud. In the shorter an aged monk named Germanus, who had the habit of reading aloud, came to the place where she lived, and she heard him read the foretelling of the Second Coming and of the Last Judgement, and understood that it was she, the sinner, whom those punishments awaited.

In the longer she woke at midnight and heard, through the wall of the house where a Christian lived, the singing of prayers and a reading of the blessedness laid up for the righteous and the recompense awaiting sinners; in the morning she sent for the man whose rule of prayer she had heard, and it was the elder Germanus on his way home from a pilgrimage. She shut herself up in fasting and repentance, was catechised and then baptised — the longer life says by Theodotus, bishop of Heliopolis — gave all she had to the poor and went into a monastery, where both lives have her stay fifty-six years and be set over it as its superior.

The longer life is a chain of raisings. A young pagan called Philostratus got into the monastery dressed as a monk and urged her back to her old life, and fell dead at her word, and was raised again at her prayer and baptised. The governor Aurelian sent soldiers for the treasure the townsmen said she had hidden, and for three days an unseen power kept them from the walls; his son, sent next, hurt his foot on the first day of the road and died, and was given back alive at her prayer, so that Aurelian and his household were baptised. Later the officer Diodorus, whose wife Firmina had died suddenly, begged her prayer and had his wife back, and he and the governor Diogenes were baptised with their families.

The last of the raisings she did not do herself. A widow’s only son was bitten by a snake in a garden and died, and she told Diodorus that the time had come for him to show his own faith; he thought himself unworthy of such boldness, but obeyed her, prayed, and commanded the dead man to rise in the name of Christ, and the young man got up before everyone there. She went back to her monastery. Under Vincentius, who became governor after Diogenes and persecuted the Christians cruelly, she was beheaded.

The year is where the two lives of one host part, and this folder now records both. The shorter closes «В 152 году, при императоре Антонине, она мученически закончила свою праведную жизнь»; the longer closes «1 марта (ок. 160 - 170) святая преподобномученица была обезглавлена», which is what the day’s own line prints as «(160-170)». Neither is chosen. The shorter life also sets her under Trajan and gives that reign as 89–117, where the Охридски пролог gives Trajan and no years at all and this folder’s floruit takes the reign’s own 98 to 117; the floruit is left as it stood.

One thing the calendar adds and the folder does not take. Both Sretensky lives say in prose that she was made superior of the monastery — «была возведена в игуменьи своей обители» in the shorter, «когда она была уже настоятельницей монастыря» in the longer — and azbyka.ru’s line for the same day names the grade outright, «Прмц. Евдокии Илиопольской, игумении». The rank word on the Sretensky line is only прмц., venerable-martyr, which this folder already holds, and the two hosts do not grade her alike, so nothing is added to `types` and no office is written. The reading is here instead.
