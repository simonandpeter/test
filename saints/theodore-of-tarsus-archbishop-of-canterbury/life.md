# Theodore of Tarsus

Theodore was a Greek of Tarsus, Paul’s own city, and a learned monk living at
Rome when, at sixty-five, he was hurried through the clerical ranks and
consecrated archbishop of Canterbury; Adrian, an African abbot from near
Naples, was sent with him to help. He reached Kent in 669, nearly seventy,
and proved anything but tired. He travelled the whole of England founding
churches and filling the sees the plague had emptied, made new ones, and
opened a school at Canterbury where Greek was taught. In Northumbria he
settled the quarrel over the see — Wilfrid had gone to Gaul for a Roman
consecration and stayed three years, and Chad had been irregularly
consecrated in his absence — by deposing Chad, recognising his worth,
regularising his orders and sending him to Mercia, while Wilfrid was
restored. In 672 he called the whole English church to Hertford, the first
council it had ever held and the first assembly of any kind drawn from the
whole country, and in 679 another at Hatfield to hold the faith against
Monothelitism. He died in 690, and his body lay long incorrupt. The dioceses
he drew still underlie the church’s administration in England, and under
him, the life says, the English church found a unity the kingdoms did not.

The Greek synaxarion keeps him on 19 September in a line — from Tarsus,
archbishop of Canterbury in 668, asleep in 690 — beside Cuthbert, Mildred,
Adrian abbot of Canterbury and Brithwald.
