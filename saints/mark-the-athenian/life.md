# Mark the Athenian

He is called Mark of Thrace in the Ohrid Prologue's heading, after the mountain he lived on, and the entry says he is also called the Athenian, because Athens was his birthplace. When he had finished the higher schools at Athens his parents died; and thinking to himself that death was unavoidable for him too and that a man should prepare in good time for an honourable departure out of this world, he gave away the whole of his property to the poor. Then he sat down on a plank in the sea, with a firm faith in God's help and a prayer that God would take him wherever it was His will; and God, who provides, kept him and brought him to Libya, or Ethiopia — the entry is unsure which — to a mountain called Thracian.

On that mountain Mark struggled for ninety-five years, seeing neither a man nor a beast. For thirty full years he waged a terrible war with evil spirits and was tormented with hunger and thirst, with frost and with burning heat; he ate earth and drank sea water. After those thirty years of the fiercest suffering the demons were beaten and fled from him, and an angel of God began to bring him food every day — bread, fish and fruit.

Just before his death he was visited by Saint Serapion, who afterwards published the wonderful life of this Mark. Mark asked Serapion whether there were now any Christians in the world who, if they said to this mountain, rise up from here and throw yourself into the sea, would see it happen; and in that hour the mountain they were on began to move like the sea. Mark waved his hand and stopped it. Such wonderworking power this man of God had. Before his death he prayed for the salvation of men and gave up his soul to God, and Saint Serapion saw angels taking Mark's soul and a hand stretched out from heaven which received it. He lived a hundred and thirty years, the entry ends, and reposed about the year 400.

The Prologue is the only source read for him. The Serapion of the story is given no see, no monastery and no by-name and is identified here with nobody; the hundred and thirty years are not turned into a year of birth, because the year the entry gives for the death is itself approximate.

The corpus holds a second man who answers to part of this. [Mark the Ascetic](/saints/mark-the-ascetic), kept by the Romanian and the Greek on 5 March, is called an Athenian on his Greek page too — a man of the fourth century who left Athens for Antioch, studied under a famous teacher there, and lived out his life in the desert of Nitria; and that folder's own entry records that the Greek calendar's same day carries a second page, for a Mark the Athenian, which says the information about him is confused and that he may perhaps be that same man. The two lives meet nowhere else: there is no Antioch here, no teacher, no Nitria and no hyena, and no ninety-five years alone on a mountain in Libya there. **Whether they are one man is not decided in this folder**, which is the same answer the other folder gives, and the Greek calendar's own word for it is that the information is confused.

**The Russian church calendar keeps him on the Julian 5 April**, the same number the Ohrid Prologue keeps, and its line is «Прп. Марка Афинского, Фраческого (400).» — venerable Mark of Athens, of Thrace, 400. The year is printed flat here where the Prologue printed it as approximate; both readings are now in the folder and the date is untouched. The name link, page 1458, gives «Марк Афинянин, Фраческий, прп.», the two epithets together. Its life is told as the account Serapion published, and it agrees with the Prologue on the shape of the thing and differs on the road: Mark studied philosophy in his youth, and after his parents died withdrew to Egypt and settled in a cave of the Thracian mountain, which this book places in Ethiopia. There is no plank and no sea voyage here.

Ninety-five years he lived there, and in all that time saw not only no human face but neither beast nor bird. The first thirty were the hardest: barefoot and unclothed he suffered cold in winter and burning heat in summer, ate the rare desert plants and sometimes dust, and drank bitter sea water, while unclean spirits swore to drown him in the sea and dragged him down from the mountain crying that nobody had ever come there since the beginning of the world. After the thirty years grace came down on him, angels brought him food, and long hair grew on his body that sheltered him from cold and heat. He told Serapion that he had seen the likeness of the divine paradise and in it the prophets «Илию и Еноха», and that the Lord had shown him everything he asked.

His questions were about the world: whether it stood in the law of Christ, whether Christians were still persecuted; and hearing that idol-worship had stopped he rejoiced and asked whether there were saints now working wonders as the Gospel says. While he spoke the mountain moved five thousand cubits from its place — the page glosses it as about two and a half kilometres — and drew towards the sea, and Mark said to it that he had not ordered it to move but was speaking with his brother, and told it to stand in its place, and it did. Serapion fell on his face in fear, and Mark took him by the hand and asked whether he had not seen such wonders in his life; and hearing that he had not, Mark wept bitterly and said: woe to the earth, because those who live on it are Christians in name and not in deeds.

Then he asked his guest to the meal an angel brought, and Serapion said that in all his life he had never eaten food so good or drunk water so sweet; and Mark answered that God sent him one loaf and one fish every day, and had doubled the table today for his guest’s sake. Before the end he prayed for the salvation of men and of the earth and of all who live on it in the peace and love of Christ, told Serapion to bury him in the cave and block the entrance, and Serapion witnessed the soul of the hundred-and-thirty-year-old elder received into heaven. Two angels in the shape of hermits then led Serapion into the inner desert to a great elder, «Иоанну», and it was to the brethren of that monastery that he told the life and the end.
