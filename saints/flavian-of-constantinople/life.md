# Flavian

Flavian was patriarch of Constantinople after Proclus, and a contemporary of Leo, pope of Rome. Like Leo he fought with resolve against the heresy of Eutyches and Dioscorus; but he did not live to see the triumph of Orthodoxy at the Fourth Ecumenical Council, held at Chalcedon in 451. Before that council, at the second council of Ephesus in 449 — the one history calls the Robber Council — he was savagely beaten and trampled underfoot, and three days later he died of it.

That is the whole of what doxologia's page prints for him, and it ends by calling him a great soldier of Christ the Emperor, a brave defender and a confessor of Orthodoxy. The page carries no troparion for him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and sets out the council that killed him. He had been a presbyter of the Archbishopric of Constantinople and skeuophylax of the church of Holy Wisdom, saint.gr says, and being known for his virtues and his spiritual gifts he succeeded [Proclus](/saints/proclus-of-constantinople) on the archiepiscopal throne of the city in 447. In 448, with a local synod, he condemned the error of the archimandrite Eutyches, who maintained that Christ had the divine nature only and that it had absorbed the human; and when Eutyches persisted in the error the synod deposed and excommunicated him. Having political support, however, and the ill-believing patriarch of Alexandria Dioscorus with him, Eutyches procured the convening of a synod at Ephesus in August 449 — the one called the robber council — where everything was arranged beforehand against Flavian. A mob was even recruited, with monks at its head and under the leadership of a monster in a cassock, the archimandrite Barsumas, and they broke into the synod and savagely beat the saint, who three days later succumbed to the wounds his murderers had given him. Two years afterwards, in 451, the Fourth Ecumenical Council condemned the heresy, anathematised Eutyches and deposed Dioscorus; and the saint's body was translated with great honours to Constantinople by [Pulcheria](/saints/pulcheria-the-empress), sister of Theodosius the Younger, and placed in the church of the Holy Apostles. The page closes with a note about the day: until the twelfth century, it says, St Flavian was kept on 18 February. Its couplet has him not strike his feet on the spiritual stumps, but run all the way to God.

The same Greek day prints a second Flavian, an anchorite of sixty years in a mountain cell whom this corpus keeps as [Flavian the Recluse](/saints/flavian-the-recluse). He is not this man, and neither page joins them.
