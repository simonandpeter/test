# Boniface of Germany

Boniface was born in the year 672 in the village of Crescent, in the region of Devon in the south-west of England, and at his baptism received the name Winfrith, or Wynfreth, which means "friend of peace". Inclined from childhood to the religious life, he entered the monastery of Exeter young, after persuading his father not to oppose him — his father having wished to keep him as sole heir of the family property — and was at once loved by the whole community.

He was remarkable there for his eagerness to learn. His biographer Willibald writes that he was endowed with a spark of the divine gift, which he tended so persistently by study that every hour and every moment of his long and tireless life served only to multiply the divine gifts poured out upon him. After some years he went to the Benedictine monastery of Nursling, made his monastic vows, perfected his Latin and became a noted teacher of Latin grammar and of poetry. At the canonical age of thirty he was ordained priest; he wrote the first grammar of Latin in English, and several poems.

When it seemed he would have a brilliant scholarly career, the longing to confess Christ before those who had not heard of him took fire in him. In 716 he went for the first time on mission to Frisia, in what is now Holland, where a tongue close to the English of that day was spoken. The work there had been begun some thirty years before, but the people had gone back to their pagan practices, and this first mission had no success; he returned to the monastery of his repentance, because, as his biographer later put it, "God had not yet sent rain upon the earth".

After two years he set out again with the blessing of his bishop, Daniel of Winchester, stopping first at Rome to pray in the church of Saint Peter, where he stayed many days in fasting and prayer and spoke with Pope Gregory II. The pope changed his name from Winfrith to Boniface, "doer of good", in honour of the holy martyr Boniface of Tarsus in Cilicia, whose day is 19 December, and sent him with letters towards Bavaria and then Thuringia, the country east of the Rhine and north of the Danube.

He worked for a time with Willibrord in Frisia, parted from him for reasons the page says are unknown, and went back to Hesse and Thuringia; having baptised two pagan chieftains he built a small church there. Meeting many difficulties, he asked the pope's counsel by letter and was called to Rome, where on the feast of Saint Andrew, 30 November 722, the pope consecrated him bishop. The territory entrusted to his mission was the whole of Germany east of the Rhine, and he was not obliged to submit to the Frankish bishops, who with few exceptions were then in a sharp state of moral decay.

In the very heart of Hesse, at Geismar, showing great courage, Boniface felled with an axe the oak of Thor, the thunder-god of the old Germans, which was the centre of their idolatrous worship; this was in the year 723, and out of that oak, helped by his disciples, he built a church which he dedicated to the Apostle Peter. A year later his mission had great success. About a hundred and fifty of his letters survive, gathered into a collection by his disciple Lullus, and he called on his English brethren, especially his old pupils from Nursling, and sent them through all the German lands to preach and to raise monasteries; by 732 the foundations of a sound and lasting Church were laid there.

Pope Gregory III gave him the rank of archbishop of all Germany east of the Rhine and the archiepiscopal pallium, with power to consecrate and appoint bishops; on a third journey to Rome, between 737 and 738, he received the office of papal legate. He founded four dioceses — at Regensburg, Freising, Salzburg and Passau — choosing the bishops from among his own disciples, and in 741 the monastery of Altaich and the bishopric of Eichstätt; afterwards three more sees at Buraburg, Erfurt and Würzburg. So he became a true metropolitan of the German lands, though he held no episcopal see of his own, and his disciple Sturm raised for him the monastery of Fulda, in the valley of the river's springs, which with Rome's approval became stauropegial and soon the greatest monastery in Germany.

He was then asked to take up the Frankish Church in its decay. Supported by Pope Zacharias and above all by King Carloman, and with the help of Pepin III, he held the Concilium Germanicum of 743 and, in 745, a synod of the whole Frankish Church, the first after a break of more than eighty years; at that synod Mainz was chosen as the metropolitan see of the German Church. In 751, or by some accounts 749, his standing had grown so great that he anointed Pepin king.

At about seventy-five, having consecrated his disciple Lullus archbishop of Mainz in 752, he went on a last mission into eastern Frisia. While he waited at Dokkum, where he had pitched camp, to baptise those he and his fellow workers had persuaded, he was killed on the day of Pentecost in the year 754 or 755, together with fifty-two others, by marauding pagan warriors. It seems he died holding a Gospel book over his head, wishing to be defended in death by the book he had loved most. This happened on 5 June; on 9 June his bones were laid at the monastery of Fulda, the place of his heart, where they are to this day.
