# Daniel Katounakiotis

Daniel was born at Smyrna in 1844 — 1846, the Romanian life says — the
youngest son, Demetrius, of a large and devout family, and a top graduate of
the Evangelical School of Smyrna. From nineteen he wanted the monastic life,
and on the advice of his confessor, a monk of the Holy Sepulchre, visited
monasteries in the Peloponnese and on Hydra, Tinos, Paros and Icaria, meeting
famous elders; on Paros he was bound in spirit to Arsenios of Paros, who sent
him to the Garden of the Mother of God, to St Panteleimon, then flourishing
as a coenobium. He was tonsured Daniel there in 1866 and made the
monastery’s secretary. Those were the years the Russian monks, still a
minority, set out to carry through the plans of pan-Slavism and take the
monastery’s government, and after the quarrels the Greek monks were forced
out, Daniel with them; and as he left, on the strength of certain slanders,
the Ecumenical Patriarchate punished him unjustly with permanent removal.
His steps took him to Metropolitan Joachim of Thessalonica, the later
Ecumenical Patriarch, who saw the injustice and offered him a house in his
diocese; at St Anastasia the Deliverer from Potions at Vasilika he did much
to raise the monastery’s spiritual life by bringing in the Athonite typikon
for the services, the vigils and the fasts. When his banishment was lifted
he went back to the Mountain and lived five years at Vatopedi, which sent
him to Smyrna on the business of its dependency there; he stayed nine
months, the metropolitan Meletios saw his gifts and offered to make him his
auxiliary bishop, and he refused and went home. In 1881 he chose the desert
of Athos for stillness — Katounakia — and built the hut that became the
hermitage of the Danielaioi.

There he gave himself to the Philokalia, to prayer and strict ascesis, and to
icon-painting, and soon his virtue joined to his experience made him the man
who could spot a delusion, set the deluded right, heal the possessed, and
bring back to the Fathers’ road the Christians who had taken up the
Makrakists’ teaching of the threefold man. From his bare hut high on the
rocks he came to be, in the mind of the Athonite fathers, a rule and measure
of Orthodoxy, the surest test of delusion even for monks far advanced, and
the voice of the Mountain’s own conscience; he wrote treatises and hundreds
of letters to bishops, priests, monks, nuns and lay people on spiritual and
theological questions. He was above all the elder of discernment — between
spirits, and between truth and delusion — and established elders and
confessors asked his guidance: Philotheos Zervakos, Amphilochios Makris of
Patmos. His bond with Nektarios of Pentapolis, whom he helped by letter, was
close, and with the Skiathos writer Alexander Moraitidis, later the monk
Andronikos; and it was he who set the young Francis Kottis — the future
Joseph the Hesychast — and his companion Arsenios on their first steps,
curbing their headlong young zeal and telling them to put themselves under an
elder first, and so keeping them off paths that would have been dangerous.
Guide, physician and liberator of the suffering, the possessed, the deluded
and the troubled, he kept vigil whole nights writing to enlighten and steady
them. His great love was the Lady of the Mountain, and the happiest thing in
his life was to leave it on her birthday, after communion, on 8 September
1929. The Great Lavra wrote to his brotherhood that in him the Holy Mountain
had lost the last holy face of the present Athonite generation; and Eulogios
Kourilas of the Lavra, later metropolitan of Korçë, that he saw no other
Daniel round about, and it frightened him — everyone crossing those trackless
parts had found an oasis at Katounakia, and now that consolation too was
gone. The Greek calendar keeps him with a full service, and its verse says
the Christ he hymned on Athos in ascesis he now hymns with his lips in
heaven.
