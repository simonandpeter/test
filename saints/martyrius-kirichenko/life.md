# Martyrius (Kirichenko)

Martyrius — Matthew Kirichenko in the world — was registered a townsman
of Sudzha in Kursk province, but his real home was the settlement of
Basovka, where his parents lived, devout people of some means; his brother
later managed a rich landowner’s estate. In 1831, in his thirty-first
year, he entered the Glinsk hermitage under the abbot
[Philaret](/saints/philaret-danilevsky) and served his first trial of
obedience in the brethren’s kitchen, where the blessed elder
[Theodotus](/saints/theodotus-levchenko) was then cook — a living pattern
of self-denial from whom the fervent novice took the first lessons of the
life that was so much to his heart; and he resolved from the start on the
cruel road of unsparing self-affliction, to root out the passions and
mortify the old man, and kept the fire of that zeal unquenched to his
blessed end, which few manage. He was entered among the registered novices
on 4 June 1835, passed through the black obediences of the house with
diligence, never missed the church rule, standing every service from
beginning to end with head bowed and eyes shut to keep his senses from
wandering, and made it his rule to speak no idle word, answering briefly
only when the obedience required, so that he was always seen recollected
and silent; and to bind his mind to the words of God he learned the whole
Psalter by heart, in imitation of Spiridon the prosphora-baker of the
Caves, and recited psalms at every task — chopping wood, carrying water,
raking hay — silently when others were near and aloud when alone, until
the Psalter became as it were the element his soul moved in. On 24 June
1840 Philaret tonsured him with the name Martyrius. Severe with himself
before, after his vows he cut himself off from everything earthly and
denied himself the very necessities: he owned nothing but the clothes he
stood in, would not so much as see money, and forbade his well-off brother
to give to the house he lived in — give to Sophroniev, to the Peter and
Paul monastery, to the other monasteries, but bring nothing here, or the
abbot and brethren will thank you and show me regard, and that is not good
for a monk, who does better in complete obscurity; let not your left hand
know what your right hand does — taking from him nothing but wax candles
for his cell prayers; and to root humility deep he refused flatly every
offer of holy orders, cutting off the very causes of honour.

Sent to the far mill, a verst from the monastery, where he could not
always come to church, he added the whole office to his long cell rule and
did it at night, his mind on the psalms even at the mill’s business. The
wooden house there was old and thatched, its plinth rotten, with two little
cells warmed by one stove, his and his helper’s, a young novice; in winter
the novice found his own cell warm and the elder’s very cold, and
discovered that the plinth outside the elder’s cell had been dug away and
holes cut through for the cold to pass, and asked leave to stop them — no,
said the elder, it will be too hot; come in the evening and you will see.
They said the evening rule together, and then the elder began prostrations,
quick as a machine and without tiring, and the young novice, strong and
eager to keep up, threw off his cassock in a sweat; when at last the elder
read the dismissal he thanked him for praying with him and asked, well,
brother, is my cell cold now? — I am wet through, father, as if from the
bath; let me go and cool down and rest, my legs are breaking — and the
elder, seeing him off with a kind smile, said, whenever it is cold in your
cell, do as you have just done and you will always be warm; the novice
said afterwards that he was afraid to go to the rule a second time. He gave
his body only enough to keep it alive; a day or two without food was
ordinary, and once, in imitation of the ancient fathers, he went fifteen
days without eating, reciting the Psalter and his rule, and told his
intimates afterwards that beyond that measure fasting was impossible; when
the cellarer asked why he had not come for provisions so long, he answered,
it is far to walk. Wanting to give the brethren a comfort as the old
fathers did, he would beg a bottle of hempseed oil and invite some of them
to the mill for hot pancakes, kneading the batter to the psalms, and when
the guests came set one to read the Psalter and the others to eat in
silence, all rising to bow three times at every Glory; the
schema-archimandrite [Iliodor](/saints/heliodorus-golovanitsky), coming early, found him bent over the long
oven-fork reading a psalm aloud into the blazing stove, drawing out the
pan, tossing the pancake and going on with the doxology — serving his
neighbour at the stove without ceasing to stand before God in his mind.

Asking the abbot Innocent to move him into the monastery so as never to
miss the common prayer, he got a cell in the tower at the corner of the
wall in the bishop’s garden and the obedience of reading his hours in the
psalter-room, where the Psalter is read unceasingly for the departed;
there he lived like a hermit, seen only in church, at the refectory and at
the rare common obediences, always with bowed head and eyes on the ground,
standing in a corner or in the choir loft sunk in the thought of God, and
striking his forehead hard with his nails at the sign of the cross, so
that a red inflamed mark was always there — to remember, he told those who
asked, the pain of the crown of thorns that the soldiers pressed into the
Saviour’s head for us sinners. His cell had no table, chair, bed or bench;
his one cassock, two under-cassocks, mantle and kamilavka, worn out and
patched, hung on nails in the passage, and an uncovered sheepskin lay
rolled in a corner for him to sit on when tired; the walls were bare but
for the icon and a rough plank shelf with the Gospel, the Epistles, the
Psalter and the works of Demetrius of Rostov. After church he stood to his
cell rule, and when sleep came made countless prostrations, never counting
them — when you make prostrations do not count them, he told others, and
you will not think how many you have made and be troubled by vainglory;
God sees and knows, and what is it to us to know — and when tired sat on
the sheepskin with Demetrius of Rostov, and when sleep came again stood and
prostrated again; he strengthened himself with sleep sitting on his
sheepskin, for no one ever saw him lie down. A woman pilgrim once gave old
monks handkerchiefs and asked their prayers; in his he found fifty
kopecks, and that night after the rule a thought came — you are old and
weak, you should keep yourself up; buy a little vodka and take a small
glass before dinner for the appetite, and your health will mend, for
abstinence kills the appetite — and pressed so shamelessly on the words of
the psalm that he tied the handkerchief and coins to a brick, went to the
deepest place in the river by the bathhouse, threw it in over his shoulder
without looking, and came back to find his thoughts gone and his cell at
peace. He never drank tea, having no teapot — it takes too much time, he
said; three times a year, when the house gave out tea and sugar for
Pascha, the patronal feast and Christmas, he took his portion to the
novice Peter who lived below him, drank one cup and left the rest. In the
refectory he seemed to eat like the others but ate like a small child, and
did not come every day, and on the days he did not come went without; in
his cell he had only a water jug and an earthenware pot, which he brought
to the kitchen in the evening to take what was left of the gruel once every
brother had been served — what goes to the cattle, he said, one may eat
without reproach of conscience, but for what is fit for the brethren one
must pray — and ate a few spoonfuls, keeping the rest for the next day,
when it had soured and begun to ferment, and when nature refused it rebuked
himself: what, you will not eat? not tasty, you do not like it? and where
is it to go now? it is holy alms; so you are not hungry yet, and when you
truly are you will eat it — so that he told the brethren gruel grew
tastier the longer it stood. In the first week of the Great Fast he neither
ate nor drank until he communicated on the Saturday, standing every service
and making every prostration the Typikon asks, and in Passion week took
nothing until Great Thursday and then nothing again until Pascha, so that
he became a walking skeleton with sunken eyes that burned with the inner
joy of the spirit, and hid his face under his klobuk; his confessor
Anastasius, seeing him so worn on the last week and told he had eaten
nothing since Sunday, sent him to eat before he would confess him, and he
obeyed. Asked how to fight thoughts, passions and desires he answered
bluntly, tighten your fasting — do not eat a day, and if they do not leave,
a second and a third, and pray and make more prostrations, and the attacks
will stop — which was his own road and the only one he knew; he had a
strong constitution to bear it, and the more discerning elders would not
send the young to him for direction, since what he had crossed in the first
heat of zeal could not safely be imitated by weaker men, who go the royal
middle road; the schemamonk [Euthymius](/saints/euthymius-lyubimchenko),
himself a severe ascetic, said, I wanted to imitate him but could not,
because he was stronger than I in body.

In the summer of 1863 he fell suddenly ill and told his confessor Porphyry
why: at night, standing at prayer, he had heard his tower shake and crack,
the walls trembling as if to fall, with cries and threats to crush him
under the ruins, and the demons, by God’s leave, had come close and beaten
him so that blue weals stood on his body and his right arm bore the marks
of a stick, and he carried it in a towel round his neck for a long time —
why does the evil enemy attack me, he said in the simplicity of his soul;
I do not touch him. He would sometimes doze in the psalter-room instead of
his cell: in the cell the enemies will not let me sleep, but here the word
of God is read and the enemy has no such freedom. That summer the elder
[Macarius](/saints/macarius-sharov) wished to move into his tower for
greater solitude, and the abbot gave Martyrius a cell in the wooden
building by the holy gate; Macarius, coming to see the tower, thought the
elder had already moved out because there was nothing in it, and persuaded
him for the common life’s sake to have a table, a chair and a bed in his
new cell even if he never sat or lay on them — old half-broken things were
brought, and the bed with three pieces of board and no sheet became his
deathbed. The new cell looked on the cathedral so closely that with the
window open he could hear the services from it; the schemamonk Euthymius
lived through the wall, and the two, both come to dispassion and purity of
heart, exchanged childlike greetings on their riches — now, father, you
have moved close to the church of God, what a blessing for us weak old men
— oh, I am rich now, glory to the Lord; I hear the holy services even in
my cell. He was brotherly and attentive for all his severity: to a young
novice from Yunakovka who asked his birthplace he first said Yunakovka,
then that if the boy was from Yunakovka he was not, and walked off — and
came back to explain that his home was the nearby Basovka, which no one
knew, so that to say so would breed a whole idle conversation, and every
idle word must be answered for at the judgement; he introduced the boy to
his visiting brother as a fellow-countryman, and at six one morning, when
they were alone at the akathist, took him up to the choir loft to make
prostrations together and thanked him after. Near the end his strength
failed but he changed nothing, telling the sacristan Jerome that at his
age the first week could not be kept whole, but a cup of hot water and a
spoon of honey on the Wednesday would carry him to Saturday; he stood the
Passion services as far as Holy Saturday and could not come to church on
Pascha, but during matins, overcoming his weakness, went out onto the
porch opposite the cathedral doors and leaned on the wall to hear the
Paschal singing through the open door a few arshins away. On the second
day of Pascha, 5 April 1865 — the register says the third day, the 6th,
but the brethren’s diaries and the writer, who was there, say the second,
at midday — he received the Mysteries and with prayer on his lips gave his
spirit into the hands of his Lord, his face lit with radiance; the
schema-archimandrite Iliodor, present, told the brethren that so holy an
end witnessed to a holy life, and as they dressed the body they marvelled
at its strange bright whiteness, and buried it to the Paschal songs of
victory over death. The life is from the Glinsk Paterikon of the
schema-archimandrite John (Maslov), who says the little told here is only
the outer part of an ascesis whose inner warfare and grace-filled
visitations are known to God alone; the Council of Bishops of 2017 entered
him in the calendar of the Russian Church, which keeps him on 9 September
of the old style, 22 September, in the synaxis of the Glinsk fathers.
