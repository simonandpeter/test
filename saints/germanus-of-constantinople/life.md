# Germanus of Constantinople

Germanus was of Constantinople. His father Justinian was one of the first senators, honoured above others with the rank of patrician, and was killed out of envy by Constantine Pogonatus when he took the Greek empire. Having killed that chosen senator, the emperor ordered his son — the blessed Germanus, still a child — to be made a eunuch and put into the clerical order of the Church, so that when he came of age he could neither stand among the lay senators nor take any revenge upon the house of Pogonatus for his father's death.

Among the clergy he lived like an angel of God, keeping far from every earthly desire and all worldly vanity. His mind was lifted up to God and the praise of the Lord was always in his mouth; day and night he was at the divine books, and out of them he sounded the depth of wisdom and drew the waters of life of the God-inspired teaching, with which, being appointed pastor and teacher, he watered the Church of Christ. Coming to the full age of a man, he shone by the manner of his life and by the word of his teaching like a bright lamp.

He was first made bishop of the city of Cyzicus, and there set himself against the heresy of the Monothelites together with the most holy Cyrus, patriarch of Constantinople, with whom he suffered banishment from the evil-believing emperor Philippicus. Afterwards that persecutor perished with a noise, the most holy Cyrus died in exile and the false patriarch John the heretic was put down; and Germanus took the throne of the patriarchate of Constantinople in the reign of Artemius, who was called Anastasius the Second.

The Orthodox raised him up with great joy, as a worthy man full of the grace of God, which was known in him by his foresight, for he saw what was to come and told it to the people. As they were first leading him to the cathedral church of Holy Wisdom and the people were running to see the newly chosen patriarch, a woman named Anna, being with child, came to him and cried: bless, father, the one conceived in my womb. The patriarch looked at her and, seeing with the inward eyes what was to be, said: may the Lord bless him by the prayers of the Holy Protomartyr.

By those words he foretold that she would bear a son, and that he would share the name of the first martyr Stephen; and the woman said afterwards to everyone, on oath, that she had seen a flame of fire come out of his mouth together with the word. When her days were fulfilled Anna bore a son and named him Stephen; and Stephen, coming of age, lived in asceticism on the mountain of Auxentius and became a confessor and a martyr of Christ, suffering for the holy icons at the hands of the evil-believing Copronymus.

Of Copronymus himself Germanus prophesied. Leo the Isaurian took the Greek empire and had this son Constantine; and when the child was baptised at the hands of the patriarch he fouled the holy font, and Germanus said of him: when this child has grown he will be a great evil to the Christians and to the whole Church, for he will defile the holy Church with heretical teachings and will shed much blood of the faithful servants of Christ. He was afterwards named Copronymus, and the prophecy was fulfilled in him.

When the emperor pressed the war on the icons, Germanus said to him: if I am Jonah, throw me into the sea — for without an ecumenical council I have no power to legislate anything new concerning the faith. Seeing at last that he could not turn the emperor from that heresy at all, he laid his patriarchal omophorion on the holy table in the sanctuary and left his office. The emperor, filled with anger at his bold words, sent his soldiers armed as for war, who struck him with their palms, beat and pushed him about, and drove him dishonoured out of the houses of the patriarchate.

He went to the quiet of the monks, spent the rest of his days among them and fell asleep in peace in the Lord. He had been patriarch fourteen years and five months. In his place the emperor raised Anastasius the heretic, who with him put the holy icons out of the Church and confirmed the heresy of the war against them; and the word Germanus had spoken over him — that he would be taken by the people to Dipin — was fulfilled when Copronymus took the city, had Anastasius stripped and beaten before the people at that same place and carried through it backwards upon an ass. He kept the throne twenty-four years more and died of the sickness called cordapsos; while Germanus found the sweet life with the holy hierarchs in the kingdom of our Lord Jesus Christ.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same civil day and gives the reign-by-reign frame. saint.gr has him born at Constantinople in 640, the son of the patrician Justinian who brought him up in great piety; at twenty he was orphaned, his father having been counted by Constantine Pogonatos among his own father's killers and put to death, and Germanus himself was made a eunuch and enrolled in the clergy. Famous for virtue, learning and holiness, he was chosen metropolitan of Cyzicus in his thirty-seventh year, and when the patriarchal throne fell vacant he mounted the ecumenical throne in 715 by the emperor Anastasius's judgment and the votes of senate, clergy and people.

When Leo the Isaurian told him to conform to the iconoclast decrees he not only refused but urged the people to resist, and so was forced to resign, laying his omophorion on the holy table. He withdrew to a family estate, Platania, and after a short illness died there at the age of a hundred on 12 May 740; he was buried in the monastery of Chora. The false synod of Hiereia deposed and anathematised him in 754, and the Seventh Ecumenical Council vindicated and praised him in 787. In his patriarchate, when Constantinople was delivered from a barbarian assault in 718, Andrew of Crete completed the Akathist Hymn. Most of his own writing was burnt by Leo's order; 104 stichera and 22 canons survived, with his treatise on heresies and councils, three dogmatic letters against the iconoclasts and eight discourses. The day keeps him with [Epiphanius](/saints/epiphanius-of-cyprus), and the two are hymned together.

The Ohrid Prologue, read for the Julian 12 May, gives this folder its first year and begins his life in a cruelty neither of the other calendars read for it records. He was the son of the first imperial senator, whom the emperor Constantine Pogonatus put to death; and that same evil emperor had the senator's son, this Germanus, castrated, and drove him by force into a monastery. As a monk, the book says, Germanus shone like a star by his life of virtue, and for that he was elected first bishop of Cyzicus, and then, when Anastasius the Second came to the throne, patriarch of Constantinople.

As patriarch he baptised the ill-famed Copronymus, who at the moment of his baptism fouled the water of the font; and the patriarch prophesied then that this child, when he became emperor, would bring some unclean heresy into the Holy Church. So it came to pass: when Copronymus became emperor he renewed the iconoclast heresy. His father, Leo the Isaurian, had begun the persecution of the icons before him; and when the patriarch Germanus set himself against it, the overbearing Leo cried out, I am emperor and priest — and threw Germanus off his throne and drove him away to a monastery, where this saint lived ten years more, until the Lord called him to Himself into the heavenly Kingdom in the year 740.

The Russian church calendar keeps him on the same menologion day, 12 мая ст. ст., and its life fills in the beginning and the end. He was born at Constantinople in the seventh century. His father was one of the first senators of Byzantium and was killed by order of the emperor Константин Погонат; the boy was made a eunuch and given to the clergy, where he studied the Scriptures deeply, and for the holiness of his life he was made bishop of the city of Кизик before he was raised to the patriarchal throne.

He was patriarch fourteen years and five months. Having rebuked the emperor to his face for heresy, he laid his omophorion on the altar table and left the throne; the angered emperor sent soldiers who beat him and drove him out of the patriarchal house. He withdrew to a monastery and spent there the days that remained to him, and died in 740 at the age of ninety-five, and was buried in the monastery of Хора at Constantinople; his relics, the page says, were afterwards carried into France. The Seventh Council wrote his name into the diptychs of the saints, and the calendar lists what he wrote: a commentary on the liturgy, a work on the hard places of Scripture, one on the recompense of the righteous after death, histories of the heresies and of the councils under the iconoclast emperor, three letters on the veneration of icons that were read at that council, and hymns and homilies for the feasts of the Mother of God.
