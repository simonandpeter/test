# Anna of Larissa

saint.gr places her in the middle Byzantine period, between the ninth century and the middle of the tenth, and calls her one more holy figure of the city of Larissa, together with [her son John](/saints/john-son-of-anna-of-larissa). It sets out its sources carefully: her synaxarion was published by Stavros Gouloulis from the codex Vaticanus graecus 1558, a sixteenth-century Menaion for June; the Synaxaria selecta of the Church of Constantinople and Nikodemos the Hagiorite both commemorate «της οσίας μητρός ημών Άννης και του υϊού αυτής Ιωάννου»; and the synaxarion is itself an epitome of a narrative by Paul of Monemvasia, of the tenth century, from his Accounts of virtuous and God-fearing men and women.

Paul wrote it down, the page says, after meeting a hieromonk who told him what he had seen. The hieromonk was travelling by sea from Rome to Constantinople when the winds forced his ship to put in at an uninhabited and barren island of the Adriatic, and he took the chance to walk inland. He had not gone far when he saw what he calls «αποσκίαμα ανθρώπου γυμνού», the shadow of a naked person, who said to him: if you wish to look upon my wretchedness, throw me one of your garments, for I am a woman and naked, as you see, and it is not fitting to be seen so by your priestly perfection. He gave her a garment; she turned to the East, knelt, and rose thanking God that she had been granted to meet a priest.

Asked who she was, she answered readily: «εγώ, τίμιε πάτερ, εκ της Ελλάδος χώρας ειμί, πόλεως Λαρίσης» — I am from the land of Greece, from the city of Larissa. Her parents were poor and left her orphaned young; after their death one of the notables of Larissa took pity on her, took her into his house, brought her up and educated her carefully as his own daughter, and, valuing her virtues, gave her as bride to his son, «not despising my poverty and low birth», as she put it. The bridegroom's relatives took the marriage very badly; he fended off their objections, saying that the criterion of his father's choice had been not wealth or noble birth but her virtues according to God. But because they would not stop, and because she saw her husband grieved, she decided to leave secretly, and so she went from Larissa and, God guiding her, came to that island.

On arriving there she found that she was with child. Nobody was beside her to help. Her son was born on that desert island of the Adriatic, and had by then lived thirty years with her; and all those years she had been entreating God to have mercy on her lowliness and send a priest to perfect her son by holy baptism. So she begged the hieromonk to go back to the ship and bring the priestly vestment and holy bread, that he might illumine her son, celebrate the holy Liturgy and make them worthy of the communion of the body and blood of the Lord. She asked one thing more: to tell nobody about them.

He did as she asked, and returned; she led him to where her son was and called him out — «έξελθε τοίνυν, τέκνον, και προσκύνησον τον εληλυθότα φωτίσαι σε» — and at a spring nearby the hieromonk catechised and baptised him, and then served the Liturgy, and both communicated. As they left, Anna asked her last favour: that if he wished to tell what the Lord had shown him, he should tell it hiding the island, lest anyone come from the report and find them. He wept, worshipped God who does strange and wonderful things without number, and went back to his ship. According to the synaxarion from the Vatican codex, Anna and her son, having thanked the priest, «τας αγίας αυτών ψυχάς εις χείρας Θεού παρέθεντο» — gave over their holy souls into the hands of God.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her and her son on the Julian 13 June, the same menologion day the folder’s Greek row carries, and under the same pairing — holy Anna and her son John — though it names neither Larissa nor Monemvasia. Taken as an orphan into the house of some great man, she was brought up there as a foster-daughter, and being a worthy girl that great man married her to his own son. When the old man died his kin fell upon the son to put his wife away for her low birth and marry another who would suit him better in family and wealth.

The son feared God and would not do it. Seeing her husband set against his own kin on her account, Anna left him secretly and fled to a far island where there was not a living soul. But she came to that island with child, and soon bore a son; and there she struggled thirty years in fasting and prayer. Then, by God’s providence, a hieromonk was put ashore on that island, and he baptised her son and gave him the name [John](/saints/john-son-of-anna-of-larissa). This book sets her in the fifth century and says she went in peace, where the Greek page puts her four hundred years later, in the middle Byzantine period; the folder holds the Greek reading and does not choose between them.
