# Ezekiel

Ezekiel was from a Hebrew city called Sarira, the son of Buzi, of the tribe of Levi, a priest of God the Most High. He was taken captive by Nebuchadnezzar and carried to Babylon together with Joachim, king of the Jews, who was called Jehoiachin the Second, in the second captivity of Jerusalem; for Jerusalem was taken three times by Nebuchadnezzar.

The first time it was taken in the days of Joachim, king of Judah, who is also called Eliakim in the holy Scripture, the son of Josiah, brother of Jehoahaz and of Zedekiah and father of Jehoiachin the Second; he was the first whom Nebuchadnezzar carried bound in chains to Babylon. At that same time the prophet Daniel was carried off with the three young men, Ananias, Azarias and Misael. After a short while Nebuchadnezzar set Joachim free again in Jerusalem as king, making him a tributary.

Joachim reigned three years under the power of Nebuchadnezzar in Jerusalem, and after those three years broke from him, refusing to pay tribute any longer. So the power of Babylon came against Jerusalem again and took the city, and King Joachim was killed and thrown out of the city to be eaten by dogs; and in his place, by Nebuchadnezzar's order, his son was made king, who was likewise called Joachim and by another of his father's names Jehoiachin, and became Jehoiachin the Second, a tributary of the kingdom of Babylon as his father had been.

Because this Jehoiachin was evil before God, not long afterwards, by the will of God, Nebuchadnezzar came again to Jerusalem and carried off Jehoiachin with all his house and a multitude of men of note and of brave men and of all who were fit and skilled for war, and the golden vessels of the temple were taken too. That was the second captivity of Jerusalem, and in that time Ezekiel was carried off, with Mordecai and Josedec, the father of that Jesus who with Zerubbabel renewed the ruined temple of Jerusalem.

The Romanian page's account of his prophecies and his wonders is a long one and the middle of it is left here unsummarised. It tells how the Hebrew people passed over on dry ground while the Chaldeans who dared to run after them by the same way were covered by the water and perished; how, judging in Babylon the tribes of Dan and of Gad and seeing that they did not honour the Lord but persecuted those who kept His law, he sent serpents and wild beasts on their dwellings, which ate their children and their cattle, and then, pitying their mortal hurt, drove the serpents and beasts away from them by prayer.

In a time of famine he multiplied food enough for the peoples by his prayers to God, and turned back from the gates of death those who were weakened by hunger to the point of dying. After that he ended with a martyr's end: seeing his own people, who were with him in the captivity, sharing in the service of the idols with the Chaldeans and taking up all their unclean practices, he rebuked them and urged them to leave off those lawless things, threatening them with the anger of God; and the chief man of the Hebrews, who had given himself to the Chaldean paganism, was filled with rage and killed him, tearing him apart with horses.

His torn body the peoples gathered and buried in the village of Maur, in the tomb of Shem and of Arphaxad, the forefathers of Abraham, and multitudes of the Hebrew people gathered at his grave and made their prayers there to the Lord of Sabaoth. The page gives no year for any of it.
