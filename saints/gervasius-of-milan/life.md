# Gervasius of Milan

Gervasius and Protasius were brothers of Milan. Their father, the page says, taught them the true faith in his own house and gave up his spirit into God’s hands three days later; and when the brothers were left orphaned they sold the house, divided all their property among the poor and set their slaves free. Then they shut themselves up alone in a house and laboured there ten years in fasting, in prayer and in reading; and in the eleventh year they began to suffer for Christ in prison, under Anulinus the governor.

While they lay in prison [Nazarius](/saints/nazarius-of-milan), who had come from Rome to Milan and spent his inheritance on the poor and on the confessors, went often to comfort them with his good words, and loved them greatly, for he saw them full of the divine love and ready at any moment to give their souls for the Lord. He was bound to them by such love that he did not wish to be parted from them, but wished to suffer and to die together with them; and for visiting them he was himself beaten and driven out of the city.

Ambrose, bishop of Milan, wrote to the brethren throughout Italy how the relics were found. In the forty days of the fast, being at prayer one night, he fell into a kind of sleep in which he was neither sleeping nor feeling, and saw two young men in white raiment lifting up their hands in prayer; he could not speak to them, and when he woke they were gone. He prayed that if it were a deceit of the devil it might be driven off, and if it were true that it might be shown him more plainly.

He fasted much, and on the second night, at cockcrow, he saw the same young men praying with him; and on the third night, when his body was weak with fasting and he could not sleep, they appeared again, and with them a third who resembled the Apostle Paul, whom he knew from the icon. That third spoke with him alone, the others keeping silence, and said that these were they who had heard his words and left the world and its riches and followed our Lord Jesus Christ, desiring nothing earthly or fleshly, and had lived ten years in Milan serving God, and had been counted worthy to be martyrs of Christ.

When the count Astasius was going from Milan to the war against the Moravians, the priests of the idols met him on the road and told him that if he wished to come back victorious to his emperor he must force the two prisoners to sacrifice. Astasius ordered Gervasius to be beaten with rods of lead until he died; and under that beating Saint Gervasius made his end.

The count had the body carried out and said to [Protasius](/saints/protasius-of-milan), „Wretch, spare your life and do not go mad like your brother”; and Protasius answered that he did not know which of them was the wretch, he who did not fear the count, or the count who feared him. Protasius was then beheaded with the sword. Philip and his son took the bodies away by night and buried them in their house in a marble coffer, as Philip’s own little book relates; and when the relics were taken out of the ground many sick were healed, devils were driven out of men and the blind saw. A blind man of the city named Severus had only to touch the edge of the garments that lay on the holy relics and his sight at once grew clear.

The page names Nero as the emperor of the persecution, Anulinus as the governor who imprisoned the brothers and Astasius as the count who killed them, and gives no year, and none is supplied here.

The Greek calendar keeps the four of them on the same day, 14 October, and saint.gr tells how they came together. The parents of Nazarius, Africanus and Perpetua, are said to have been disciples of the Apostle Peter, who drew them to the faith and baptised them; their son was orphaned young in a corrupt society and kept pure and blameless, and at twenty he divided what he had among the poor and set out preaching. At Mediolanum he met two devout men, Protasius and Gervasius, and the three of them together won many by the divine word, from the crowd and from the higher classes alike. Then Nazarius went into Gaul, where among those who believed was a young soul, Celsus. When he returned to Mediolanum he was arrested; Protasius and Gervasius had been taken before him; and the eparch Anoulinos, seeing that they would not change their faith, beheaded them all, together with the young Celsus. The page adds that the relics of Protasius, and the head and the rest of the relics of Gervasius, are at the Roman Catholic Ambrosian Basilica of Milan.

The Russian calendar keeps the four of them on 14 October too, and gives the only date any calendar gives them: the reign of Nero, 54 to 68. He and Protasius were twins, born at Mediolanum to rich Roman citizens, Vitalis and Valeria. Their parents were tortured to death for the Christian faith; left orphans, the brothers gave their property away to the poor, set their slaves at liberty and gave themselves to fasting and prayer, and for confessing Christ the pagans shut them in prison. It was there that Nazarius, visiting the Christians of the city, came to know them and set himself to easing what they suffered, and was beaten and expelled for it.

Nazarius and Celsus came back to Milan after their own deliverance and visited the two brothers in the prison; the visit was reported to Nero, who had those two beheaded, and the brothers were executed soon afterwards. Many years later, in the reign of the emperor Theodosius, Ambrose, bishop of Mediolanum, uncovered the relics of the martyrs by a revelation from above, and they were carried in solemn procession into the cathedral of Milan, where they became famous for many healings.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him with [Protasius](/saints/protasius-of-milan) and [Nazarius](/saints/nazarius-of-milan) on the Julian 14 October under one heading, and says in one line what the other books read for this folder do not: the two of them were twins, and the sons of blessed parents who were disciples of the faith as well, Vitalis and Valeria. They were already in prison at Mediolanum when Nazarius came there preaching and served them with great love, which is how the three of them come to stand as one company.

They were still in that prison years later, when Nazarius returned from Gaul and was thrown in with them again. Soon after Nazarius was beheaded the two brothers were cut down as well, by a voivode the book names Astasius, who was passing through Mediolanum on his way to a war against the Moravians; hearing that the two would not sacrifice to the idols, he was afraid he would lose his battle on that account, and ordered them beheaded at once. The relics of the two brothers, the entry ends, were revealed to [Ambrose of Milan](/saints/ambrose-of-milan) in a wondrous vision.
