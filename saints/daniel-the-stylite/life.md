# Daniel the Stylite

Daniel was born in Mesopotamia, between the Tigris and the Euphrates, to a mother who had been barren and reproached for it and who was given him after prayer; the life says she was healed of her barrenness by his birth. His parents wanted to give him at once to the monastery near them, but the abbot would not take him so young. At twelve he left his father's house without telling anyone where he was going, came to that monastery, and when they asked him why, answered that he had come to live in Christ and to die to the world.

He grew there in the monastic life, and twice went out to see [Symeon the Stylite](/saints/symeon-the-stylite) on his pillar. When his abbot died and the brothers pressed the abbacy on him he put another man forward in his place and slipped out of the monastery secretly, meaning to see the holy places at Jerusalem and then go into the desert for quiet; but the Samaritans were making war on the Christians in Palestine and the road was shut. He came instead to the neighbourhood of Constantinople and settled in a place the demons were held to own, where stones were thrown at him in the night and a terrifying voice was heard; and there, where the demons had lodged, the service of God came to be sung day and night.

People began to come to him from the country round, and some of the clergy, envying it, went to Anatolius the patriarch to have him driven out; the patriarch would not, and when he fell ill soon after he sent for Daniel and was helped by him. After nine years in that place Daniel saw in a vision a tower whose height went above the clouds, with Symeon standing on the top of it and two shining young men beside him, and heard a voice calling him up; and when he asked how he could climb so high, Symeon sent the young men down to bring him, embraced him and told him to take courage, to be strong and great-souled and to stand well and manfully. Sergius, Symeon's disciple, came from Antioch with Symeon's cowl, and it was given to Daniel.

So he went up onto a pillar, and never came down from it again all his life. The owner of the ground, Gelasius, complained to the emperor and to the patriarch Gennadius that a tower had been built on his land without leave; the emperor took no notice, the patriarch gave Gelasius authority to pull him down — and Gelasius, hindered in the attempt, afterwards built him a higher tower and held him in great honour. He stood through the winters, through rain and wind and a cold the life says cannot be told, and the emperor came weeping to touch his feet and beg him to spare himself.

He had from God the driving out of demons, the healing of sicknesses and the gift of foresight. He prayed for the emperor Leo, who had no sons; he told the city that the great fire would stop after seven days, and it did; heretics hired a harlot, Bassiana, to lie about him, and the lie was shown up. The emperor had him ordained priest as he stood on the pillar, the patriarch reading the prayers from below and the people crying Axios; then a ladder was set up, and he received the priestly signs from the patriarch's hand and communicated with him. When Basiliscus drove Zeno out and troubled the Church, he came down — the one time — and went into the city, healed a leper on the road, and did not leave until the emperor sought peace with him.

He came to deep old age and foresaw his end, and wrote a testament for his disciples and had it read to them while they wept: he was going to the common Father and would not leave them orphans; they were to follow humility, to serve others, to love hospitality, fasting, vigil and poverty. A possessed man standing by the tower said he saw many angels and saints coming down from heaven, and named them; and Daniel gave up his soul full of days, having lived eighty years and three months, with the hair of his head parted in two and four palms long, and his beard likewise. Three crosses of stars shone above the tower until he was buried beside it, and with him were laid the relics of the three youths of Babylon, Ananias, Azarias and Misael, because he had commanded that the honour of those who came should go to them and not to him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 11 December, and its page sets his pillar in a frame of its own before it tells his life: all the saints are to be wondered at, it says, but not all of them are to be imitated, and the achievement of the stylites is one of those works that is admirable without being imitable — the Cross has its own worth, outside every social usefulness.

Daniel was born in 410, saint.gr says, in the village of Maroutha in the district of Samosata, of devout parents called Elias and Martha; he was born of a mother who had been barren, and so his parents promised to dedicate him to the service of God. While he was still young he used to go to the neighbouring towns and explain the Gospel; then he went to a cenobitic monastery, where he gave himself to devout exercises, to theological study and to the cultivation of humility. Once, on a journey with the abbot of the house, he met [Symeon](/saints/symeon-the-stylite) the Stylite and took his blessing.

When the abbot died, Daniel went to Symeon again and asked his advice about where to go; Symeon advised him to go to Constantinople, and Daniel did so, settling in the precinct of the church of the archangel Michael on the Propontis. A little later he saw a vision of Symeon calling him, and interpreting it he built a high pillar and settled on top of it. The purpose of settling on the pillar, the page says, was the struggle for the wiping out of the passions and the gaining of more virtues. He received the gift of foresight, worked many wonders, and his part in the Council of Chalcedon was considerable. He died at eighty, full of the fruits of righteousness that are through Jesus Christ.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same menologion day, gives the same eighty years and puts his repose one year earlier, in 489; neither year is chosen here. It disagrees with the Greek page about his village too: both set his birth in the district of Samosata in Mesopotamia, and this book calls the village Vitari where that one calls it Maroutha. His parents were Christians, Elias and Martha; he was begged of God by the tearful prayers of a barren mother, and as her only son was dedicated to God's service from infancy. In his twelfth year he took the monastic habit, and he visited [Symeon the Stylite](/saints/symeon-the-stylite) and was blessed by him.

Longing for solitude he left the monastery and withdrew into a deserted idol-temple on the shore of the Black Sea, where he bore countless assaults from demons and overcame them all by patience, by prayer and by the sign of the cross. After that he climbed up on a pillar, and stayed on it until his death, bearing heat and cold alike and the attacks of men as well as of demons. Many disciples gathered about the pillar, and he led them towards eternal life by his example and his words.

God rewarded this devoted servant of his with great grace in this life as well, so that he worked many wonders useful to men and foretold things to come; and people streamed from every side to the foot of his pillar, asking help and counsel of God's saint. Emperors and patriarchs came to him as simple men did. The emperor used to bring his guests from other countries, princes and great men, and show them Daniel on the pillar, saying to them: here is a wonder in my empire. He foretold the day of his death, taught his disciples as a father teaches his sons and took leave of them; and before his death his disciples saw angels, prophets, apostles and martyrs above the pillar.
