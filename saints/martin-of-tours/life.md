# Martin of Tours

Martin lived in the fourth century, saint.gr says, and came from Sabaria of Pannonia. From a young age he had begun to be catechised in the word of the Lord; at fifteen he was enrolled in the army, where he served under the emperors Constantine and Julian.

When he was with the army at Amiens he met a beggar who asked him for alms. Having nothing but his uniform and his weapons, the saint took off his cloak and gave it to him to warm himself; and the next night the Lord appeared to him in his sleep and said, showing him the cloak: Martin, though you are still a catechumen, you have warmed me by giving the beggar your cloak.

When he had been baptised, Martin went to Saint Hilary, bishop of Poitiers in Gaul. Having got the education he needed he became a cleric and then bishop in Tours. He was marked out for his holy life and for the great services he did his flock, and he took care for the catechising of the people round about; he was much loved, and had eighty disciples whom he used in his works and his teaching. He fell ill in the village of Candes, and there gave up his spirit to the Lord at the age of eighty-one.

Another synaxaristic source, the page adds, has a different notice of him: that he was a count and a general in the reign of Trajan, which it dates 98 to 117, who renounced his offices and the glory of the world and became a monk; that for seven whole years he practised the monastic life and the reading of the holy scriptures; and that for the purity of his life and his great virtues he was chosen bishop of the city of Konstantinos in Gaul, where he lived according to the commands of the Gospel and died in peace as a wonderworker. The page prints both and settles neither; its distich says the world was not worthy of him, and that leaving it he went away into a great world.

The Russian calendar keeps him on 12 October, a month before the Greek, and tells the Western life. He was born in Pannonia at the beginning of the fourth century and wanted the monastic life from childhood, with Anthony the Great before him as his example; but the family was not Christian and his father insisted on the army, and that is how he came to Gaul, serving there as an officer. It was while he was still a soldier that he tore his cloak in two one winter and gave half of it to a man with no clothes at all, whom pious tradition identifies with Christ.

When he could leave the army he withdrew to the desert place of Ligugé near Poitiers, where a small monastery grew up around him and became, in the words of his biographer, the seed-ground of monastic life in Gaul. He was brought to Tours by a trick — called in to pray over a sick woman — and proclaimed bishop, having until then avoided even the diaconate and preferred the humbler office of exorcist. His kindness and his care for the sick, the poor and the hungry earned him the name of the Merciful.

From the see he founded Marmoutier almost at once, on the Eastern rule: goods in common, unquestioning obedience, silence, one meal a day, coarse and simple clothing. Many bishops came out of it to preach among the pagan Celts, and two thousand monks are said to have gathered for his funeral in 397, when Marmoutier itself held no more than eighty. He died at prayer in the church at Candes, above the confluence of the Vienne and the Loire; the people of Tours took the body out through a window and carried it home upriver by boat.

No saint of the Christian West, the page says, was so honoured after his death — thousands of churches and settlements carry his name, his basilica at Tours was the great religious centre of Merovingian and Carolingian France, and his cappa was a relic of state to the Frankish kings. The life written by his contemporary Sulpicius Severus became the pattern for Western hagiography and the first spiritual reading of generations. Its day line then prints his death as «ок. 400», where its own life says 397.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 12 October, the day the Russian calendar keeps him on and not the Greek’s eleventh, and it opens with a birthplace no other book read for this folder gives: he was born in Pannonia, in a town of Styria, in the year 316, of heathen parents. His father was a Roman officer, and so the young Martin was put into the army himself against his will — though he was already a catechumen in the Church, which he had loved with his whole heart from early childhood.

Travelling one winter with his companions towards the city of Amiens he saw a beggar before the gate, all but naked and shivering in the frost. Martin pitied him, fell behind the others, took off his soldier’s cloak, cut it in two with his sword, gave one half to the beggar, wrapped himself in the other and went on. That night the Lord Christ appeared to him in a dream wrapped in that half of his cloak, and said to his angels: Martin is only a catechumen, and see, he has clothed me with his own garment.

He left the army, was baptised at once, and baptised his mother. Then he became a monk in the diocese of Saint Hilary, at Poitiers, and lived a life full of true asceticism. He was extraordinarily humble, and for that humility God gave him an abundant gift of wonderworking, so that he even raised the dead and drove out evil spirits. Against his own will he was made bishop in the city of Tours; and after much work in the Lord’s vineyard, and a hard struggle both with the heathen and with the Arian heretics, he gave up his holy soul into the hands of his Lord in 397.
