# Sabbas the Goth

In the land of the Goths there was a cruel persecution of the Christians. A Gothic prince came into the village where this devout man Sabbas lived and asked the villagers whether there were any Christians among them; they assured him on oath that there was not one. Then Sabbas stood out before the prince and the people and said that no one was to swear on his behalf, for he himself was a Christian. The prince looked at him, saw a poor and wretched man, and let him go in peace, saying that such a one could neither do harm nor do good.

The next year, at Pascha, a priest the entry names Сансал came to that village and kept the bright feast with Sabbas. The pagans heard of it, fell on Sabbas's house without warning, and set about beating the two servants of God without mercy with staves; and Sabbas they dragged naked through thorns besides. Then they bound them both to a tree and offered them meat from the sacrifices to the idols; but the men of God remembered the apostle's words and refused to eat of what had been offered to demons.

At the last the prince condemned Sabbas to death and handed him over to soldiers. Sabbas went to the place of execution full of joy, praising the Lord. The soldiers saw that he was a good man and wanted to let him go on the road, but that grieved him greatly, and he told them that they were bound to carry out what the prince had commanded. So they brought him to a river, tied a stone about his neck and threw him into the water; and his body was cast up on the bank.

Later a Greek commander the entry names Јуније Соранос, waging war on the Goths in the days of the emperor Valens, found the body of Sabbas and carried it away to Cappadocia. He suffered, the entry ends, in his thirty-first year, in the year 372. The priest who kept Pascha with him and the commander who recovered his body are named in the entry and are identified with nobody in this corpus; the Prologue is the only source read for him, and it gives him no birthplace, no parentage, no trade beyond the village, and no hymn.

This corpus already held a second witness to that transfer before it held him. The life of [Bretanion of Tomis](/saints/bretanion-of-tomis), written out of the Romanian calendar, says that the bishop of Tomis had an important part in drawing up and sending the documents for the transfer of the relics of Sabbas the Goth to Cappadocia, and that it was Bretanion who wrote the martyr's act of Sabbas. That is the same transfer this entry describes and the same emperor's wars behind it, and the two accounts name different men for the two halves of the work; neither was read against the other's source here.

**The Russian calendar's life is much the fullest of the two read for this folder, and it names the man who received his relics.** Sabbas was a Goth, it says, baptised among many others when the bishop Wulfila was preaching to that people, and after his baptism he lived a life of virtue: reverent, peaceable, temperate, plain, silent — though he silenced the idol-worshippers — avoiding women, spending all his days in prayer, often singing in church and caring for its good order, and preaching Christ without fear.

When the Gothic princes and judges began their persecution at the prompting of the pagan priests, Christians were pressed to eat meat offered to idols, and pagan kinsmen would substitute ordinary meat to save their Christian relatives. Some accepted the deception; Sabbas refused it and said that a Christian must confess his faith openly. His village drove him out and then asked him back, and when the persecution grew worse and the villagers meant to swear before the judge that there was not one Christian among them, he said aloud, “Do not swear on my behalf, for I am a Christian.” The judge, seeing his poverty, decided he could neither help nor harm anyone, and let him go.

Later a Gothic commander named Atharid fell on the village at Pascha. Sabbas had set out to keep the feast with the bishop Guthicas and was turned back on the road by an angel; the presbyter Sansalas had returned from Greece by then, and the soldiers seized them both. The priest was carried on a cart and Sabbas, naked, was driven behind it through thorns and beaten with a staff and with whips; in the morning he could ask his tormentors to look at his body for any mark of the thorns or their blows, and there was none. He was stretched on the axles of the cart and beaten all day, and in the night a pious woman untied him. Offered the idol-meat with his freedom, Sansalas answered that they would sooner be crucified than eat what devils had defiled, and when a servant drove a spear into his breast Sabbas told him the blow was no harder than a stroke with soft wool.

Atharid ordered him drowned. On the road to the river Mussova he gave thanks aloud for being counted worthy to suffer, and when the servants talked among themselves of letting an innocent man go he cried out to them to do what they had been told, for he could see the angels come in glory to take his soul. They threw him in with a heavy log tied to his neck. He suffered, the life says, on 12 April 372, when he was thirty-eight years old — a day three days before the day the calendar keeps him on, and an age the Ohrid Prologue reckons as his thirty-first year. The executioners dragged the body out and left it on the bank, and Christians hid it; afterwards a Scythian chief named Junius Saranus, a Christian, carried the relics into Cappadocia, where they were received with honour by [Basil the Great](/saints/basil-the-great).
