# Laurence of Salamina

saint.gr's life of him begins at Megara in Attica, in the first half of the seventeenth century, where he was born to simple parents, Demetrius and Kyriake, devout in the Orthodox faith and devoted to the Church. In the world he was called Lambros Kanellos. When he came of age he married a woman named Vasilo and had two sons, John and Demetrius, and with his family he lived a pious and unpretentious life through the very hard years of the Turkish rule. His trade was farming, but he knew the builder's craft as well.

His life, the page says, was wholly virtuous, with a heartfelt traditional Orthodox piety and prayer, and those gifts made him a friend of God and of his saints. So it was that one night, while he was out with fellow townsmen in the fields, the Mother of God appeared to him in a vision and called him to go to a place she showed him and build her church. The place was in the northern part of Salamina, across from the Megarid shore at the spot called the Great Pine, today's Nea Peramos.

The old man would not decide to carry out the order, so the next night she appeared again and pressed him harder; and because he stayed in his doubts he saw her a third time, commanding him: «Τάχιστα πορεύου, ἄνθρωπε, εἰς τὴν νῆσον, εἰς τὴν ὁποίαν σοῦ εἶπον, νὰ ἐκτελέσῃς τὸ παρ᾿ ἐμοῦ προσταττόμενον» — go with all speed, man, to the island of which I told you, and do what I command. He went back to Megara frightened and told the vision to friends and acquaintances, some of whom believed it and some doubted, and he stayed in his house undecided until she appeared once more and threatened him.

Then he made his decision and came down to the shore to cross. But the sea was so rough, and there was no boat anywhere, that crossing looked impossible. As he sat there heavy and despairing he heard a voice from beyond this world telling him to throw his cloak on the water, sit on it, and be carried to the island without danger. Trusting the command absolutely and putting off every fear, he crossed the sea on his cloak and reached Salamina safe and unhurt.

He went at once to the place the Mother of God had shown him, and digging with great labour among the ruins of an older monastery he found her wonderworking icon, blackened by the damp but a real treasure, the page says, for the island and for the whole Orthodox Church. The icon was named the Phaneromeni, the revealed, because it was revealed to him; and the monastery he then rebuilt, in 1682, took the same name. Becoming a monk he received the monastic name Laurence, and he served as abbot of that house, gathering hieromonks and monks, teaching and setting the example of a holy life, and making the monastery famous and revered in his own time.

He first built the small chapel now honoured in the name of St Nicholas, and later the great catholicon, which he did not live to see painted with the frescoes that are admired there still. God endowed this simple man with wonderful spiritual gifts, among them the working of wonders, which he did while he was still alive. One of them was the healing of the wife of an Ottoman official whom the doctors could not cure: the report that Laurence healed the sick by his prayer reached her, and against her husband's strong objections he was called to their house in Athens, where by prayer and the sign of the Cross traced on her body he saved her from certain death. The husband's thanks took the form of restoring to the monastery an olive estate at Vlychada, on the Megarid shore opposite, which had belonged to the ruined house before and which he had been holding unlawfully.

He lived a good many years in asceticism and prayer, and fell asleep in the Lord on 9 March 1707 — others say the 6th — the day of the Forty Martyrs, as an ill-spelt note in a manuscript kept at the monastery from those years shows. His son John, by then a monk under the name Joachim, succeeded him as abbot, and his wife also became a nun and was renamed Vassiane. The transfer of his memory to 7 March, the page says, appears to have been made by the monks of his monastery so that it should not fall together with the great feast of the Forty Martyrs. His venerable head, in a silver casing, lies for veneration in the little church of St Nicholas.
