# Theodotus of Kyrenia

In the island of Cyprus there is a city that doxologia's life page spells Cirene and its calendar line Chirinia; in that city Theodotus was bishop. He was of Galatian stock, born of Christian parents and brought up in learning, and from his youth, having trained himself in wisdom and good works, he went over to Cyprus, where he taught the Greeks to leave the error and deceit of the idols and to believe in Christ, the true God.

He turned many from pagan uncleanness by the preaching of the word of God and led them into the way of salvation, and for that he was chosen bishop of the city. There was then a great persecution of the Christians, the pagan Licinius reigning, and Sabinus was governor in Cyprus; and Theodotus, wishing to suffer for Christ, disputed boldly with the Greeks, rebuking their error and preaching Christ the true God.

When the governor gave orders to seize him, the bishop did not wait to be fetched, but rose and went to him of his own accord, and said that here was the man he was looking for: he had not hidden, nor was he brought by force, for the truth must be shown and not concealed. He told Sabinus that the pagans were more fearful than frogs, since one Christian had thrown a whole city and an army into confusion. The governor, not bearing the rebuke, ordered him beaten without mercy with raw sinews.

Many of the pagan people, seeing his endurance and hearing his words, believed in Christ, mocked the idols and reviled the tyrant; so Sabinus ordered him back into prison, saying that the people must not be deceived by his teaching, and took counsel on what death to put him to. Days passed and his wounds multiplied, and the faithful came with clean cloths to wipe them.

Then Constantine the Great overcame Maxentius by the power of the Cross and gave liberty to all Christians, and an order came from him that the persecution should cease and that those held in chains for Christ should go free. Theodotus grieved deeply at it, for he had wished to die in torments for Christ. Being set free, he went back to his own city, and after two years more upon his throne he fell asleep in the Lord, having received a double crown, of the priesthood and of martyrdom.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and gives the persecutor a name. saint.gr puts him at the beginning of the fourth century, when idolatry was in danger of expiring and sought, like a mortally wounded beast, to fall on the Church with whatever strength was left it; and it says that Theodotus, bishop in Kyrenia of Cyprus, by his great zeal for the Christian faith and the ground he was winning inside the idolatrous world, provoked the anger of the governor Sabinus. Sabinus arrested him and tried to persuade him to deny his faith in Christ, and Theodotus not only did not deny it but spoke boldly to the governor against the error of idolatry and adjured him to renounce the false gods. Then Sabinus tortured him hard, but before the rising of the Christian population he took fright and ordered the wounded hierarch carried to prison. Even there Theodotus did not leave off his work: he found men to whom he handed on the truth, and so made a small flock inside the prison. Later, under Constantine the Great, he was set free and went on with his work more zealously still; and two years after that, the page says, in the year 315, he died, leaving an unforgettable example to clergy and people.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović tells the same story more briefly. It has him chosen bishop for his wisdom and his virtue, and governing the Church of God with love and zeal, until the persecution under the wicked emperor Licinius brought him before a judge and into many torments. When the torturer Sabinus counselled him to renounce Christ and bow to the idols of the heathen, he answered that if Sabinus knew the goodness of the God he hoped in, who would count these short torments worthy of eternal life, he would wish to suffer in the same way himself.

They drove nails into his body, the Prologue says, and he prayed to God with thanksgiving; and thinking his end was near he went on counselling and teaching the Christians who stood about him. Then, by God's providence, [Constantine the Great](/saints/constantine-the-great) proclaimed liberty for the Christians and ordered that all who were held under judgment for Christ should be let go. The saint was freed, came back to his place in Kyrenia as the torments had left him, lived two years more, and fell asleep in the Lord whom he had served faithfully.

The Prologue closes by putting the end of his earthly life in the year 302, and that is the one thing in the entry this folder has not taken. The interval recorded here is the reign of Licinius, 308 to 324, read off the Romanian life; and the Prologue's own sentences stand against its own year, since the persecution it describes is Licinius's, the liberty that frees him is Constantine's, and two years are counted after that. The year is recorded as a disagreement and not resolved.

The Сретенский monastery’s calendar keeps him on the Julian 2 March with the kontakion printed under his name, which this folder now carries, and a life that agrees with the Romanian on the whole course of the thing and differs from it on two points worth setting down. It makes him a Galatian of Asia Minor by birth and bishop of Kyrenia in Cyprus, and dates the persecution by the reign it gives as 307 to 324, where doxologia.ro gave the same reign as 308 to 324; the reign is not him and no bound of his floruit is moved for the difference.

The narrative is the one the folder already carries, and told the same way. He preached Christ openly and called the pagans to leave the service of idols; the governor of Cyprus ordered him sought out and brought to judgement, and he did not wait for the soldiers but went himself and said, I am the man you are looking for, and I have come to preach Christ my God. He was beaten without mercy, hung on a tree and scraped, and put in prison; brought out five days later he did not stop preaching, and was laid on iron gratings over a fire and then had nails driven into his feet and was made to walk.

Many who saw it believed, and the governor stopped the torture and shut him up again rather than go on. Then [Constantine the Great](/saints/constantine-the-great) gave the Christians liberty of religion and he was freed among many others; he went back to Kyrenia and, after two years in his see, departed to the Lord in peace about 326. That is the second difference, and it is a finding rather than a disagreement: the folder had no year of death at all, and this calendar gives one both in its life and on its own line, «(ок 320-326)», which is the interval entered here.

One thing this calendar does not say with one voice. The day’s line grades him «Сщмч.», a hieromartyr, which is the grade this folder already holds from the Romanian and the Greek; the title on its own name link reads «Святитель Феодот Киринейский, епископ, исповедник», a confessor; and its life has him die in peace after the persecution was over, which is what a confessor is. The two readings are one host’s and they are not reconciled here: nothing is added to `types` and nothing taken away, and the reading is written down instead. The day prints the same kontakion twice over, once with «орошаеши» and once with «орошавши»; the first is taken and the variant is recorded here.
