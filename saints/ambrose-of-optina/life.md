# Ambrose of Optina

Ambrose was born on 21 November 1812 in the district of Lipetsk, in the government of Tambov, into the large family of a village sacristan. It was the patronal feast of the village church that day, and peasants were gathered round the house where he was born; he used to say afterwards that as he had been born in the middle of the people, so he lived in the middle of them. In the world he was Alexander Mikhailovich Grenkov. He was quick and lively, went from the church school at Lipetsk to the seminary at Tambov, and was remembered there as a boy who ran about while the others studied and then answered as if he were reading out of the book.

After the seminary he taught for a time in a private house and then at the church school at Lipetsk. He fell gravely ill, promised to become a monk if he recovered, hesitated when he did, and fell ill again. He left Lipetsk for Optina secretly, telling nobody and not even asking the leave of the diocesan authorities, and wrote to his bishop only once he had arrived. At Optina he was received into the skete, given an obedience in the kitchen, and then taken as helper to father [Macarius](/saints/macarius-of-optina), whose closest disciple he became.

What grew in him under that teaching was a height of spirit and a strength of love that he spent, once he was an elder himself, on other people's griefs. The life says he was one of those elders of Optina to whom anyone could come in a moment of heaviness or misfortune, that people came to him from every corner of Russia and laid their sufferings before him, and that to him were entrusted both the most hidden matters of the inner life and questions of money and of household. He was blamed by men who understood neither eldership nor him for busying himself with what they thought was not a monk's affair.

His strength failed at last at Shamordino, the women's community he had fostered and that the life calls his dear child, where he had tried more than once to leave and could not. He weakened towards the end of the winter of 1891, seemed to recover in the spring, and sank again in the late autumn. He was buried on 15 October beside his teacher, father Macarius, by his disciple the hieroschemamonk Joseph; he had appointed that day for the feast of an icon of the Mother of God he had had painted, and it became the day of his own burial. He had foretold that a bad odour would come from his body, saying it was because in his life he had received much honour he had not earned, and the life says it grew less as the days passed until on the last day a sweet scent came from it.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 10 October, and gives his household and his dates. saint.gr says Ambrose was born on 23 November 1812 in the village of Great Lipovitsa in the region of Tambov; that his father was called Michael Theodorovich Grenkov and his mother Martha Nikolaevna; that they had eight children in all and that he was the sixth of them; and that his baptismal name was Alexander. His devout family brought him up by the commands of the Gospel, though he himself was from childhood very lively as well as very intelligent.

He studied at a priestly seminary and was at first a teacher; then he became a monk at Optina, a deacon on 2 February 1843, and a priest at the end of 1845 — the page gives 9 December — at the age of thirty-three. Soon afterwards his health grew worse, but his life was a holy one and of the greatest benefit to those about him. He had the gift of foresight, and in 1872 he founded a women's community. The trials he underwent were many, and he stood through them a rock of patience and a teacher of the divine virtues in deed. He died on 10 October 1891, and was canonised in 1990.

The Russian calendar gives him the day's great rank — the polyeleos icon stands immediately before «Прп. Амвросия Оптинского (1891)» and before no other line of the day — and the life it reaches is long enough to be where almost everything here now comes from. He was Alexander Mikhailovich Grenkov in the world, born on the twenty-first or the twenty-third of November 1812 in a clergy family of the village of Bolshiye Lipovitsy in the Tambov diocese. He finished the church school and the seminary successfully and then went neither to the academy nor into the priesthood: he was for a time a tutor in a landowner's household and then a master at the Lipetsk church school. Lively, cheerful, kind and quick-witted, he was much loved by his fellows.

In his last year at the seminary he fell dangerously ill and vowed to become a monk if he recovered. He recovered and did not forget the vow, but put it off for some years — he hesitated, in his own word — and his conscience gave him no peace, so that spells of carefree gaiety alternated with spells of sharp grief, hard prayer and tears. Once at Lipetsk, walking in a wood nearby and standing on the bank of a stream, he distinctly heard in its noise the words: praise God, love God. At home, away from curious eyes, he prayed ardently to the Mother of God to enlighten his mind and direct his will.

He did not have a determined will, and said so to his spiritual children in old age: you must obey me from the first word; I am a yielding man, and if you argue with me I may give in to you, and that will not be for your good. Worn out by his own indecision he went for counsel to a known ascetic of the district named Hilarion, who told him: go to Optina, and you will be experienced. He obeyed, and arrived at Optina in the autumn of 1839, where the elder [Leo](/saints/leo-of-optina) received him kindly.

He was soon tonsured and named Ambrose, in memory of the hierarch of Milan, then ordained hierodeacon and later hieromonk. When [Macarius](/saints/macarius-of-optina) began his publishing work he was one of his closest helpers, being a seminary man who knew the ancient and the modern languages — five of them. Soon after his ordination he fell ill, and the illness was so heavy and so long that it broke his health for good and all but tied him to his bed; because of it he could never serve the liturgy or take part in the long monastic services to the end of his life. The page reads that illness as providential: it tempered his lively character, kept him perhaps from self-conceit, and made him enter more deeply into himself. It is a monk's profit to be ill, he said afterwards, and in illness one should not be cured but only half-cured.

He went on with the publishing after Macarius died, and the Ladder of John Climacus and Macarius's own letters and life came out under his hand. But that was not the centre of his work. His soul looked for living and personal dealings with people, and he came quickly to be known as an experienced guide in practical as well as spiritual things, with a mind the page calls unusually quick, sharp, observant and penetrating, lit and deepened by constant concentrated prayer; and by the grace of God that penetration passed over into seeing what could not be seen.

The page keeps one of the answers. A young priest who had asked a year before for the very last parish in the diocese could not bear its poverty and came to ask a blessing to move; the elder shouted to him from a distance: go back, father, he is one and there are two of you. Asked what that meant, he said: the devil who tempts you is one, and you have God for a helper — go back and fear nothing; it is a sin to leave a parish; serve the liturgy every day and all will be well. The priest went back, worked patiently, and years later was famous as a second elder Ambrose.

Tolstoy came away from a conversation with him saying that this father Ambrose is a wholly holy man, that he had spoken with him and his soul had become light and glad, and that when one speaks with such a man one feels the nearness of God. Yevgeny Pogozhev said that what struck him was the holiness and the unfathomable depth of love in him, and that looking at him he began to understand that the meaning of the elders is to bless and approve life and the joys God sends, to teach people to live happily, and to help them carry whatever burdens fall to them. Rozanov wrote that the gold had gone through the fire of scepticism and had not tarnished.

His day in the Optina skete began at four or five in the morning with the morning rule, which took more than two hours, and after it he was alone to pray and to prepare. From nine he received, monastics first and then lay people, until dinner; at about two a scanty meal came, and an hour or an hour and a half alone; then vespers, and the receiving again until night; at about eleven the long evening rule, and not before midnight was he alone at last. He did not like to be seen praying and the cell-attendant read the rule from another room. One monk broke the prohibition once and came in, and saw him sitting on the bed with his eyes fixed on heaven and his face lit with joy.

He kept that up, day after day, for more than thirty years, and in the last ten he took on one more thing: the founding and ordering of a women's house at Shamordino, twelve versts from Optina, which held a thousand nuns besides an orphanage and school for girls, an almshouse for old women and a hospital. The page calls it not only one more material care but a cross laid on him by Providence, and the thing that ended his ascetic life.

He spent the whole summer of 1891 at Shamordino as though hurrying to finish what was unfinished there, and though he named days for his departure more than once, obeying the consistory's orders, his growing weakness made him put them off. Word came at last that the bishop himself, displeased with the delay, meant to come and take him away. The bishop had got half-way, and had stopped for the night at the Peremyshl monastery, when a telegram was handed to him announcing the elder's death. He changed colour and said in confusion: what does this mean? It was the evening of 10 October, the 22nd new style. He was advised to go back to Kaluga the next day and answered: no, this is evidently the will of God; archbishops do not bury simple hieromonks, but this is a particular hieromonk — I wish to perform the burial myself.

It was decided to carry him back to Optina, where he had spent his life and where his own guides, the elders Leo and Macarius, lay. On the marble over him are the Apostle's words that he became weak to the weak that he might gain the weak, and all things to all men that he might by all means save some; and the page says those words say exactly what his life's labour meant.
