# Paul (Yagodinsky)

Paul Yagodinsky was born on 8 December 1879 in the village of Saigushi in
the Ardatov district of Simbirsk province, today the Chamzinka district of
Mordovia, the son of the psalmist of the local church. He was educated at
the seminary, married in 1900 and was ordained deacon, serving in the
village of Shugurovo in the Ardatov district, and after a time was
ordained priest there; five sons were born to him — Nicholas in 1901,
Michael in 1907, Constantine and Leonid in 1914, Sergius in 1918. By the
recollections he was hard-working, kind and scrupulously honest, did not
try to hide his thoughts on what was happening in the years after the
revolution and spoke of what troubled him and displeased him, and so his
name came into the lists of the counter-revolutionary kulak rising — the
so-called “chapan” rising — that broke out in the district in 1919. In
the twenties his household, like all the clergy’s, was taxed very heavily,
harder to pay each year, and in 1929 he was sentenced as a non-payer of
state taxes to eight months of forced labour, which he served. Life was
changing sharply, and the minds of believers with it: some parishioners
listened more to the new authorities, some waited, but part of the village
still trusted its pastor, came to him for support and counsel and helped
him, and he did his priestly duty carefully, baptising the newborn,
marrying and burying, rousing many to action, heartening many with warm
encouragement, supporting the weak and defending the wronged. In 1930 the
district executive committee closed the church at Shugurovo and he was
arrested; after that his former parishioners could turn to him only now
and then when some rite had to be performed, and freed in 1933 he moved
to the church of Buzaevo in the Bolshiye Berezniki district. Widowed by
then, he lived by turns with his sons, each of whom had found his way by
1937 — Nicholas a military doctor at Sverdlovsk, Michael and Constantine
collective farmers, Leonid in the Red Army in the Far East, Sergius in a
military band in Moscow. Once the chairman of the village soviet advised
him to give up his trade, renounce his orders and take some settled job,
and he answered in plain calm words: no, I have not renounced and have
not thought of renouncing the rank of priest; my religious convictions do
not allow it; I shall die in the rank of priest. On 5 August 1937, at his
house at Shugurovo, a search found nothing unlawful, and the same day the
Chekists arrested him — the third and last arrest — and put him in the
cells of the district NKVD, then in the Saransk prison. Questioned that
day he repeated that he did not mean to lay aside his orders, and to the
charge of spreading slanderous rumours of famine in the Soviet Union and
the inevitable fall of the Soviet power answered: though I am a convinced
believer, I carried on no anti-Soviet agitation anywhere. The indictment
of 13 August recorded that the accused did not admit guilt; the troika of
the NKVD of the Mordovian republic, sitting on 22–23 August, charged him
with active part in organising the counter-revolutionary kulak rising and
calling the people to terrorist acts against communists, and sentenced him
to be shot, and the sentence was carried out between ten and midnight on
3 September 1937 — 21 August of the old style, the day the Russian
calendar keeps him. The Council of Bishops of 13–16 August 2000 glorified
him among the new martyrs and confessors of Russia.
