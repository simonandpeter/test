# Jude, brother of the Lord

Jude was one of the twelve apostles of Christ, of the line of David and Solomon. He was born in Nazareth of Galilee, of his father Joseph the carpenter, who was afterwards the betrothed of the most pure Virgin Mary, and of his mother Salome — not the Salome of Bethlehem, doxologia says, but another, the daughter of Haggai, son of Barachiah and brother of Zacharias. Joseph took her in lawful marriage, and she bore him the sons named in the Gospel of Matthew: James, Joses, Simon and Jude.

This Jude is called Jude of James, that is the brother of James who is called the brother of the Lord; out of humility he held himself unworthy to be called the Lord's brother after the flesh, because at the beginning he had sinned against him in ignorance, partly through unbelief and partly through want of brotherly love. Of the unbelief, the page cites the words of Saint John, "Neither did his brethren believe in him," and Theophylact's comment that the sons of Joseph reviled him out of a rotten judgement and out of envy, since it is the way of relations always to hate their own more than strangers.

The page then turns to his epistle, and says that he showed great things in few words. He writes against those who would turn the grace of God into an excuse and take up teachings unworthy of salvation, and sets before them the angels and the men whom God punished: the evil angels, whom God bound in darkness with everlasting bonds and keeps for his judgement because they did not keep their own order, and the people brought out of Egypt, whom he destroyed in the wilderness because they did not walk after God's calling but turned aside into stubbornness.

He bore many pains and labours, passing through many countries, preaching Christ, baptising, confirming the peoples and guiding them to salvation. Then, having gone into the parts of Ararat and turned a multitude from the error of idols to Christ, he was taken by the idolaters, and after they had tortured him much they hanged him on a tree in the form of a cross; and being pierced with arrows by the unbelievers he finished his contest and his course, and passed over to Christ God.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 19 June, and saint.gr opens by saying that the biographical data of Saint Jude are somewhat confused, because they are mixed up with those of the Apostle Thaddaeus whose memory is kept on 21 August. It then sets two authorities side by side. S. Eustratiades in his Hagiology, it says, has this Jude a brother of Christ and brother in the flesh of the Apostle James the brother of God, Jude and James being children of Joseph by another marriage; and that by tradition Jude preached the Gospel in Mesopotamia, visited Edessa, and was seized at the city of Ararat by unbelievers, who put him to death by shooting him with arrows.

Michael Galanos in his Lives of the Saints, on the other hand, has the Apostle Jude one of the Twelve, son of Alphaeus and brother of James the Less, who was an apostle too; that he bore the surname Thaddaeus or Lebbaeus; that after Pentecost, having worked a while in the Church of Jerusalem, he preached the Gospel in Mesopotamia amid many dangers, and at last was martyred at Edessa. Both authorities agree, the page insists, that he has nothing whatever to do with Judas Iscariot. It closes by quoting the short catholic epistle of Jude in the New Testament, at the twentieth and twenty-first verses, on building oneself up on the foundation of the most holy faith and keeping oneself in the love of God.

saint.gr also reports, on Eustratiades's authority, that the Lavriotic codex I 78, folio 2156, carries on this same day the memory of another holy Apostle Jude, called the Zealot, who after the Ascension went about all the cities and villages teaching the saving message of the risen Christ and, having brought a multitude to repentance, reposed in peace. That second Jude is a notice out of a manuscript and not a line of the day's listing, and no folder is made for him here.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 19 June, the same menologion day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep him on, and gives the same genealogy the Romanian page gave: Salome the daughter of Haggai, son of Barachiah, and Joseph the carpenter, and their four sons James, Josiah, Simon and Jude. It gives no year, as neither of the other books did. It adds one scene that explains his humility better than the explanation does. When the aged Joseph, before his death, wished to give Jesus a share of the property along with his other children, all of them opposed it, and Jude among them; only James willingly divided a part off his own share and set it aside for Jesus.

So, this book says, though he might have called himself the Lord’s brother as much as James did, he did not do it, out of humility and out of shame, because at the beginning he had not believed. His epistle opens, and the Prologue quotes it with its reference: Jude, servant of Jesus Christ, brother of James. He is called Levi too, and Thaddeus — and the book is careful to say that there is another Thaddeus, of the Seventy, kept on another day, while this Thaddeus, or Jude, was one of the great apostles.

He preached the Gospel through Judea, Samaria, Galilee, Idumaea, Syria, Arabia, Mesopotamia and Armenia — eight regions, where the Greek page named two. At Edessa, the city of Abgar, he completed the preaching of that other Thaddeus, which is the one place this book brings the two men near each other, and it is a succession in a city and not a kinship. And when he was preaching in the parts about Ararat he was taken by the pagans, crucified on a cross and killed with arrows, that he might reign for ever in the kingdom of Christ.
