# Mitrophan of Constantinople

Mitrophan lived in the days of Constantine the Great, the first Christian emperor. He was the son of Dometius the Roman, a man of imperial family, for Dometius was the brother of Probus, who had been emperor at Rome before. Dometius, being a man of understanding, saw through the deceit and the wandering of idol-worship, and knowing the Christian faith to be right and true he renounced the lying gods and believed in the true God, our Lord Jesus Christ.

Because in those days great impiety of idol-madness was done at Rome, and innocent Christians were killed there every day without number, Dometius left Rome and came to Byzantium with his two sons, Probus and Mitrophan. The bishop in Byzantium then was Titus, a holy man well pleasing to God. With him Dometius stayed, he and his sons, learning the faith in Christ and the law of the Lord and being well guided in a life of good works; and the bishop, seeing him cleaved to Christ with his whole heart and soul, burning in spirit and serving the Lord, set him among the clergy of the church and ordained him priest.

When Bishop Titus departed to the Lord, Dometius took the see after him; and after the end of the blessed bishop Dometius, his son Probus took the patriarchal throne; and after Probus was taken from this life, this Saint Mitrophan, the son of Dometius and the brother of Probus, was raised to the hierarch's throne of Byzantium. Then the great emperor Constantine, coming into the parts of Thrace and visiting Byzantium, saw Mitrophan, spoke with him, and knew him for a man greatly pleasing to God.

He marvelled at his amended life and at his wisdom and loved him very much, and wishing to hear his God-inspired words he took him with him to Rome. Afterwards the great Constantine determined to move the imperial throne from Rome to Byzantium, for he judged the place very beautiful and was pleased that an imperial city should stand there, since it was rich in the fruits of the earth and set beside the Euxine Sea between Europe and Asia, with good roads by sea and by land. So he founded a great city with fair walls and called it after his own name, Constantinople, or New Rome.

He brought Mitrophan with him from old Rome to the new, calling him his father, and obtained for him the patriarchal honour from the three hundred and eighteen holy fathers who took part in the first Ecumenical Council at Nicaea; and so Mitrophan was the first patriarch of Constantinople. Being unable, for his old age and his weakness, to come to that council himself, he sent in his place his chorbishop [Alexander](/saints/alexander-patriarch-of-constantinople), an honourable, holy and aged man who had borne many labours in Thrace and in Illyricum for the peace of the Church, and who held the patriarch's place in the council and contended against Arius.

When the council was finished, the right-believing emperor asked all the hierarchs to go with him to the most holy patriarch Mitrophan, who was ill on his deathbed, and to visit him. They went to him with the emperor on a Sunday, and as they talked the emperor said: «O most honoured father, I see thee weakened by age and sickness; tell us, then, and show us who will be worthy after thee to be shepherd of the flock.»

Mitrophan answered with a cheerful face that the Holy Spirit had truly spoken through the emperor's mouth, since he had been thinking on that very thing seven days before, and the Lord had revealed to him that in ten days he would go from here; that after him Alexander his servant would come to the patriarchal throne, being truly worthy of the choice and of the gift of the Holy Spirit; and that after Alexander the heir of the throne would be Paul, who was then among the readers.

Then, looking towards the divine Alexander, patriarch of Alexandria, he said: «O brother, thou too wilt leave a chosen heir after thee.» And taking by the hand [Athanasius](/saints/athanasius-of-alexandria), his archdeacon, he said: «Behold the brave soldier of Christ. He will be thy heir after thee, and not only will he stand firm with my brother Alexander against the impiety of Arius, but he will enter into great struggles; and many sufferings await him, together with the brave Paul.»

So the saint prophesied what was to come; and after those ten days from the Lord's revelation he ended his life in peace, on the fourth day of June, having lived from his birth a hundred and seventeen years. The page gives no year for his death. He lives now, it says, in the life that has no end, standing before the throne of the great High Priest who has passed through the heavens, Christ our Saviour, whose glory is unto the ages.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 4 June, and saint.gr puts years and kin to a life doxologia left undated. It says he was the first bishop of Constantinople, from 313 to 327, the son of Dometios and the nephew of the emperor Probus. When the First Ecumenical Council met at Nicaea in May 325 he could not be there himself, being by then very old and ill, and he sent as his representative the protopresbyter Alexander, a willing and God-fearing man.

In the years of his archbishopric, the page says, many great building works of the Reigning City were founded, and among them the famous churches of Holy Wisdom, of Saint Irene and of Holy Power. Metrophanes died in 327 — possibly, the page adds, at the age of a hundred and seventeen — and left as his successor his worthy protopresbyter Alexander, who is honoured on 30 August; for he believed that the saving work of the Church is advanced still more by a right episcopal succession, answering so to the word of Scripture that a bishop must be blameless, that nobody may be able to say anything against him.
