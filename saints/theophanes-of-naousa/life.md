# Theophanes of Naousa

Theophanes was born at Ioannina and as a young man left everything for the Holy Mountain, where he was tonsured at the monastery of Docheiariou; in fasting, prayer, vigil and the denial of every needless thing he was a pattern to all the monks, and in time was chosen abbot. Later, over some disagreement with the monks, he left the Mountain and came with his sister’s son to Veria in Macedonia, where he founded a monastery in honour of the Mother of God; when it flourished in the spiritual life he left his nephew to govern it and went to Naousa, where he founded another, to the holy Archangels. He died in peace in the fifteenth century, and his wonderworking relics lie at Naousa to this day, the Prologue says, and show forth the great power of God.

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 19 August, and doxologia.ro prints the Ohrid Prologue's notice of him in Romanian. It says he was born at Ioannina, left his father's house while still young and went to the Holy Mountain, where he was tonsured a monk in the monastery of Docheiariou; that he became an example to all by the holiness of his life, in vigils, fasting, prayer and every labour, and was for that chosen abbot by the whole community with one heart and one mouth. When trouble arose in the monastery through the devil's tempting he left the Mountain with his nephew and went to Veria in Macedonia, where they settled in a monastery dedicated to the Mother of God; and when that community was established in holy living he left it in his nephew's charge and withdrew into the wilderness of Mount Naousa, where he founded a community in the name of the Holy Archangels. He fell asleep in peace in the fifteenth century, and his wonderworking relics rest on Mount Naousa.

The Greek calendar keeps Theophanes on the same day, 19 August, and calls him the patron of Naousa in Imathia. saint.gr says he was born at Ioannina at the beginning of the sixteenth century, that while still young he left for the Holy Mountain and joined the community of the monastery of Docheiariou, and that the virtue he tried to hide showed him to the brethren, who made him their abbot after the repose of their elder. In those years his sister wrote to him that the Turks had seized her son and taken him to Constantinople to make him a Janissary; Theophanes went to the City, found the boy before they had changed his faith, and after great efforts got him free and brought him away.

They returned to the Holy Mountain, and after a probation he tonsured his nephew a monk; but the brotherhood feared Turkish reprisal, the community divided over it, and Theophanes ended the quarrel by leaving with the boy for Thessalonica and then Beroia. There they were moved by what they heard of the skete, stayed at the monastery of the Forerunner, and were given a blessing to build a kathisma ten minutes below it towards the river, in the name of the Panagia. Disciples gathered, the skete's valley was full with its fifty brotherhoods, and so he raised the monastery of the Taxiarchs near Naousa instead.

At the building of it, the page says, the master mason would not approve the site, and Theophanes asked the Archangels for a sign: the plans were laid down in one place and wherever the Archangels moved them, there the monastery was to be built — and they were found where he had said. Another time a bear tore apart the little donkey that carried the materials, and he yoked the bear in the beast's place. He spent his last years going between the skete and the monastery of the Archangels, and in old age withdrew to the kathisma of the Panagia, where he died on 19 August and was buried in the monastic manner.

After his exhumation the fathers, certain of the holiness of his life, set his skull in a worked silver case among the relics of the monastery of the Forerunner, and reburied the rest of the bones under a shrine. Centuries later the Turks destroyed the skete, the kathisma came down and the grave was buried in the rubble; at the beginning of the twentieth century the skull was stolen by men of Naousa who wanted it in their own town. The grave was opened in 1926 and the bones — some sixty pieces — were placed in the sanctuary of the monastery of the Forerunner; today, the page ends, only a few fragments survive.
