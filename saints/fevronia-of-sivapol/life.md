# Fevronia of Sivapol

In the days of the pagan emperor Diocletian there was at Rome a prefect named Anthimus, whose son Lysimachus was betrothed to the daughter of a senator called Prosphorus. Dying, Anthimus gave the young man into the charge of his brother Selinus. The emperor then sent the two east to destroy the Christian faith, promising Lysimachus his father's office on his return; and they went with an army, taking with them a kinsman of Lysimachus, the count Primus.

In Mesopotamia, at Palmyra, they destroyed the Christians with every kind of punishment, for Selinus was savage and without humanity; but Lysimachus pitied the Christians, and his mother, the life says, had taught him not to shed their blood. From Palmyra they went on to the city of Sivapol, which the life places within the borders of the Assyrians and under the rule of Rome.

In that city stood a convent whose abbess was Bryene. Fevronia had been taken into her hands at two years old and had never been seen by anyone of the world; she read the Scriptures to the sisters and taught them, and her teaching was famous through the whole city. Hieria, a noblewoman, came to hear her in a nun's habit, spent a night weeping over what she had never heard, and brought her parents and their whole household to Christ and to baptism.

When word came that Selinus and Lysimachus were near, the clergy of the city fled and the bishop hid. Bryene would not let her nuns go: they had not yet seen the war and were already beaten. One sister, Etheria, argued for flight, and in the end all the sisters went, kissing Bryene and Fevronia as they left; only Thomais, the second after the abbess, stayed. Fevronia, who was ill, said that Christ her bridegroom was alive, and that she would not leave that place but would die there and be buried there.

At dawn the soldiers broke the convent doors with axes and would have killed Bryene, and Fevronia threw herself at their feet begging to be killed first. Primus drove them out and told the women to flee while they could; and going back, he told Lysimachus of the young nun's beauty. Lysimachus said he would not be an enemy to the brides of Christ, and asked him to take them out of the convent and hide them from Selinus's hands.

Selinus had her brought out and stripped before the crowd, and she answered him that no wrestler goes to the contest clothed, and that she came naked to the struggle to overcome his father Satan. She was stretched out over a fire and beaten with rods by four men; hung on the rack, her sides torn with iron combs and her wounds burnt; and when she would not answer, seventeen of her teeth were drawn out one by one. The people cried out again and again for the judge to spare her, and he would not.

He had her breasts cut off and the wounds burnt; Hieria cried out at him from the crowd, asking whether he had not himself been fed at such breasts, and was only spared because his friends feared the city would rise. Then he ordered both her hands cut off with an axe, and both her feet, and she herself stretched out the second foot to the block. Hours later, hearing that she still breathed, he had her throat cut like a sheep's and her head taken off; and Lysimachus went home from the spectacle weeping.

Selinus, hearing his nephew's lamentation, rose up and walked about the palace, and a sudden terror fell on him: he was struck dumb, roared like an ox, and dashed his head against a marble pillar and fell dead. Lysimachus said, Great is the God of the Christians; blessed is the God of Fevronia; God has avenged innocent blood. He had Selinus buried outside the city after the pagan custom, and told Primus to make a coffin of incorruptible wood, to call all the Christians without fear, and to gather up the severed head and hands and feet and breasts and teeth, and the very earth her blood had fallen on, and bring them to the convent.

Bryene fell as one dead at the sight, and the sisters who had gone with Etheria came back and venerated the body with tears. When a bishop afterwards came to take a portion of the relics for a church he was building, the earth shook so that the whole city was moved and he could not open the coffin; at his entreaty, and at Bryene's, the martyr's own hand gave him one of the teeth that lay on her breast. It was carried away in a small gold vessel with psalms and candles, the church was consecrated with it, and the blind saw and the lame were made straight that day. Bryene lived two years more, set Thomais in her place and went to the Lord; and Thomais, who wrote this life, says she saw Fevronia's passion with her own eyes and had the rest from Lysimachus's own mouth.

The life names Diocletian and prints no year, so none is recorded here.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 25 June, and tells the same story with the places and the year named. saint.gr says she was a bride much sought after for her bodily beauty, and that her pure soul shone as brightly; that for this reason, at seventeen, she chose the way of ascetic struggle and self-restraint in the monastery where her aunt Bryene was abbess, in Mesopotamia, in the city of Nisibis, which is called Antioch of Mygdonia and lay on the border of the Byzantine and the Persian states. She fitted herself quickly to the hard rules of the monastic life, found time besides to study and go deep in the Scriptures, and became a pattern among the other nuns for her prudence, her zeal, her readiness and her humble mind. When the troop hunting Christians under Selinos reached the monastery, which the page puts in 288, the other nuns got away; she was ill and could not be moved, and the abbess Bryene and the sister Thomais stayed by her.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her on the Julian 25 June, the same menologion day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep her on, and tells the story short with several things in it that neither of them has. She was the daughter of a senator of Rome, it says, and betrothed herself to Christ and took the habit in the East, in the land of the Assyrians, in the monastery where her aunt was abbess. Lysimachus wished to marry her; but the emperor Diocletian suspected him of being a secret Christian and sent him east with his uncle to arrest and kill Christians — where the Romanian account above has the emperor promise him his father’s office on his return. The uncle was savage as a beast and spared nobody; Lysimachus spared the Christians wherever he could and hid them from him.

Having laid the Christians of Palmyra waste he came to the city of Sivapol, near which was a convent of fifty women ascetics, and she was one of them; she was only twenty, this book says, and was honoured both in the house and in the city for her great meekness, wisdom and self-restraint. In that monastery, it adds, the rule of blessed [Platonida](/saints/platonida-the-venerable-6-april), a former abbess, was kept: that every Friday be spent in prayer and in the reading of the holy books only, without any other work. The abbess had appointed her to read the holy books to the sisters standing hidden behind a curtain, so that nobody should be distracted and taken by the beauty of her face.

When she would neither deny Christ nor consent to marry a mortal man she was scourged, her teeth were knocked out, and her hands, then her breasts, then her feet were cut off, and at the last she was beheaded. But that same day a terrible punishment of God overtook her torturer: a frenzy entered into him and a deathly horror took hold of him, and in it he struck his head against a marble pillar and fell dead. Lysimachus had her body gathered up and carried to the monastery, where it was honourably buried, and he himself was baptised with many other soldiers.

Many healings came of her relics; and she used to appear on her feast day and stand in her usual place among the sisters, and all the sisters looked on her with fear and joy. This book ends by saying that she suffered honourably and passed over into eternal blessedness in 310, and that in 363 her relics were taken to Constantinople. The 310 is the year recorded on this folder; the 363 is a translation of relics and is recorded in no field, a translation not being a repose. The Greek account above dates the troop reaching the monastery to 288, which is not written into the field either, that year being out of another book than the one read here.
