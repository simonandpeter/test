# Symeon the Barefoot

Symeon the one-tunicked and barefoot was born in the village of Vathyrema of Agia, to a priest father — papa Andreas, a teacher of the hidden school — and to Aikaterini. From a small child he showed signs of virtue and of holiness; he learned the sacred books and helped his father, chanting at the services. The village's Turk-loving headman wanted to marry him to his daughter Triantafyllia, and threatened his father that if the son did not consent the child-levy would take him.

So by force, one evening in his own house, he married Symeon to his daughter, with a priest brought in from elsewhere. Within the year his wife bore a boy, whom they named Demetrios; but Symeon was deeply grieved and went about full of thought. To his father-in-law, who asked him, he confessed that the child was not his and that he had never known Triantafyllia as a wife. Since nobody believed him, death by fire was decided on; and while the fire was being made ready in the square, Symeon asked to see his wife one last time before the people. He went up to the boy and asked him who his father was, and the child, speaking wonderfully, pointed out the village's field-guard — and so Symeon was acquitted.

He first became a monk at the monastery of Oikonomeion, or Komnineion, of Kissavos, living, the page says, «με αυστηράν νηστείαν, άμετρον αγρυπνίαν, ολονύκτιον στάσιν, ανυπόδητος και μονοχίτων» — with strict fasting, measureless vigil, standing all night, barefoot and in one tunic, wearing only a poor old torn garment. There he was ordained deacon. He kept the same life on the Holy Mountain at the Great Lavra, where he was ordained priest, and when the monks of Philotheou came and begged him to take their spiritual direction he agreed and laid a strict order upon them.

Some would not come into obedience: they rebelled, and on the Sunday of Pascha they tied him to a cypress outside the monastery church, beat him hard and shut him in the monastery tower. With one monk's help he left Athos for Pelion, to the region of Flamouri, where he stayed three years under an apple tree, suffering bitterly from the cold in winter and from the heat of the sun in summer. Then he built the famous monastery of the Holy Trinity of Flamouri, where a multitude of monks gathered, and afterwards went about preaching — a forerunner, the page says, of Cosmas of Aetolia — through Agia, Tyrnavos, Elassona, Lamia, Servia, Grevena, Agrafa, Thebes, Athens, Euboea and Epirus.

In Euboea he was accused of trying to make Christians of the Turks, and the pasha decided to burn him in the square; it is even said that he helped to pile the wood himself. But when the pasha saw him standing barefoot and poor before him in his old rags, he revered him and let him go. Back at Flamouri he found new monks and needed a monastery built quickly; having no money, he went to Constantinople to ask the patriarch for leave and for help. In the patriarch's antechamber a Turkish officer who had served in his own village recognised his holiness and ran to the sultan, Suleiman the Magnificent, whose daughter was gravely ill.

Symeon healed her wonderfully, and the sultan, to thank him, gave him the girl's jewels and sent by ship to Flamouri on Pelion all the building material the monastery needed; and so the monastery of the Transfiguration of Flamouri was built, a five-domed cross-in-square basilica of Athonite type. On 19 April 1594, at over a hundred years of age, he visited the City, and there he reposed in the Lord and was buried at Halki in the Princes' Islands. His disciples from Flamouri took up his honoured relics at the exhumation and brought them to his own monastery, where his skull is kept to this day.
