# Plesius

Plesius was from Egypt, one of six young men who suffered at Caesarea in Palestine under the governor Urban, in the second year of the persecution Diocletian ordered. Doxologia's life for the day keeps the six together and names them: Plesius, [Timolaus](/saints/timolaus), Romulus, two called Alexander and one called Dionysius. A pagan feast was being kept in the city, and a great crowd had come in from the villages around to watch the Christians tormented.

Seeing it, the six bound their own hands behind their backs — the sign, the life says, that they wished without fear to suffer for Christ and were ready to be given either to the fire or to the beasts — and ran into the middle of the spectacle, crying out before the governor that they were Christians.

Urban would not destroy them at once, because they were young in body; he coaxed them to sacrifice, and then had them imprisoned. A few days later [Agapius](/saints/agapius-of-caesarea-in-palestine), long known among the Christians for his confession, was shut up with them together with his servant, making eight. All were tried and tormented repeatedly, all refused to deny Christ, and all were beheaded on this day.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the company on the same menologion day and tells it differently again. Its heading is „Свети мученик Агапије и седморица с њим“ — Agapius and seven with him — and it says they all suffered at Caesarea in Palestine under the prince Urban, in the time of the emperor Diocletian. All seven except Agapius, it says, were quite young men, and were not yet Christians, nor had they ever been baptised with water; but their baptism was by blood.

One day the seven youths watched Christians being tortured — some on the fire, some on the gallows, some before the beasts — and seeing with what patience they bore it all they took fire with zeal for Christ, bound their own hands behind them, and came so bound before Urban, saying: we too are Christians. His flatteries and his threats were wasted on them. [Agapius](/saints/agapius-of-caesarea-in-palestine), a man of standing in that city who had already suffered much for Christ, joined himself to the youths and kindled in them the faith and the love of the Lord still further. All were beheaded with the sword in 303, which falls inside the reign this folder already records and is not written over it, and passed over to the courts of the King of Heaven.

One name differs. Where the Greek line reads Πλήσιος and the Romanian Plisie, the Prologue reads Публије — the same slot, in the same company of eight, in the same city and the same persecution, and this folder is read as that man. The Serbian form recorded here is therefore Публије and not a Serbian spelling of Plesius, because Публије is what the page prints.
