# Barlaam Hutinski

doxologia.ro's calendar names him and gives no life. The line for 6 noiembrie reads «Sfântul Varlaam Hutinski», and his page on the site carries the name, the day, the epistle and gospel appointed for the day, and twelve icons — it has no Viață tab and no Tropar tab, and the twelve pages filed under «Articole despre sfânt» are the icons again and carry no text.

The one sentence of prose the page itself prints is its own description: «Curând, călugărul a fost cunoscut în toată țara și au început să vină la el pentru sfătuire și binecuvântare conducători, nobili și oameni obișnuiți, unii dintre ei cerând chiar permisiunea de a rămâne împreună cu monahul îmbunătățit» — soon the monk was known through the whole country, and rulers, nobles and ordinary people began to come to him for counsel and blessing, some of them asking leave to stay with him. That he was a monk is all the page says of his state; it names no country, no century, no monastery, no year and no manner of death.

The epithet «Hutinski» is kept here as the calendar prints it, because the page nowhere writes out the place it stands for.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 6 November, under the same epithet — «ὁ ἐν Χουτινῇ», he of Khutyn — and says as plainly as doxologia did that it has no life for him: «Δεν έχουμε λεπτομέρειες για τον βίο του Οσίου». What its page does carry, and the Romanian one did not, is the year of his death in the head line, «(; - 1192)», with no year of birth. It prints no couplet, no hymn and nothing of his monastery.

saint.gr said it had no details of his life. days.pravoslavie.ru has two life pages behind one index, and they are the whole of this folder's story. He was Алексей in the world, the son of rich and notable citizens of great Novgorod, «Михаил» and «Анна», and from his childhood he kept away from games and company, read the holy books and spent his time at home in prayer and fasting. When his parents, afraid for his health, pressed him to spare himself, he answered that nowhere in all his reading had he found parents advising their children to anything bad, and that it is not food and drink but fasting and prayer that bring a man to the kingdom of heaven; and they let him live as he wished.

**The two pages disagree about how he became a monk.** The longer one has him give away everything he owned after his parents died and go out into the wilderness to an ascetic named «Порфирий», who tonsured him with the name Barlaam; the shorter has him withdraw as a youth to the suburban Лисичий monastery and be tonsured there. Both are recorded and neither is chosen. They agree on what followed: he settled ten versts from Novgorod at a place called Хутынь, a bad place with an evil name that people were afraid to walk to, and he knew it was the right one because a bright ray shone out of the thick of the forest as he came to it.

He lived there in one cell, working all day and praying all night, in a hair shirt and chains the monastery still kept afterwards, and the well he dug himself is still whole. Men came, and a wooden church of the Transfiguration was built in memory of that light, and then a stone one in its place before he died. He told princes and governors to remember three things: that they rule over men like themselves, that they must rule by the laws, and that they will not rule for ever and must give God an account of their judgements.

The longer page gives him the gift of foresight and three stories of it. He stopped an executioner on the Volkhov bridge and asked the crowd for the condemned man, who was given him and became a monk at Khutyn; and at the next such killing he told his driver to drive on and would not hear the family, and explained afterwards that the first man had been justly condemned and had repented, so that God gave him time, and the second was condemned innocent and was let to die lest he become a bad man, and so received a martyr's crown instead.

He greeted the prince «Ярослав» with his newborn son before the news of the birth had come, and stood godfather to the child; that was in 1190. He promised the archbishop of Novgorod that he would come to him on a sledge on the Friday of the first week of the fast of the apostles, and deep snow fell in the night and a hard frost held all that Friday; and when the archbishop grieved over the weather he said that without it there would have been famine in the whole country, because the frost had killed the worms eating the roots of the corn. The harvest that year was unheard of.

He raised a peasant's son who had died on the road to the monastery, and then told the father he had been mistaken and the boy had only been numbed with cold, and forbade him to speak of it while he lived. Before his death he committed the brotherhood to «Антоний» — whom the longer page has coming from Jerusalem and the shorter from Constantinople — and said that he was leaving them in body only and would be with them in spirit. He died on 6 November 1192. Almost the whole city came to the burial, and the monastery kept his rule of giving alms on that day to every poor man who came, however many came.

The day page carries three hymns under his name — two troparia and a kontakion — and all three are kept here in the Church Slavonic it prints, with the English rendered for this site. Every bystander above is left in the calendar's own Cyrillic, parents, ascetic, prince and successor alike, because none of them is identified with any folder in this corpus and a name in a story is not a relation.

The Ohrid Prologue has him born and raised a Christian in great Novgorod, tonsured after his parents' death and given over to a hard ascetic labour, and founding his monastery on the bank of the river Volkhov at the place where a heavenly light was shown to him. In life and after his death a great wonderworker, it says: seeing into men's secrets, driving out unclean spirits, and healing every disease in people.

Two wonders stand in the entry. A servant of Prince Basil Vasilyevich was gravely ill and begged to be carried to the saint's grave, charging them that if he should die on the road they were to carry him dead to the saint; and so it happened — he died on the way, and they brought him dead into the monastery, where he came to life, stood up, and bowed before the grave. Then the tsar ordered the grave opened; and no sooner had they begun to uncover it than a flame rose out of it and licked up the walls of the church, and the tsar was so frightened that he fled the building, forgetting his staff in his haste, which the book says is kept beside the saint's grave to this day.

For the memory of that wonder, it adds, he is kept a second time on the Friday after the Sunday of All Saints — a feast reckoned from Pascha and not a fixed day, recorded in the Serbian attestation and not given a row of its own. The year the entry prints for the opening of the grave is 1471 and the tsar it names in the same sentence is Ivan the Terrible, and this reader could not make the two agree out of this book alone. Nothing is chosen from them: the year goes nowhere, the folder's own attested 6 November 1192 is untouched, and the disagreement is recorded here.
