# Mnason the Ancient Disciple

saint.gr's calendar for 19 October keeps «Άγιος Μνάσων ο αρχαίος μαθητής», Mnason the ancient disciple, and begins from the Acts of the Apostles, where he is named: after those days, Paul and Luke and their companions made ready and went up to Jerusalem, and some of the disciples came with them from Caesarea, bringing one Mnason, a Cypriot, an ancient disciple, in whose house they were to lodge.

From his synaxarion, the page says, we learn that Mnason was born at Tamasos of idolatrous parents. When he was grown, they sent him with his friend Theonas to Rome, to settle the quarrel between the idolaters of Politiko and the village of Pera over which of their false gods was the greater. At Rome the two friends met some of the apostles of the Seventy, who seem to have taken refuge there after the stoning of Stephen, and were taught by them about Jesus Christ. The little they heard lit in them a deep longing to know more, so they cut their stay short and left for Jerusalem, to find the Apostle Peter and John the Evangelist. In the Holy City they met the beloved disciple, heard from him the whole life and work of the Lord, received holy baptism, and went home to Cyprus.

There they learned with great joy that the apostles Barnabas and Mark and Paul had come, and Mnason set out to find them; in a cave he met the apostles with the newly enlightened bishop [Heraclides](/saints/heraclides-of-tamasos), and hurried to become his companion and helper. From the cave at Tamasos, which served them as a church, they went out preaching, and the word of the Cross became a spring of comfort and hope. God enriched Mnason with the gift of wonders. Passing the temple of Asclepius in the middle of Tamasos he was grieved for his countrymen, and said: in the name of Jesus Christ, the Son and Word of the true God, I command you to fall and be broken. The statue and the whole temple fell to pieces at once.

The false priests denounced him and the crowd rushed to kill him; he stood unmoved, looked them in the face and breathed upon them, and small and great lost their sight and cried out for mercy, so that he sealed each with the sign of the cross, gave them back their sight and baptised them, about three hundred in all. The page tells of a man raised from the tomb, at which four hundred idolaters believed and were baptised the same day, and of the hard creditor Alexander, struck dumb and withered for blaspheming, then forgiven, healed and baptised; of a river that stood still for him, of possessed and blind men healed, of a storm stilled and a shipwreck prevented, and of rain in a drought.

He succeeded his teacher and protector on the episcopal throne of Tamasos, and his work went on into deep old age. When the time came to leave the world he called his disciples, counselled them, told them not to grieve, and ordained Rhodon in his place by the common vote of all the citizens of Tamasos. Three days later, on 19 October, his soul left the earth; at the kissing of the relic, the page says, the blind saw, the lame walked and demons fled, and his disciples buried him near Heraclides. A monastery later stood a little beyond his tomb, of which only a small church survives; the Russian monk and traveller Vasily Barsky, going round Cyprus in 1735, reports it still working with six monks.

The same calendar keeps a second Mnason the day before, on 18 October, as bishop of Cyprus, and its whole notice of him is one sentence — the holy Mnason, bishop of Cyprus, was martyred by the sword — with the verse that Mnason, yielding like a tower and bowing his head, awaits a fall from the sword before he falls to the ground. The page then adds a conjecture of its own, and it is worth repeating exactly: it is possible, it says, that he is the same saint who is kept on 19 October as Mnason the ancient disciple, although that one fell asleep in peace.

This corpus held the two days as two folders. The author ruled on 3 October 2026 that they are one man, and this record is the two; the calendar's own «it is possible» stands, and so does the thing it stumbles over — one notice has him die by the sword and the other in peace in deep old age, and nothing in either page reconciles them.
