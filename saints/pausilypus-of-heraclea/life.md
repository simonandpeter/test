# Pausilypus

saint.gr's line for the day calls him «Άγιος Παυσίλυπος ο εν Ηρακλεία της Θράκης αθλήσας», Pausilypus who contended at Heraclea in Thrace, and the page behind it gives him a second form of the name, Παυσολύπιος. It sets him in the years of the emperor Hadrian, which it dates 117 to 138, and says he was born at Rome and came of a noble and wealthy family. Because he was encouraging and standing by the Christians who were walking to their martyrdom he was himself seized and brought before the emperor.

The emperor asked him whether he believed in Christ, and Pausilypus answered that he not only believed but was ready to shed his blood for him. For that he was handed over to Practus, the eparch of Europe, who gave orders that he be beaten with rods three hundred times without mercy. The martyr bore the torture and refused to sacrifice to the idols, and because he would not obey he was bound and led out to be beheaded.

On the road, the page says, he broke his bonds, got away from the soldiers, and so was delivered from a martyr's death by the sword. Having been saved he stood still in some place and prayed to God, and there he gave up his martyr's soul in love to the Lord, receiving an unfading crown and immortal life. The couplet over the entry plays on his name: Pausilypus, leaving the sojourning of earth, comes to a dwelling where grief is made to cease.

**The Russian church calendar keeps him on the Julian 8 April**, which is the day the Greek keeps him on, and its line is «Мч. Павсилипа (ок. 117-138).» — three words and a bracketed span of years. His name link, page 1706, is titled «Павсилип, мч.», rank abbreviation and nothing else, no see and no toponymic where the Greek line gives him Heraclea of Thrace; and **it is the only one of the eleven name pages behind this day that lists a single day of memory**, the other ten all carrying two or three. The Russian gives him no second form of the name, where the Greek page gave him Παυσολύπιος beside Παυσίλυπος.

**The span of years is the first date this folder has ever carried.** The line brackets «ок. 117-138», about 117 to 138, which is the reign the Greek page dates and attributes but does not turn into a date; it is written here as a death between 117 and 138 on a `traditional` basis, with both pages named in the note. No year is narrowed out of it and no birth is invented: a reign is a bound and not a date, which is why the interval is twenty-one years wide and the display says so.

Its life tells the same end as the Greek page and differs from it in four places. It was **a denunciation by the pagans** that brought him to trial, it says, and he was brought before the emperor himself, where he fearlessly declared himself a Christian; he was beaten with **iron rods**, where the Greek page has rods and counts three hundred strokes; and he was handed over to a governor it calls «Прецию», who is the Practus the Greek page makes eparch of Europe. He was unbending, and the governor ordered him put in irons and led out to execution.

**And on the road he prayed for exactly what happened.** The Russian life has him pray fervently that the Lord deliver him from the hands of the executioners and send him a speedy death; the Lord heard him, and the martyr, beaten and weakened, felt such strength that he tore the iron fetters apart and ran from his guards, who ran after him; and he died as he ran. **The Greek page gives the escape and the death but not the prayer**, and it has him stop and pray in some place and give up his soul there. The Russian closes with a thing no other book read here has: «Христиане с почестями погребли тело мученика», the Christians buried the body of the martyr with honours. The Russian prints no couplet and no hymn for him, and the folder stays without one.
