# Sabbas Stratelates

Sabbas lived in the time of Aurelian, emperor of Rome, and held the office of stratelates, which the Romanian life glosses as voievod, commander. He was a Goth by descent. The account calls him a faithful and well-pleasing servant of the heavenly King, and says that he visited those who were suffering in the prisons for Christ, served them out of his own goods, strengthened them to endure and urged them on to a fearless struggle. His life was so amended that for its purity and its austerity he was given power over the demons and drove unclean spirits out of men.

He was denounced to the emperor as a Christian. Brought forward, he confessed Christ boldly, threw down the soldier's belt and laid aside the rank of commander, showing himself ready for every torment. They hung him up and beat him and burned him with torches, then threw him into a cauldron of boiling pitch; and he came out of it whole and unhurt, kept by the unseen power of God. Seventy soldiers who saw that wonder believed in Christ and confessed him with a loud voice, and at the tyrant's word they were all beheaded.

Sabbas was thrown back into prison, where at midnight, as he was praying, Christ appeared to him shining with the light of his glory and told him not to fear but to take courage. Brought out for a second examination, he was pressed towards the idols by flattery and then by threats and by cruel torments, and would not yield; so they threw him into the river, and by drowning he came to the harbour that no storm troubles. The page names Aurelian but prints no year.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and gives him a nation, a reign and a year. Sabbas was a Goth by descent, saint.gr says, and lived in the third century, in the years of the emperor Aurelian, and served at Rome. The emperor did not know that Sabbas was a Christian; but Sabbas for his part did not think he ought to take cover when the Christians were being persecuted and imprisoned, and used to carry food to the prisoners himself and offer them his brotherly love and support. The prison officers denounced him to the emperor, who tried in vain to persuade him to sacrifice to the idols, and then ordered his sides torn and his wounds burned with lighted torches; and his endurance, the page says, showed itself worthy of his faith.

When they saw they could achieve nothing his death was decreed. Because his rank was high, the news that he was to be put to death brought crowds of spectators to the place of execution; and there a cauldron of pitch was boiled, and the officer in charge of the detachment ordered the martyr thrown in. By a wonder he stayed unharmed. The sight stirred great amazement, and seventy of the idolaters standing by believed in Christ and were beheaded on the spot — [the Seventy Soldiers](/saints/seventy-soldiers) whom the Greek calendar keeps on this same day. Sabbas himself, with a great stone tied about his neck, was thrown into the river Tiber, and so took the unfading crown of martyrdom, in the year 272. A second saint stands on this day for his sake and not for his own: [Sabbas of the Kyiv Caves](/saints/sabbas-of-the-kyiv-caves), whose Greek page says plainly that his memory is kept on 24 April «εξαιτίας του Αγίου Μάρτυρα Σάββα του Στρατηλάτου».

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović gives the fullest account of him read for this folder, and names the emperor. In the time of the emperor Aurelian this glorious Sabbas lived at Rome and held the rank of a commander; by descent he was of the Gothic people. He often visited the Christians in the prisons and helped them out of his own property, and for his great purity and fasting authority over unclean spirits was given him by God. When he was denounced as a Christian he stood boldly before the emperor, **threw his soldier's belt down in front of him** and confessed Christ the Lord openly.

He was racked with various torments — scourged, scraped with iron, scorched with candles — and did not succumb to them but appeared alive and whole; and his comrades the soldiers, seeing plainly that God was helping Sabbas, received the faith of Christ. **Seventy of them in number** were beheaded at once by the emperor's order. To Sabbas in the prison Christ the Lord Himself appeared in a great light and strengthened His martyr. Then Sabbas was condemned to death by drowning, and thrown into a deep river, where he gave up his soul to God in 272.

The 272 agrees exactly with the year this folder already held off saint.gr, so no date was added and none changed; the deep river here is the Tiber there, and neither reading was altered on the strength of the other. The emperor Aurelian, the Gothic descent, the visits to the prisons, the belt thrown down and the Lord's appearing in the prison are the entry's own additions to what was read here before. **No `locations`**: Rome and the river are two words in a narrative.

The Russian calendar makes him a Goth and a soldier of one named reign. He came of the Gothic people, it says, and rose by his courage to the high rank of воевода-стратилат in the service of the emperor Aurelian, whose years it gives as 270 to 275. He had been a Christian from his youth and followed the commandments closely, helping the destitute and visiting imprisoned Christians in their gaols; and for the purity of his life he received from the Lord the gift of wonderworking, healing the sick and driving out demons in the name of Christ.

When the emperor learned that he was a Christian he demanded that he renounce the faith. The martyr threw off his soldier’s belt and confirmed that he would not leave it. He was beaten, scorched with candles and thrown into a cauldron of pitch, and remained unhurt; and seeing his torments seventy soldiers believed in Christ and were cut down with the sword on the spot. He was thrown into prison, and at midnight, while he prayed, Christ appeared to him and shone round him with the light of His glory, bidding him not to fear but to be bold. Strengthened, he bore new torments in the morning and was drowned in the river, in the year 272.
