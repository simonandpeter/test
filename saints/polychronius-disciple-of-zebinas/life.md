# Polychronius, disciple of Zebinas

saint.gr keeps four ascetics together on this day and gives them one page, and says at the end whose book it is drawing on: Theodoret of Cyrrhus wrote their lives in his Philotheos Historia. The venerable [Zebinas](/saints/zebinas) set his hermitage on a mountain and lived there as an ascetic in prayer and fasting. Polychronius came to him, and imitated his venerable teacher in unceasing prayer and in self-restraint.

The venerable [Damian](/saints/damian-disciple-of-polychronius) and [Moses](/saints/moses-disciple-of-polychronius) were disciples of Polychronius. The synaxarion relates, the page says, that Moses stayed on in his elder's cell, while Damian went off to the small town of Niara, where near the threshing floors he found a deserted little house and lived in it as a hermit. Our venerable and God-bearing fathers fell asleep in peace.

The page prints two distichs, one for Zebinas alone — the divine Zebinas reaches a divine end, the life he had among men having come to its end — and one for the other three: Polychronius and two fellow ascetics, the three of them together filling out the times of their life. There is no year on the page for any of them, and no hymn.

The Russian calendar keeps the eight Syrian hermits together on this day, and of them all it gives most to Polychronius, the disciple of [Zebinas](/saints/zebinas), who imitated his elder's life and spent days and nights in fasting and vigil. He wore no chains, for fear that pride should be kindled in him by them; instead he had a heavy oak root dug out of the ground, and would lay it on his shoulders at night while he prayed, and hide it away when anyone knocked at his door. By his prayer he asked rain of God in a drought, and he filled a stone vessel with oil for those in need — so full that two or three of the bystanders held out their joined hands and these filled with oil too.

He would take hold of the feet of everyone who came to him and fall down before him, whether the man were a soldier, a craftsman or a peasant; and when the governor of the province was brought to see him and he was told who the visitor was, he held the man's feet and asked a favour of him, and the favour was that the governor should pray to God for him. He kept nothing: men brought him gold in their lifetime and left it to him in their wills, and he would take none of it, but told the givers to be the stewards of their own gift. In his cell, the writer says, he never found anything but two figs.
