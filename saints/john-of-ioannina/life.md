# John of Ioannina

His country was the Greek land called Epirus and his city Ioannina, which the page remembers as the seat of Pyrrhus long before. His parents were Orthodox Christians, and he was left an orphan while still young. He went up to Constantinople to live by the work of his hands, took a place in the market among the craftsmen and the traders, and did well at his trade, for he was prudent, feared God and kept his commandments; the people of the city called him the Epirote, after the country he came from.

The city was then held by the Agarenes, and many Christians had been drawn away to the faith of Mohammed — among them several of the shopkeepers whose stalls stood about his own. He disputed with them often and reproached their apostasy openly, being young and unafraid in speech. They came to hate him for it, and to envy the prosperity of his trade, and looked for a charge that would destroy him. John understood what they were preparing and was not afraid; he desired rather, with his whole soul, to suffer for the truth of Christ.

On Great Friday in the year 1500 he went to the first priest of the Church of Constantinople, his spiritual father, made his confession, told him of the enmity of his neighbours, and said that he wished to suffer for Christ, and asked his counsel. The priest, seeing how young he was, would not advise it: the devil, he said, puts such thoughts into many, urging them toward martyrdom so that when they weaken under the torments and fall he may make them a laughing-stock to the world, as men who began and did not finish.

The young man answered that he hoped in his Christ, who would not give him over to the mockery of his enemies but would strengthen him to overcome. The priest warned him that the spirit is willing and the flesh weak, that such a thing needs long preparation, unceasing fasting and prayer, and that God gives a man to know what he must do once he is cleansed; and he sent him away in peace, promising his prayers. The next day John came back with a bright face, saying he had seen himself in the night in the midst of a flame, unburnt and rejoicing as the three youths in the furnace of Babylon, and the priest blessed him to be crowned.

When he had gone back to his stall, the men who had plotted against him asked one another aloud whether this was not the man who had openly denied his faith at Trikke in Thessaly, and how he came to be a Christian here. John turned on them and said that it should never be that he denied Christ his God, at Trikke or anywhere else, for in Christ he lived and would live always and was ready to die for him. They laid hands on him and dragged him to the judge, being themselves accusers, witnesses and judges in one, and charged him with having taken the faith of Mohammed in Thessaly and become a Christian again in Constantinople.

They began the trial with feigned pity and tears, coaxing him to deny Christ; then with threats; then they beat him without mercy and threw him into prison while they took counsel. Day after day they brought him out, asked him the same question, and beat him with knotted staves until his body was one wound and the ground was red. It was the days of Pascha, and under the blows he sang «Hristos a înviat din morți» and the rest of the feast's hymns, and told them to beat harder, for they would never make him a stranger to his Christ. Between the beatings he was kept in irons, in hunger and thirst.

When no torment would move him they condemned him to be burned. He was led out on an iron chain like a sheep to the slaughter, struck on the neck, reviled, and urged by some in pity to spare his youth; but he went as to a great honour. At the place where a great fire had been lit he did not wait to be thrown in, but leapt into the flame himself, and the executioners caught the end of his chain and dragged him out. He asked them why they would not let him burn and be an offering well-pleasing to his Christ. The judges then chose another sentence: the sword, and the head and body to be thrown into the fire afterwards.

So he received the crown of martyrdom on the eighteenth day of April. When the fire had gone out, the faithful gathered up some of the bones that were left of it and kept them with reverence.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same day as the Romanian calendar and tells the same martyrdom, with a different year and two details of its own. He was of Ioannina, it says, the one-time capital of king Pyrrhus; when his poor parents died the young John moved to Constantinople and carried on his trade there, for he was a craftsman. Not long before, the Turks had taken the city, and many Christians had denied Christ out of fear and accepted the faith of Mohammed; John's shop stood among just such renegades, and the more he burned with love for Christ the more plainly he showed himself a Christian before these men who had betrayed him, disputing with them about the faith and at last reproaching them for the betrayal. So they dragged him before the court and falsely accused him of having taken Islam earlier and returned to Christianity now.

He was beaten with staves and iron rods and thrown into prison. The next day — it was the day of Christ's Resurrection — they led him out to the examination again, and he came out singing joyfully that Christ is risen from the dead, and told his torturers bravely to do what they would, so as to send him the sooner out of this short life into the life eternal: he was Christ's servant, he said, he followed Christ, he was dying for Christ, that he might live with Him. Then he was chained and brought to the burning place, and seeing the great fire made ready for him he ran and leapt into the flame himself; and his torturers, seeing how he loved a death by fire, pulled him out of it and sentenced him to the sword. When they had cut off his head they threw the head and the body into the fire. Afterwards Christians raked the ashes and gathered some remains of his honourable and wonderworking relics, and buried them in the Great Church in Constantinople.

The entry dates the martyrdom to the eighteenth of April 1526, where the Romanian life dates his confession to Great Friday of the year 1500 and the martyrdom to the eighteenth of April of that same Paschal season. The day is the same in both and the year is twenty-six years apart. Nothing is chosen between them: this folder's death now carries the whole of the disagreement, 1500 or 1526, with both readings named in its note.

The Russian church calendar keeps him on the same day as the Romanian and the Prologue, and dates the martyrdom with the Prologue to 1526. Its reading agrees with both in every turn of the story and adds four particulars of its own. It says what he did with what his parents left him: he bought a small shop in the city market and took up a craft, and was industrious and honest in his work, so that his affairs prospered — but his soul reached not for earthly goods but for the kingdom of heaven. It puts the vision on the night before Great Saturday, when he saw himself standing in a fiery furnace and singing the doxology of the Lord, and says that he read it as the command to go to martyrdom, received the Holy Mysteries, and asked his priest’s blessing a second time.

It gives the judge a motive the other two readings do not: he tried to talk the young man over to the faith of Mohammed because he valued him as a skilled and honest craftsman. And it says how the days in prison were spent — several days of hunger, thirst and merciless beating — before the sentence of the fire. The rest is as the folder already had it: he stepped of his own accord into the middle of the flame, was pulled out of it, was cut down with the sword, and had head and body thrown into the fire, and the Christians gathered the bones the fire had spared and bore them with reverence to the cathedral church.

One small disagreement between the Russian page and the Romanian life is worth setting down. The Romanian has his accusers charge him with having denied Christ at Trikke in Thessaly and returned to the faith at Constantinople, and the Prologue says the same; the Russian has them reproach him only for having promised to deny Christ and not kept his word. Neither the place nor the earlier apostasy appears in it at all. Nothing was chosen between the readings and the folder’s `death` interval, 1500 or 1526, stands as it was.
