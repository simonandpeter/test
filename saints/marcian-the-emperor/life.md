# Marcian the Emperor

Doxologia gives Marcian no life of his own: the page it offers for him is the life of the empress [Pulcheria](/saints/pulcheria-the-empress), and he enters it partway through. When the emperor Theodosius was forty-two years old and his end came upon him, he told Pulcheria of a revelation he had had at Ephesus, in the church of Saint John the Theologian: that after his death Marcian the soldier would take the sceptre of the Greek empire. He asked her to help Marcian to the imperial throne.

Marcian was by birth from Turcia, as the page writes it, the son of some soldier, himself a brave soldier, wise in everything, skilful, merciful and good; and from his youth he was marked out for the empire, being kept from chances of death. Going once to Philippopolis he found on the road the cast-out body of a man lately killed, and, pitying him, began to dig a grave. Others coming along the same road took him for the murderer, brought him into the city and gave him to the judge, who condemned him to death, since there was no one to witness for him; and he would soon have been executed if the true murderer had not been shown by a revelation from God.

Set free with honour, he served in the Greek army under the general Aspar. When the Vandals beat the Greeks and took many of them alive, Marcian was among the prisoners brought before Gaiseric, their commander. Gaiseric climbed a high place at noon in a great heat, and saw from far off Marcian asleep on the ground with an eagle flying above him, wings spread, making shade for him against the sun. He understood what it meant, called the prisoner to him, asked his name and his country, and said: if you would live whole and free, swear to me that when you come to the imperial dignity you will never make war on the Vandals, but keep peace with us. Marcian swore it, and went home with honour.

Coming back from captivity to the Greek army, in a lower rank, he fell sick on the road when the Greeks marched against the Persians and stayed in the Lycian city called Sidyma, where two honourable brothers, Tatian and Julian, took him into their house and nursed him for the great love they bore him. When he rose from his bed all three went out together after birds, and, lying down to rest in the noonday heat, fell asleep; Tatian woke first and saw the same great eagle over the sleeping Marcian, shading him with its spread wings. He woke his younger brother, and the two of them prophesied that he would be emperor and asked what he would do for them then. If it falls out as you prophesy, he said, you shall be to me in place of a father.

After the death of Theodosius the younger, Pulcheria took counsel with the nobles and the commanders of the armies and they chose Marcian the soldier for the empire, as a man worthy and chosen of God. Being emperor, he called the two brothers, honoured them with high office and set one over Thrace and the other over Lycia; and he kept his oath to Gaiseric, holding the peace with him unbroken to the end.

Pulcheria wished to go back to her solitary life once Marcian was settled, but the new emperor and the whole council of the palace begged her to stay and help them govern; and that could not be unless she became the emperor's wife. She had promised her virginity to God, she told them, and was bound to keep it until death; and the emperor said that he too had promised his purity to God. For the need of the Church, which the heretics were then troubling, she was constrained to be joined to Marcian — but in a joining that did not wither the flower of her virginity, and she kept it untouched to the end of her life. She was called the emperor's wife and was in truth a sister to him; he was called her husband and was in truth a brother. It was at this holy empress's urging, the page says, that the council of the fathers gathered at Chalcedon against Dioscorus and Eutyches the archimandrite. Doxologia prints no year for Marcian and no troparion.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, on one line with his wife, and tells most of the story through her. saint.gr says he came from Thrace, that he married [Pulcheria](/saints/pulcheria-the-empress), and that on 25 August 450 he succeeded her brother Theodosius the Second on the throne. The political state of things was troubled: the robber synod of Ephesus had already been held the year before, which exiled the patriarch St [Flavian](/saints/flavian-of-constantinople) — whom the Greek calendar keeps on 16 February — and the Orthodox Church was scourged by the heresy of Eutyches. The two devout sovereigns then convened at Chalcedon, in the year 451, the Fourth Ecumenical Council, which condemned the heretical opinions of Eutyches and of Dioscorus. The page's account of Pulcheria beside him has her born 19 January 399, daughter of the emperor Arcadius and Eudocia, proclaimed Augusta in 414, and the one who persuaded her brother to convene the Third Ecumenical Council against Nestorius, taking the part of St [Cyril of Alexandria](/saints/cyril-of-alexandria) while Theodosius, pushed by Chrysaphius, supported the heresiarch. Pulcheria fell asleep in peace on 10 September 453, and Marcian in 457. Its couplet leaves their marriage a puzzle: of Marcian and Pulcheria together, it says, I cannot tell whether to call them unwedded or wedded.

The Greek page heads 17 February for both of them, but this corpus already keeps Pulcheria on the Greek 10 September, which is the day of her falling asleep as this same page gives it; her row is left where it stands and the second Greek day is recorded only here and in the reader's report.
