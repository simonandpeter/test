# Basil (Kishkin)

Basil — Vladimir Timofeevich Kishkin in the world — was born in 1745 in
the village of Klyushnikovo in the Fatezh district of Kursk province, of
a noble family. At seven, pondering the psalmist’s words about purity of
heart and wishing to make them real, he resolved to leave his parents’
house and go to a monastery for the saving of his soul; he went to Sarov
and stayed there in obedience, and after four years asked the abbot’s
leave to go on pilgrimage to Kiev. Living some time at the Caves Lavra he
went often to the holy caves, falling with prayer and tears before the
relics of God’s saints and begging them to bless his purpose and show him
the way, and went round the fathers who laboured there for counsel and
for a place. One elder, foreseeing in him a good athlete of Christ, told
him to go to the Miropolye monastery, “that place will be your
salvation”, and there after three years, for his self-denial and
exemplary life, he was tonsured with the name Basil, and laboured on
until Catherine II suppressed the house with others, when with his
elder’s blessing he moved to the Korennaya hermitage in Kursk province.
There he lived long in attentive ascetic life, practising the work of the
mind and helping the brethren and all who came to his cell door with
spiritual counsel; going often to the Zadonsk monastery he became the
companion and then the disciple of Tikhon of Zadonsk, who taught him to
fight the passions and enlightened his spiritual understanding, and his
chief treasure from the saint was the art of unceasing Jesus prayer.
Burdened by the honour men paid him and by visitors who grew ever more
numerous, he left for Mount Athos and lived with disciples at the skete
of the Prophet Elijah, winning everyone’s love; but the Turkish threat
soon drove him and his disciples to Moldavia, where at Neamț he found the
elder Paisius already dead (1794), and after a stay he went back to
Korennaya, where his virtues drew to him no small number of monks and
laymen seeking their salvation.

At the start of 1800 he moved to the White Shores hermitage, was ordained
hieromonk the next year, and restored the house so quickly that within
three months the brethren numbered sixty; his disciples Seraphim, from
Korennaya, and Leonid, the future elder of Optina, tonsured by him in
1801, came there, and so did the honoured elders Cleopas and the
schemamonk Theodore, disciples of Paisius Velichkovsky, who brought the
Neamț rule of common life and services, which Basil and Leonid
established. There Basil first introduced the daily revealing of thoughts
for all the monks and unquestioning submission to the elder’s will, with
the unceasing prayer as an obligation; many of his disciples became
superiors of other houses and carried Paisius’s teaching further. His
care for the hermitage’s outward good never drew him from the chief
thing, the salvation of the flock God had given him: he had a special gift
for governing, peace and concord among the brethren being his first
concern — for where they are, there is Christ; he assigned obediences with
great skill, so that the well-born often bore the burden of labour more
patiently than those used to it; and he loved bodily work himself. When
the brethren went to the haymaking and left their cells unlocked he would
go round to see who was doing what and who lacked what, leaving a small
comfort, a bagel, a gingerbread, a comb; where a cell was unswept he swept
it himself and taught the monk to keep it clean, and your guardian angel
will honour your work; above all he counselled staying in the cell in the
fear of God, reading the God-bearing fathers, saying the Jesus prayer with
the mind as if threading its words on the heart, and prostrations
according to strength but often and with compunction — lying down to
sleep, think that you lie in your coffin, and wait: behold, the Bridegroom
comes at midnight, and there will be judgement. After the evening rule he
went into the forest, where he had a cell, and there in the quiet spoke
alone with God alone, returning at nightfall. After 1804 he suffered
persecution from one of his own disciples and lived mostly at the Svensk
monastery until 1810 and at the convent of Sevsk, reconciling the
quarrelling and teaching them Christian monastic life; then at Rykhly, at
Kremenets and at Korennaya, and on a missionary journey along the Don,
where with God’s help he converted the leader of the Molokan schismatics;
a second attempt to reach Athos was turned back halfway by the Turkish
war.

From 1811 he lived in various houses, giving priceless help in their
spiritual ordering, and in 1816 settled at the Glinsk hermitage, to which
by his prayers and the brethren’s the Lord gave the abbot
[Philaret (Danilevsky)](/saints/philaret-danilevsky): for ten years he
worked with Philaret in the spiritual direction of the house and the
introduction of the strict Athonite rule, the abbot caring for its outward
prosperity and the elder for its inner order — instructing the brethren,
comforting the faint-hearted, humbling the proud, reconciling the hostile
by his own guilelessness, and above all rooting out drunkenness and urging
the brethren to shun anger and keep in mutual love, so as not to be a
scandal to the world. Yet in those ten years he did not escape the enemy’s
temptations, was driven from the house by others’ envy, and in 1827, on an
elder’s advice, went to the Ploshchansk hermitage. There he overstrained
himself in heavy work and was so ill that the brethren counted him dead;
but after a while he came to and said he would live eighteen months more,
and prepared for his departure in a hermit’s cell, keeping every daily
service but the Liturgy and giving himself unceasingly to the prayer of
the mind, saying in his heart by turns the prayer to Jesus and to the
Mother of God; shortly before his death, by a sign from above, he opened
his door again to those who thirsted for counsel and help. A week before
his end, on the first day of Pascha, 19 April 1831 of the old style, he
received the Holy Mysteries and his face visibly shone; he was ill through
Bright Week, and on the Monday of Thomas week asked his disciple to light
candles before the icons, call the superior and gather the brethren, took
leave of them, and died on 27 April 1831 of the old style, eighty-six
years old, and was buried between the main and the Nicholas altars of the
Kazan cathedral of the hermitage. The Lord had given him many gifts —
clairvoyance above all, the healing of spiritual and bodily ills,
humility, love of enemies, patience and disinterest — and he was the
builder of many monasteries of men and of women, staying in each as long
as it took to raise it.

The old opinion of him as an elder of God-pleasing life was confirmed
early in the twentieth century, when a woman of Brasovo, fifteen
kilometres from Ploshchansk, dreamed of him telling her he could heal the
incurable illness she had suffered for years, and was well after a
panikhida at his grave; another woman was healed after her; the faithful
came to pray at the grave for healing and took sand from it, a booklet on
the blessed elder’s life, clairvoyance and wonders was printed for the
people, the possessed who came declared themselves whole, and the
monastery set a font-shaped tomb over the grave with three windows for
the sand, a canopy against the weather and a wide railing. The monastery
was closed in 1921 and its church made a parish church; the Bee commune
and a children’s colony of eighty settled in it, whose activists demanded
that the elder’s remains be opened, and on 16 November 1924 a large
commission, with some two thousand people from the villages round
looking on, opened the graves of Basil and of the builder Paphnutius,
found three skeletons it could not assign, and took the skulls to Bryansk
for anti-religious propaganda; the abbot Nicodemus (Spiridonov) was tried
in seven volumes for “fraudulent acts at the grave of Basil Kishkin” and
fined a hundred roubles. The true relics, it seems, the commission never
found: in the mid-twenties boys from the children’s home, prising open the
monastic vaults behind the cathedral’s altars for valuables, found a body
that looked asleep, fled in terror, and the adults of the commune covered
it with lime and earth for fear of an epidemic. The hermitage was revived
in 1994, and from 11 April 2002 the brethren dug for three days for the
grave the nineteenth-century books put between the altars of the Kazan
and Nicholas chapels, two metres down — the commission of 1924 had dug in
the wrong place — and on the third day, at the abbot Sergius’s word, found
the relics under the lime, a fragrance rising from the earth all three
days as it had for two weeks some years before, and carried them into the
church the same day, the brethren reading the seventeenth kathisma in turn
as they were raised. The Council of Bishops of 2017 entered him in the
calendar of the Russian Church, which keeps him on 9 September of the old
style, 22 September, with the synaxis of the Glinsk fathers, and in the
synaxes of the saints of Kursk and Bryansk.
