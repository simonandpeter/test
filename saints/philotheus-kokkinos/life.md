# Philotheus Kokkinos

Born about the year 1300 at Thessalonica, he was given the name Fokas at his baptism, and his mother came of a Jewish family. He had an exceptional education from Thomas Magistros, in whose service he was. He embraced monasticism early and withdrew to Mount Sinai, and from there went to the Holy Mountain — «cel cu adevărat de aur şi preaiubit şi cauză a binelui tuturor celor care au locuit şi locuiesc în el», truly golden and beloved and the cause of good to all who have dwelt in it, as his biographer writes.

He lived first at Vatopedi, where he came to know Sabbas the fool for Christ, whose life he afterwards wrote; then he went to the Great Lavra and became a disciple of [Gregory Palamas](/saints/gregory-palamas), whose life he would also write. He signed the Hagiorite Tome in 1339, and in 1342 became abbot of the Great Lavra in succession to Macarius, who was then chosen metropolitan of Thessalonica. His time as abbot was not without troubles, the page says, for he had supported Palamas from the first.

In 1346 he wrote two treatises, on the light of Tabor and against Akindynos. In 1347 Patriarch Isidore made him metropolitan of Heraclea in Thrace, with the title of first among the bishops; in 1350 he ordained the Athonite Kallistos I patriarch; and at the hesychast council held at Constantinople in 1351 he stood with Palamas and himself drew up the Tome of its acts.

He was ecumenical patriarch twice, from 1353 to 1354 and from 1364 to 1376. In 1368 he convoked a council which recognised the sanctity of Gregory Palamas and appointed his commemoration on the second Sunday of Great Lent, and he composed the saint’s service on that occasion; the anti-Palamites were condemned at the same council. He governed with such wisdom, the page says, that the Ecumenical Patriarchate became in his day a beacon of Orthodoxy, and he worked against both the Ottoman threat and Latin propaganda by strengthening and uniting the Orthodox.

His learning made him a remarkable and many-sided writer. His works are dogmatic and polemical, against Gregoras, Akindynos, Barlaam and Manuel Petriotis; many are hagiographical, on the Athonite saints above all — Gregory Palamas, Germanos Maroules, Isidore Boucheiras, Sabbas of Vatopedi, and Nicodemus the New of Tismana — and on Saint Anysia, Saint Demetrius the Myrrh-streamer, the apostles, the Three Hierarchs, Onuphrius, Febronia, Phocas, John Chrysostom, Theodore Tiron and Thomas. He wrote prayers, homilies, liturgical and canonical works, and synaxarion lives.

As a hymnographer he was distinguished for the depth of his matter and the elegance of his style. He left the ecumenical throne in 1376 for age, and fell asleep three years later, in 1379. He was buried with imperial honours in the monastery of Christ Akataleptos at Constantinople, and his tomb became a source of wonders. He was honoured as a saint very soon after, and was painted in the katholikon of Ravanica in Serbia by Thessalonian iconographers; on the Holy Mountain he is usually painted in the narthex among the hymnographers.

Doxologia’s life says his memory is kept on the fifth Sunday of Great Lent and on 11 October, and that the Church honours him as a famed ascetic, a monk and a defender of the right faith, remembered beside Photius the Great, Mark Eugenikos and Gregory Palamas. The page prints no troparion for him.
