# Theodora of Arta

Theodora was born about the year 1210, most probably at Thessalonica, of the great and lordly Byzantine family of the Petraliphai, of Norman descent, which had settled first at Didymoteichon and had been honoured with high offices; her father John held the title of sebastokrator and governed Thessaly and Macedonia. He died early and left her an orphan while she was still small, and the protection of the family was taken up by her uncle Theodore, duke of Epirus. She grew up at Servia of Kozani with her brothers and sisters, under her devout mother Helen.

There Michael II, on his road to Arta, met her and asked for her at once in marriage; the wedding was held at Servia with every magnificence in the year 1230, and they came with a bright company to Arta, the capital of the state of Epirus, where Michael was shortly proclaimed Despot. The young duchess became the first lady of the Despotate, and, her biographer the monk Job says, was not carried away by the glory of her station but understood the more that she must live in virtue and sober-mindedness, in humility and love, in mercy and meekness, and give herself wholly to the service of God and of men.

Their happy time was short. Michael was drawn into unchastity by a noblewoman of Arta named Gangrene, who so enslaved him that he set implacable hatred against his wife in his heart, forbade all help to her and ordered that she should not be spoken of in the palace nor her name even be uttered. Theodora set patience and a humble mind against his high-handedness; slandered and driven out, she left the palace already with child, and for five years, with her firstborn son Nicephorus who was born in exile, she suffered cold and heat, hunger and thirst, abandonment and loneliness, unknown and poorly clothed, crossing hills and ravines to escape her husband's fury.

She found a little comfort with the priest of Prenista, who came upon her one day as she was gathering greens for herself and her small child and at length got her to say who she was; and for a while she was sheltered in his house. Then the nobles of Arta, provoked by the Duke's loose life and by the arrogance of Gangrene, drove the woman out of the palace and required their ruler to change his life. Michael came to himself, sent trusted men to find Theodora and bring her back, and received his lawful lady again with much repentance and honour; and in memory of it, and for a sign of his repentance, he raised the monastery of the Kato Panagia, whose inscription of penitence the page quotes from the north arch.

Four more children were born, John, Demetrius, Helen and Anna. Strengthened by her trial, Theodora became the guide of her husband's soul and took an active part in the government, in his works of peace and in his defeats alike; she stood for the keeping of the Orthodox faith against the Latin pressure for union, married her daughters into the West with that in view, worked for peace among the Greek states and for the recovery of Constantinople, and was called for it the peacemaking saint, «Εἰρηνοποιὸς Ἁγία». After about forty years of married life Michael II died, having lived well and in the love of God.

Theodora ran at once to the monastery. For some ten years she lived as a nun in the monastery of St George, which is now the monastery of St Theodora — her life ascetic and her polity angelic, her biographer says, bearing the weight of pains and ascesis, keeping night and day in unceasing prayer, cleansing her body with fasting and serving the sister nuns readily, the protector of the wronged and the support of widows and orphans. She prayed for them, gave her last commands and, rejoicing, gave her spirit into the hands of God at about seventy. Her body was buried in the narthex of the katholikon of her monastery, where her tomb is to this day.
