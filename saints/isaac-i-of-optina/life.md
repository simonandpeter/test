# Isaac I of Optina

Isaac — Ivan Ivanovich Antimonov in the world — was born at Kursk on 31 May
1810 into a patriarchal merchant family respected by the townspeople for its
unimpeachable honesty and strict Christian way of life, its mercy to the
poor and its adornment of the town church. The future great elder of Optina
grew up in an atmosphere of love, obedience to his parents, firm keeping of
the church’s rule and moral strictness: a modest, kind boy, quiet and
reserved but not gloomy, not without a joke, witty and simple in company.
The thought of the monastery ripened in him over long years while he had to
help his father in trade, and by thirty-six it was fixed — his elder brother
Michael’s entry into monasticism helped — and in 1847, a grown man with his
mind made up, he left his father’s house for the skete of Optina, already
famous for its elders, where every brother was placed under one. The novice
John was given to Macarius, whose name was known far beyond the monastery,
and under him passed his years of obedience: the apiary, then the bakehouse
and the kitchen of the refectory, the choir, and — with the elder’s blessing
— bookbinding, keeping the cell rule of every novice without fail, and after
the rassophore in 1851 and the mantle in 1854 ever stricter with himself in
the inner work, the services, food and sleep. Fleeing every ambitious
thought and so every promotion, he was ordained hierodeacon in 1855 and
hieromonk in 1858 purely out of obedience to Macarius, and stayed as
modest, sincere and open with the brethren as before, and harder on himself.

So his whole life might have passed, from strength to strength; God wanted
otherwise. In 1860 the dying Macarius left him to the guidance of Ambrose,
Macarius’s own disciple, and in 1862, on the death of the superior Moses,
Isaac was made his successor, and led the monastery for more than thirty
years. He finished the building Moses had begun, great for its day: the
church of All Saints at the new cemetery, a new iconostasis in the Kazan
cathedral and a rebuilt one in the Vvedensky, new wall-paintings, a
monastery hospital with a free dispensary and its church of Hilarion the
Great, a bookshop, a two-storey store, the water supply, a new guesthouse,
and a great many buildings restored or built afresh; he bought forest to
settle the question of fuel, and meadow at the Bolkhov mill, opened a
candle factory and encouraged the orchards and gardens, so that in the
second half of the century Optina became one of the flourishing monasteries
of Russia. But the main thing to him was a father’s strict care that the
brethren kept their obediences and the rule, with no exception made for
himself: igumen, and archimandrite from 1885, he did nothing in the
monastery without the elder’s blessing and taught the brethren the same —
fathers and brothers, one must go to the elder to cleanse one’s conscience
— and stood in the queue to his confessor Ambrose with everyone else, and
spoke with him on his knees like a simple novice. His last years brought
many sorrows, above all Ambrose’s departure for the Shamordino community:
twenty-nine years I was superior beside the elder and saw no sorrows, he
said; now it must please the Lord to visit me, a sinner, with them. His
health failed and he took the schema in his cell; soon Ambrose died, and
secret denunciations followed that the superior was too old and ill to
govern, which the brethren rose as one to deny — but his strength was
going. He died quietly among his weeping children, with a last word to
them: love God and your neighbour, love God’s church, in the services and
in prayer seek not earthly goods but heavenly, and end your days here, in
this holy house where you began the monastic life. He died on 22 August /
4 September 1894, a true heir of the tradition of eldership — the strict
obedience of the whole brotherhood to the elder-confessors whatever their
rank — that set Optina apart from the other monasteries of Russia. The
Russian calendar keeps him on 22 August (Julian).
