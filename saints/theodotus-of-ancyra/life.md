# Theodotus of Ancyra

The life of Theodotus that doxologia prints is signed by Saint Nilus, who says he lived with the martyr from the beginning, was with him in the bonds of the prison, and writes only what he saw with his own eyes. Some say, Nilus allows, that Theodotus lived at first no differently from anyone else, that he was lawfully married and kept an inn for profit; let each say what he will, he writes, and the last struggle of his martyrdom adorned the whole of his earlier life.

Before his martyrdom he did much good. Married though he was, he fought against the desires of the flesh and subdued the body to the spirit, fulfilling the apostle's word that those who have wives should be as though they had none, until he could teach others purity and sober wisdom. He had abstinence and fasting as the beginning of all good things and armed himself with them as with a shield; he counted the mortifying of the body a fitting pleasure for a Christian; and he overcame the desire of riches by giving away everything he had to the poor. What was most to be wondered at in him was that, pretending to live as an innkeeper, he was carrying on a great spiritual trade, winning many souls for God and working secretly like an apostle under that appearance.

When the governor Theotecnus came to the borders of Ancyra, the report of his coming alone so terrified the faithful that the churches were emptied at once and the hidden places and the mountains were full of fugitives. In the city were seven virgins, grown old in chastity and the fear of God, who had betrothed themselves in maiden purity to Christ; the eldest of them, the blessed Thecusa, was Theodotus's aunt. The governor put them to many torments and could not force them to the idolatry, and then gave them over to some debauched young men to be defiled. Thecusa took hold of the feet of the boldest, threw back the covering from her head to show him her grey hairs, and asked him to think of his own mother; and the youths were put to shame, wept, and left the virgins untouched.

They were drowned afterwards in the lake, with stones at their necks, for refusing to serve the idols at the washing of them; and Theodotus, who had been praying for them in fear that one might weaken, took counsel with Polychronius and Theocharides how to get their bodies out of the water and give them burial. His aunt Thecusa appeared to him in a vision and reproached his sleep. The three went out at night; the soldiers guarding the lake fled at the sight of an armed man shining like fire, whom the life names as the martyr Sosander, and a wind emptied the water to the far bank so that the bodies were seen. They cut the ropes from their necks with sickles, carried them away in carts and buried them beside the church of the holy patriarchs. Their names, the life says, were Thecusa, Alexandra, Claudia, Phaina, Euphrasia, Matrona and Julia, and they suffered on the eighteenth day of the month of May.

In the morning the city learned that the bodies had been taken. Christians were seized everywhere and examined, and Polychronius — Theodotus's own kinsman and friend, the betrayer the saints had warned him of — saw the sword over his head and told the truth. The pagans dug up the relics of the virgins and burned them, and looked for Theodotus; and when he heard of it that evening he determined to give himself up. He came of his own accord to the judgement and to the torments, and bore them, the soldiers said afterwards, as though he had been of brass or of iron; and he was beheaded in a field outside the city, before a great crowd of men and women.

He had told the brethren that when the priest Fronto came from the village of Malos with his ring as a token they should give him the body if they could take it secretly. Fronto came, sat down with the guards, gave them his own wine to drink, and heard from them the whole story; and while they drank, the body was taken. He went off as though looking for his donkey and did not come back. The donkey, led by an angel, stopped at the very place where Theodotus had once eaten with Fronto and praised it as a fit place to receive martyrs' relics, and would go no further until its master came; and there Fronto, with some faithful brethren, buried the martyr with honour and afterwards built a church in his name.
