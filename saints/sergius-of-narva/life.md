# Sergius (Druzhinin)

The future bishop — Ivan Prokhorovich Druzhinin in the world — was born
on 20 June 1863 in Novoye Selo in the Bezhetsk district of Tver province
to a well-off peasant family, with only a home education, having taught
himself his letters. In 1881 he came to live in St Petersburg, where his
cousins were nuns of the Resurrection Novodevichy convent; in the capital
he worked for a time as a horse-tram driver, and in 1881, at his cousins’
urging, entered the Valaam monastery of the Transfiguration as a novice —
where the obediences were too hard for his weak health, so that on the
superior’s advice he soon passed to the Holy Trinity St Sergius hermitage
near Strelna, known for the labours of Ignatius (Brianchaninov), received
there on 9 September 1887 under its superior Ignatius (Malyshev),
Brianchaninov’s spiritual son. Many of his family had gone into
monasteries, he told his interrogators in 1931, and from twelve he had
frequented the monasteries where his mother’s kin lived; at the hermitage
he was under the elder Gerasim, once a rich landowner, for ten years,
then under the archimandrite Barlaam, the igumen Agathangel and the
superior Michael — a schooling that confirmed him in true Orthodoxy,
monastic life, obedience to the church authority and devotion to the
throne. Made a novice on 4 December 1889, he was tonsured Sergius, for the
hermitage’s patron, on 24 September 1894, ordained hierodeacon on 20
November and hieromonk on 24 April 1898, and was assistant sacristan from
1894 and sacristan for thirteen years from 1902, keeper of all the
monastery’s treasures but its money. Two years after his ordination he
became a spiritual guide: in summer the grand duke Dmitry Konstantinovich
lived at Strelna in the Konstantinovsky palace and his elder brother
Konstantin Konstantinovich with his large family at Pavlovsk, devout men
who often came to the monastery’s services and afterwards to the
superior’s, where the young hieromonk served them tea and monastery bread;
chosen by them, he served in the palace chapels at Strelna in summer and
Pavlovsk in winter, and before Easter 1900, at Pavlovsk, the grand duke
asked him on behalf of all the Konstantinovichi to be their confessor —
refused at first for his want of theological training, and accepted the
same day at the personal pleading of the elders of the family, the queen
of the Hellenes Olga among them. He was their confessor eighteen years,
until the arrest of most of that branch of the Romanovs in the spring of
1918, parted from them only in 1904–1905 as a military chaplain in
Manchuria; the imperial children wrote to him in 1903 sending their pocket
money for a stone floor in the refectory and asking prayers for the sick
Gabriel and Oleg, and he shared the family’s wide charity, carrying the
petitions of Strelna and the pilgrims to the grand duke twice a year and
chairing from 1904 the society of zealots of faith and mercy at the church
of Andrew of Crete, with its orphan school and almshouse. He consecrated
the palace chapel of Alexander Nevsky at Strelna in 1912, blessed the
Shamordino metochion at Strelna in 1916, served in the Marble Palace,
confessed and served for the grand dukes through the war, prepared a son
of Sergei Mikhailovich for confession, and met the emperor more than once
— at Christmas and Easter, when as superior of the hermitage he alone of
the monastics had the right to exchange the Easter kiss with the tsar, and
last at Christmas 1916, when the sovereign talked with him a long while: a
meek, humble, wonderfully modest man, he told the OGPU, more like a plain
little officer than an autocrat, so that he met the abdication with deep
sorrow, grieving for God’s anointed, and never believed the Rasputin
stories, having asked the tsar’s valet of twenty-four years, his spiritual
son, under confession, who swore they were lies. In 1915, on the grand
duke’s recommendation and Metropolitan Vladimir’s petition, and after the
sick superior Michael had asked for him by name, he was made superior of
the hermitage and archimandrite on 24 May — a house of seven churches,
five chapels, nearly ninety monks, a model farm, an invalids’ home, a
school of sixty boys, a hospital with an out-patients’ department of six
thousand a year, a pilgrims’ house and, from 1914, a hospital for the
wounded; by 1916 the brethren were a hundred. The revolution changed
everything: he refused the queen of the Hellenes’ offer to leave with her
for Greece — I wish to stay with my brethren in the hour of trouble, and
not only when I rode in grand-ducal motor-cars — and some of the
Konstantinovichi stayed in Russia by his blessing and received the
martyr’s crown, Igor, Konstantin and Ioann at Alapaevsk in July 1918 and
Dmitry, closest to him, shot in the Peter and Paul fortress in January
1919. In March 1917 twenty-five monks denounced him to Bishop Benjamin as
the creature of the grand duke, of Metropolitan Pitirim and of Rasputin,
in twenty-two points, and his supporters answered that he had strictness,
but a just strictness, only for order; the inquiry bore them out, the
mutineers withdrew their statement, both sides begged that he stay, and
the brethren wrote to the procurator of the hospital, the refectory, the
churches and the vestry he had enriched — though from June 1917 a spiritual
council governed the hermitage with him. He received the October
revolution as the gravest calamity for the country and the irrevocable
ruin of the old Russia; the harassments began, and in early 1919 the
brethren, in the new disorder, drove out their strict superior, who
begged Metropolitan Benjamin for shelter in the Alexander Nevsky Lavra,
having nowhere to lay his head. From 1920 he was rector of the parish
church of Andrew of Crete at Volodarskaya station, where the local
people, who knew him from the charitable society, helped him settle and
where he made an atmosphere of warm and unselfish fellowship in the years
of civil war, famine and ruin — the grand dukes’ confessor a close friend
of the plain people of the settlement, who brought him the cabbage he
loved; the archpriest Michael Cheltsov found him in 1920 modest, quiet,
wronged and cowed, and pitied him. Briefly arrested in the summer of 1922,
he held with Patriarch Tikhon on the seizure of church valuables — an act
of gross violence and arbitrariness — and rejected the Renovationist
administration outright, his parish a founder of the Petrograd autocephaly
that fought the Renovationists, unlike the hermitage’s brethren who fell
into the schism; close to Bishop Nicholas (Yarushevich), he supported him
in exile with letters and parcels. In 1924, for his thirtieth year of
priesthood, his venerators gathered thousands of signatures for his
consecration, and Fr Michael Cheltsov, grateful for his registration at
his church after prison, petitioned the Patriarch; the Petrograd bishops’
council objected — Krasnoye Selo had asked for no bishop, village
parishes had never had one, and “the parishes that recognise him” was a
dangerous formula — and even threatened to withdraw from the diocese’s
guidance, but Tikhon, changing the title, judged him worthy, summoned him
to Moscow and on 10/23 November 1924 consecrated him bishop of Narva —
Narva itself having been ceded to Estonia in 1921 — so that he went on
serving and living at Volodarskaya, politely ignored by the Leningrad
episcopate as an ill-educated simpleton and not even invited to the Lavra
on Alexander Nevsky’s day. After the arrest of three of the four bishops of
the council in December 1925, Gregory of Shlisselburg invited him to serve
wherever he wished, and in 1926–1927 he served most often at the Izmailovo
Trinity cathedral, the former synodal metochion, the Protection church on
Borovaya and above all the Resurrection cathedral, growing close to its
rector Basil Veryuzhsky; in August 1927 Bishop Nicholas (Yarushevich),
wanting the see for another, had him retired, which wounded and angered
him, and the Synod began to call him bishop of Koporye. Of Metropolitan
Sergius’s declaration he learned from his spiritual son the archpriest
Sergius Tikhomirov, shot in 1930, who held that no communion could be had
with a traitor and Judas; he signed the letter of six bishops carried to
the metropolitan by the Leningrad delegation, still concelebrated with
Nicholas on 6 December 1927 as the only one of the six to do so, but soon
joined the nascent Josephite movement — the Soviet power is a godless
power, and to support it is to become godless oneself; the deputy locum
tenens had led the Church down a false road to perdition, and after
counsel with Bishop Demetrius (Lyubimov) and the archpriests John Nikitin,
Basil Veryuzhsky and Theodore Andreev he went over consciously to that
group of clergy to stand with them in defence of true Orthodoxy and, if
need be, to die. On 13/26 December 1927 he and Demetrius signed the act of
separation and handed it to Nicholas; the reprisals, now known from the
OGPU’s own papers to have been inspired by Tuchkov, followed on 30
December with a ban on serving, under which, frightened, he told Nicholas
he dissociated himself from the schismatics — and after Metropolitan
Joseph’s vivid letter of 7 January approving the separation he went back
to his fellows, was stripped of the title of Koporye on 25 January and
given to the canonical court of the bishops, ignored the decrees, called
himself bishop of Narva again and gradually became one of the leaders of
the Josephite movement, serving on Sundays and feasts with Demetrius at the
Resurrection cathedral and on other days at Andrew of Crete, which the
Josephite archpriest Ishmael Rozhdestvensky helped him keep in Orthodoxy
when its rector tried to return it to the metropolitan, and where fourteen
hundred of the settlement’s nineteen thousand were still registered
parishioners at its closing in 1931. On 12 October 1928 he and Demetrius
secretly consecrated Maximus (Zhizhilenko); after Demetrius’s arrest in
November 1929 he governed the Josephite diocese of Leningrad about a year
at Metropolitan Joseph’s direction and was in practice the leader of the
true Orthodox across the country, ordaining priests who came from all over
the Soviet Union, refusing at interrogation to name any, working above all
with the village clergy since collectivisation, church closures and
crushing taxes had turned the peasants against the Soviet power — sending
Theodore (Kozlov) to Nadbino, Barsanuphius (Kuzmin) to Utorgosh, Nikon
(Katansky) to Krasnoye Selo, ordaining for Uglich, Serpukhov and Sarov,
blessing Alexander Pospelov’s move to “Nicholas the Great Cross” in
Moscow, and welcoming the trust of Bishop Paul (Kratirov) of Kharkov, since
not all the Josephites, in the provinces or the capital, accepted him:
the radical Victorinus Dobronravov refused to be his helper, some
suspected, quite groundlessly, that he had sold himself to the GPU because
he alone had not been arrested, and in April 1930 an open breach came when
he held that the parish councils must accept the registration the new law
of 1929 demanded and sharply criticised those who called it criminal and
sinful — whereupon a whole group of true Orthodox priests, Dobronravov,
Voznesensky, Ushakov, Vasilyev and others, ceased to recognise him and
went underground. They went to Metropolitan Joseph in his exile at the
Nikolo-Modensky monastery, who backed the bishop entirely on registration
but, hearing complaints of his hot-headed character, sent him ten
commandments limiting his rights to serving and prayer — be equally
indulgent, kind and respectful to all — while forbidding the others all
persecution of him and declaring in a leaflet of 26 July 1930 that his
worthy brothers Sergius of Narva and Basil of Kargopol had not departed one
jot from the purity of Orthodoxy: I am with them and they with me, and
those who are not with them are not with me. After that he referred every
important question to Joseph until the metropolitan’s arrest on 12
September 1930, went on ordaining and tonsuring, and in March 1931
described a conference with the archimandrite Nikon at which, for the
preservation of true Orthodoxy after the arrests, candidates were named
for secret episcopal consecration — whether he and Basil (Doktorov)
performed them cannot be said with certainty. Those who remembered him in
the capital spoke of a wise, kind man who could turn sharp — stout,
rosy-cheeked, good-natured, with a soft smile, a guest of the protodeacon
Michael Yakovlev’s family, who gave the boys who served him as staff-bearer
and lamp-bearer his photograph with a tender inscription; a supporter of
legal forms of church life, he stayed at heart a convinced monarchist,
kept in touch with his spiritual children in emigration — a greeting
reached him from the grand duke Gabriel in 1928 through a singer of the
cathedral travelling abroad — and gave believers leaflets on the coming of
Antichrist, for these were, he said, the times of persecution and of
Antichrist. In 1931 the OGPU fabricated in Leningrad its second mass case
of the “counter-revolutionary monarchist church organisation of the true
Orthodox”, arresting eighty-nine from September 1930 to April 1931, he and
Basil (Doktorov) on 7 December 1930; the indictment of 30 May 1931 named
seventy-six, fifty of them clergy, and on 8 October 1931 the OGPU collegium
gave him five years in its own places of confinement. Questioned six times
in February and March, he bore himself bravely and hid nothing: the Soviet
power sought to destroy the Church and mocked its holy things, and the
Church could not be an indifferent spectator but must grieve and fight for
its existence; true Orthodoxy could exist only under a monarch, who alone
could restore peace, love and order in ruined Russia; twenty years the
grand dukes’ confessor, he was wholly devoted to them, held the sovereign
God’s anointed, hated the Bolsheviks as monsters of the human race for the
murder of the tsar and his heirs, for the shooting of clergy and the
faithful, the destruction of the Church and the thousands of the
fatherland’s sons destroyed, for which they would answer and the Orthodox
people would not forgive them; the persecutors of Christ’s faith had
gathered to the power from all the world, the people groaned under it, its
collectivisation would not save it, and he and his fellows held that true
Orthodoxy through the Church would lead the country the Bolsheviks were
ruining to victory over the enemies and persecutors of the faith. We know
how to respect enemies, an investigator told a fellow-prisoner at night —
Bishop Sergius Druzhinin says to our faces, I do not believe you; you will
not build a happy life on earth without God; such enemies we respect. He
served his term in the Yaroslavl political isolator from December 1931,
nearly four years but for three months in the Butyrka hospital in 1935,
and tradition has Archbishop Demetrius (Lyubimov) dying there in his arms
on 17 May 1935; not freed at the term’s end, he was sentenced on 7 October
1935 by the special board to three years’ exile in the Mari region and
sent on 5 December to Yoshkar-Ola, where he lived his last two years in the
house of Anna Komelina, once a sister of the closed Tsarevokokshaisk
convent, at 94 Volkov street, arrested and sentenced with him later. He
served in secret, honoured by the local people as a holy elder, his house
a magnet for those seeking spiritual fellowship from the town and the
districts of the Mari region and the Josephites of Vyatka, and from early
1936 for his spiritual children from Leningrad; clergy who had left
Metropolitan Sergius visited, and in February 1937 the Mari bishop Barlaam
(Kozulya) came twice to beg him to reconcile with the metropolitan — he
promised to write to the professors and do as they advised, and seems not
to have done so — while the Josephite bishop Hilarion (Belsky), exiled at
Kozmodemyansk, came in the summer of 1937 to beg him not to join the
Sergians and Renovationists who had sold themselves to the Bolsheviks, and
was shot at Yoshkar-Ola on 31 August. His health broken by prison, the old
bishop was surrounded by believers as by a family caring for a father and
comforted by his gifts, who brought food and clothes; a former parishioner
sent an antimension and vestments — keep them somehow, that they do not
fall into the enemies’ hands — on which he perhaps served the Liturgy at
home, since there was no Josephite church in the town; the warden of the
Fyodorovsky cathedral of Detskoye Selo wrote from Archangel exile, and in
March 1936 the pensioner Ekaterina Kiseleva came from Leningrad to nurse
him, having found her spiritual father’s address through the Red Cross. The
clouds gathered: on 14 February 1937 the local NKVD arrested the peasant
Igor Stenkin, who confessed to a group of twenty-one true Orthodox
Christians meeting in his house and Praskovya Kirpichnikova’s at Vazhnanger
since the arrest of their priest in 1932, because the church had gone
over to the Sergian orientation, which they held to have left Orthodoxy
and sold itself to the Soviet power — a group in correspondence with
Archbishop Seraphim (Samoilovich) and pastored by the remarkable confessor
Chariton Poido, a novice of Svyatogorsk and the Trinity-Sergius Lavra
before the war, four years a German prisoner, a priest imprisoned in 1920
for witnessing to the renewal of icons, exiled to the Mari region in 1927
and sentenced again in 1930 and 1932, serving in hiding from 1936, who at
his interrogation on 2 September 1937 declared that he did not recognise
the Soviet power, the power of Antichrist, and fought it by defending the
Church and Christ’s teaching, and taught the peasants not to join the
collective farms or obey a power whose laws were godless. Stenkin told the
investigator he paid no tax and no deliveries, signed no Soviet document,
sent his children to no Soviet school, used no Soviet medicine or goods,
would not fight for a power created not by God but by Satan, prayed for
the tsar and awaited a power that favoured religion — and said that Bishop
Sergius shared these convictions, praising the tsar Nicholas at every
meeting and counting him among the saints; portraits of Nicholas II cut
from calendars were the “material evidence” of monarchist restoration. The
bishop’s monarchism drew him to the true Orthodox Christians, though he
never called for disobedience to all Soviet law nor denied the grace of the
Sergian clergy’s sacraments; but the Mari NKVD found it profitable to
fabricate an underground counter-revolutionary group headed by a bishop
known across the country. Five days after Poido’s arrest on 26 August the
charge and detention were drawn up against him as an exile who had made
himself the counter-revolutionary monarchist-church element of the town
and united under his leadership all the counter-revolutionary groups of the
True Orthodox Church in the republic, the warrant issued on 5 September,
and he was seized at his flat on 7 September 1937 after the interrogation
of all the arrested true Orthodox and of the nuns who lived as a community
with their former abbess Magdalena (Bolshakova) and answered as briefly and
negatively as they could — did you use religion to fight the Soviet power?
no, I do not admit it, said the aged Anastasia (Zadvorova). The Sergian
priest John Demidov supplied the link, testifying that from the first the
exile had surrounded himself with the most reactionary clergy and monastics
and carried on active counter-revolutionary organisational work uniting the
groups of the True Orthodox Church. He was questioned once only, on the day
of his arrest, and three times asked whether he admitted guilt of
counter-revolutionary activity answered each time with a categorical no,
naming no one and giving the inquiry not one fact to seize on; understanding
that no confession could be had, they did not call him again. The
indictment of the same day called him an irreconcilable enemy of the
Soviet power who throughout its existence had fought for its overthrow and
the restoration of the monarchy, organiser of the counter-revolutionary
monarchist organisation “True Orthodox Church” with a church-administrative
centre in Leningrad under the Moscow centre, former of cells of the
pogromist Black Hundred element, who at Yoshkar-Ola had resumed his work
among clergy and nuns spreading defeatist agitation and rumours of war and
the fall of the Soviet power and inciting collective-farm women to leave
the farms, and had corresponded with members of the organisation outside
the republic and held an illegal counter-revolutionary conference. Nineteen
were charged and eighteen sentenced — the bishop, Fr Chariton, fifteen
former sisters of the convent aged fifty-six to seventy-two, mostly of Mari
peasant families, and the laywoman Maria Bulygina who had sheltered the
priest; church books and portraits of the imperial family were the
evidence, and every sister denied guilt bravely. On 11 September 1937 the
troika of the Mari NKVD sentenced the bishop, the priest and the nuns
Antonina (Shakhmatova), Anastasia (Zadvorova) and Eudocia (Starodubtseva)
to be shot and the rest to ten years, of whom only two nuns survived the
camps. Bishop Sergius was shot at seven in the evening of 17 September 1937
— 4 September of the old style, the day the Russian calendar keeps him —
in the cellar of the Yoshkar-Ola prison; his grave and those shot with him
are unknown, presumably in one of the town cemeteries. His file keeps a
photograph with a touching inscription — I give you the image of my face,
and in an hour of golden leisure look on it and pray for me; the ailing
Bishop Sergius — and he was rehabilitated only in 1989. His memory lived in
the hearts of his fellow-workers and spiritual children, among the people
of Volodarsky and in the Mari villages, where even those who knew nothing
of his confessor’s road could not forget his kindness, simplicity and
warmth; the Russian Church Abroad canonised him in October 1981, and on 11
March 2020 the Holy Synod of the Russian Church entered his name in the
synaxis of the new martyrs and confessors of the Russian Church. The Russian
calendar keeps him also in the synaxis of the saints of St Petersburg.
