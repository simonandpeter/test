# Eusebius of Samosata

Eusebius was bishop in the city of Samosata, under the patriarchate of Antioch. He was a man of right belief and full of zeal towards God, and he showed himself steady, brave and unmoved in the orthodox confession at the time when the heresy of Arius had grown strong and harmed many, and the Church of Christ was tossed by it like a ship in a storm at sea. For after the death of Constantine the Great the East fell to his son Constantius, who was deceived by the Arian heresy and helped and fought for the Arians, persecuting the orthodox hard.

His zeal first became known in this way. When Eudoxius, patriarch of Antioch and of one mind with the Arians, went over to the see of Constantinople for its wealth, a synod of all the bishops of Syria met at Antioch to choose another in his place. The orthodox bishops were then few and the Arians many; and Eusebius, who sat among the first at that synod, knew Meletius, bishop of Sebaste in Armenia, to be orthodox and to hold wholly to what had been settled at Nicaea, and counselled them all to choose him.

Those of the Arian mind, not knowing Meletius's true faith and supposing him one of themselves, took the advice readily, drew up the act of the common election, confirmed it with their own signatures, and gave it into Eusebius's keeping. Meletius was brought to Antioch with imperial consent and with great honour, the whole people coming out to meet him; and once enthroned he taught the people first the good life and good manners, clearing the crooked way out of their hearts before sowing in them the seed of the right faith.

When the people pressed him to say plainly what he believed, he preached the faith confirmed at the first universal council of Nicaea and confessed the Son of God to be of one essence and one nature with the Father, uncreated and the Maker of all. The Arians were greatly troubled; the orthodox rejoiced to see such a hierarch in the apostolic chair. So thirty days after he had received the patriarchate the Arians drove Meletius out, slandered him everywhere as a heretic, and moved the emperor to condemn him to exile. He was taken out of Antioch by night, and an Arian, Euzoius, was put in his place.

Seeing the Church of Antioch troubled by heretics and so undeserved a wrong done to Meletius, Eusebius grieved greatly, rose up, and went away to his own city without telling anyone. The Arians, remembering that the act of Meletius's election, agreed in common and signed by all their hands, was in his keeping, and fearing to be judged one day for having chosen a man with one voice and at once driven him out, asked the emperor to send after Eusebius for it.

The life that follows tells at length of the sufferings that came upon him from the Arians, and of the reign of Valens, under which he was exiled. When Valens had perished badly the orthodox emperor Gratian succeeded, and at once released from banishment all the hierarchs and other consecrated persons who were exiled for the right faith and told them to take their sees again. So Meletius returned to Antioch, Pelagius to Laodicea and Eusebius to Samosata, and there was great joy among the orthodox.

Having taken his see again, Eusebius busied himself with the other hierarchs over the good order of the Church; and seeing that certain chairs stood empty after the Arians were put out, he took pains, with Meletius's counsel and consent, to choose men worthy and firm in the right faith, and consecrating them set them in those sees — Acacius at Beroea, Theodotus at Hierapolis, another Eusebius of his own name at Chalcis, Isidore at Cyrus, and at Edessa, after the removal of the holy Barses, Eulogius the Confessor.

The last of the bishops made by his hands was Marinus, for the city called Doliche, which was full of the faith of Arius; and Eusebius went there with him himself, meaning to cleanse the church of heresy before setting the orthodox bishop on his throne. As he entered the city an Arian woman threw a brick down from a roof, struck him on the head and wounded him badly; and from that wound he sickened and passed to the Lord.

As he lay dying he put all who were with him on oath to take no revenge at all upon that woman, following his Master, who prayed for those who crucified Him, saying: Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do. So he ended as a martyr, suffering at the hands of the Arians for the Son of God, who is for ever of one essence and one nature with the Father. His body was carried back to Samosata and buried with honour and with much grief and lamentation from the Christian people, and in his place was chosen Antiochus, his sister's son, under whom the Church of Samosata flourished in the right faith. The life names Constantius, Valens and Gratian and prints no year, so none is recorded here.
