# Isidora of Tabennisi

Isidora struggled in the monastery of Tabenna, of the Tabennesiotes, in Egypt, in the fourth century. She took up the heavy cross of folly for Christ, pretending to be mad and possessed, and the other sisters despised her so far that they would not eat with her. So she stayed in the kitchen, did all the work there and was the monastery's dishcloth, fulfilling in deed the word that is written: if any man among you seems to be wise in this world, let him become a fool, that he may be wise.

She wore a rag on her head while all the rest had the proper cap, and kept her rule so. Not one of the four hundred sisters in that monastery saw her eat in all the years of her life; she did not sit at table and took no piece of bread, but was content to wipe the crumbs off the tables and to wash the filth from them. She never reviled anyone, never murmured, and said neither little nor much, whether she was struck or mocked or insulted or abused.

An angel appeared to Pitirim, the tried hermit of Mount Porphyrite — the life gives his name also as Pitirum or Pitirion and says he is commemorated on 29 November — and asked him why he thought himself so devout, sitting there; if he wished to see a woman more devout than himself, he should go to the monastery of the Tabennesiote women and find the one wearing a diadem on her head, for she had never parted her heart from God among all that crowd, while he sat there and wandered through cities in his mind. He who had never gone out went, and the teachers dared to let him in, since he was famous and old.

He asked to see them all; she would not come, and when she was told that Pitirim wished to see her she came in, and the old man saw the rag on her forehead, fell at her feet and asked her blessing. The sisters cried out that she was mad and that he should not bear the insult, and he told them they were the mad ones and that she was his mother and theirs. They fell at her feet and confessed what each had done to her — one had thrown water on her back, one had struck her with her fists, one had blown her nose on her. He prayed for them and left; and a few days later Isidora, unable to bear the honour and burdened by the praise, went out of the monastery, and nobody knew where she went or how she ended.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, and saint.gr tells the disclosure a little differently. It is out of the Gerontika, the page says: the venerable Pityrion, whose memory is kept on 29 November, was informed by the Lord of Isidora's virtue, and when he came to her convent he asked that all the nuns be gathered. They came, and he saw no halo on any of them, though God had assured him that Isidora would have one. So he asked whether there was any other nun in the house, and was told that there was a mad one; and when the mad one was brought in, he saw the halo upon her head, and it was disclosed that Isidora had been feigning madness for the love of Christ.

Where the Romanian life has her slip away after that, unknown to anyone, saint.gr ends otherwise: having struggled in a way pleasing to God in a monastery of Egypt, she fell asleep in peace in the year 365. Its kontakion puts the whole of her in one line — longing to attain the state above, she chose humility for the short road, and by it was brought to Christ as a fruit glorious and delightful.

The Ohrid Prologue, read for the Julian 10 May, keeps her on the Slavonic day rather than the first of the month, and tells the story at more length than either calendar this folder was built from. She lived in the fourth century, it says, a nun in the women's monastery at Tabennisi, and she made herself out to be mad so as to hide her virtue and her struggle. She did the filthiest work of the house; she fed on the slops from the dishes; she waited on everyone and anyone; and she was despised by everyone and anyone.

In that time an angel of God disclosed the secret of her to a great ascetic named Pitirim. Pitirim came to the women's monastery, and when he saw her he bowed to the ground before her, and she bowed so to him. Then the sisters told him that she was mad. "You are the mad ones," Pitirim answered them, "and this woman is greater before the Lord than I am and than you are; I only pray God to give me what is appointed for her at the Dread Judgement." At that the sisters were ashamed, and begged forgiveness of Pitirim and of her both; and from that time everyone began to show her honour.

And it is the honour she fled. To escape it she ran away out of the monastery, this book says, and died nobody knows where, about the year 365. That one sentence holds both halves of what the other two books gave separately — the flight and the unknown end are the Romanian life's, and the year is the Greek page's, which has her instead fall asleep in peace in a monastery of Egypt. The Prologue gives no place for her death at all, and names no other saint than her visitor.
