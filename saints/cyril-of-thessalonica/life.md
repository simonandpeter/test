# Cyril of Thessalonica

saint.gr says the only source that preserves the martyrdom and the service of the venerable martyr Cyril is the Athonite manuscript 347 of the monastery of Dionysiou, a text in a learned language and most probably the autograph of the anonymous compiler of the martyrdom, which it dates between the end of the seventeenth and the beginning of the eighteenth century. According to that martyrdom Cyril, whose name in the world was Kyriakos, was born at Thessalonica in 1544, in the reign of the sultan Suleiman; his father Peios came from the province of Pelagonia and lived with his family in the quarter of the city's acropolis.

At ten years old Kyriakos was left an orphan, and two uncles on his mother's side took him into their care, one of whom was a Mohammedan. In the end the Mohammedan uncle took him over entirely and sent him to a tanner, also a Mohammedan, to learn that trade. But Kyriakos, following the advice of his other uncle, who was a devout Christian, decided to leave his guardian in secret and to follow some monks of the Holy Mountain who happened to be there. Because he was small and could not be lodged in the monastery, he lived as an ascetic for eight years at a dependency of Chilandar, and at twenty-two travelled with two Chilandar monks to Thessalonica, where he met his Christian uncle.

Coming down from the acropolis towards the harbour with his cousin, he met his Mohammedan guardian by chance, who after all those years recognised him and at once called other Muslims to seize him on the charge that he had once embraced Islam and afterwards denied it. He was taken before the Turkish judge of Thessalonica, who tried to talk him out of the Christian faith and, failing, put him in prison overnight; the next day he tried again with promises and with threats, and when that too was in vain he sentenced him to death by fire.

The furious crowd and the executioners led him to the place of his sentence, the old Byzantine Hippodrome of the city, by the church of Saints Constantine and Helen, which the synaxarion of his service is the first text to mention. The Turkish prefect tried one last time, as the custom was, to turn him with offers of comfort and pleasure, and Cyril refused him, saying that for him the only wealth was Christ; and so the prefect ordered the executioners to throw him on the pyre, which burned his body to ashes on 6 July 1566. The synaxarion adds that dogs' bones and the bones of dead beasts were thrown into the fire with him, so that his body, consumed, became as ash — a testimony the page says was confirmed in 1972 by a clay casket of bone and cloth mixed with ashes, found in the foundations of the old church of Saints Constantine and Helen at Hippodrome Square when it was pulled down.
