# Nikon of Sicily

Nikon was born at Neapolis in Campania. He was a soldier, comely, cheerful of face and brave in war, but a pagan in faith, born of a pagan father and taught the worship of idols. His mother was a Christian and always urged him towards the knowledge of Christ. Telling him of the power of the Cross of Christ, she would say: my beloved son, if ever you fall into danger, as often happens in war, sign yourself with the sign of the Cross and you will escape your enemies' hands, and neither arrow nor sword will wound you.

It happened that the Roman army went out from Rome against the Greeks, and Nikon found himself in the midst of the enemy in great straits and in danger of death, seeing many of his companions killed. He remembered his mother's counsel, lifted his eyes to heaven and armed himself with the sign of the Cross, promising that from that hour he would be Christ's servant, he and the mother who bore him. Then he took courage, and with his spear killed about a hundred and eighty of the opposing ranks and drove off the rest, and both armies marvelled that they had never seen such a soldier.

He went home and told his mother what the Lord had done for him through the power of the Cross, and she gave thanks and prayed that her son might be counted worthy of baptism. He asked her how a man becomes a full Christian, and she answered: fast forty days, be taught the Christian faith by a Christian priest, renounce Satan and all his works, believe in Christ and receive holy baptism. Nikon said it was better for him to be the Lord's servant than an idolater and a soldier, and that he would worship nothing but the one God, the maker of heaven and earth and sea.

He bowed to the ground before his mother and asked her prayers. She made him swear to come back and bury her, being old, when he had received baptism; then she gave him gold enough for the road and her blessing, and let him go to look for a Christian priest — for in that hard time of persecution the Christian teachers were all hidden in the deserts and the mountains, and it was difficult to find one. Nikon went down to the harbour, found a ship and sailed for Constantinople, and after he had gone there was much searching for him at Neapolis among the soldiers, because of the bravery he had shown in the war.

He came to the island of Chios, climbed a high mountain and spent eight days in prayer that God would show him where he might find a servant of the Lord to give him the baptism he desired and teach him the mysteries of the faith. The next day a ship was waiting for him, its sailors told by an angel of the Lord to wait for the man who would come down from the mountain with the staff that bore a cross. In two days he reached a mountain called Ganos, where Theodosius, Bishop of Cyzicus, was hiding from the pagans with a crowd of monks, and was among them as their abbot.

It was revealed to Theodosius from God who Nikon was; he went out with his monks to the harbour, took him into his cave, taught him, baptised him in the name of the Holy Trinity and gave him the holy Mysteries. Nikon remained in the cave monastery learning the divine books and attending to the monastic life, and was clothed in the monastic habit for his gentleness. He was patient in labour, burning in love, unmatched in fasting, unwearied in the night prayers, and the whole brotherhood and the bishop wondered at him.

After three years an angel stood before Theodosius in a dream and told him that before he departed this life he should make Nikon bishop in his place and entrust his flock to him, and should order him to move with all the brethren southwards into Sicily, so that the monks should not perish there by the swords of the barbarians who would soon overrun the place. Theodosius made him first deacon and then priest, ordained him bishop, entrusted him with the monks, a hundred and ninety in number, and fell asleep in the Lord.

Nikon buried him with honour, took all the monks aboard ship and sailed to Lesbos, where he stayed two days near the town of Mytilene, and then to Naxos; and from there in twenty-two days he reached Italy and came to his own Neapolis, where he found his mother still among the living. Nine of his old comrades in arms fell at his feet and begged him to take them with him, and he did. They all sailed to the parts of Sicily and came to a high mountain of Tauromenium, where they made gardens and orchards and stayed; there the bishop baptised the nine who had been his fellow soldiers and tonsured them as monks.

After enough years had passed, and the persecution of Christians not ceasing, word was brought to the governor of Sicily, Quintianus, that there were men living by the river Asinos who honoured the God of heaven, had Bishop Nikon for their teacher, and would neither obey the imperial laws nor honour the gods. He sent a company of soldiers to take them all, and they were hurried to judgement. When the governor understood that their faith was unchanged and firm, he said that unless he had them killed quickly they would lead many more astray, and the disciples were beheaded, a hundred and ninety-nine of them.

Nikon he kept in chains while he thought of a worse death. That night an angel appeared to him in the prison and told him to take courage, for Christ had received the sacrifice of his hundred and ninety-nine disciples as a sweet savour and they had entered the bridal chamber; and he saw a maiden bearing light who said that Quintianus had killed the servants of Christ and was plotting crueller torments for their teacher, and that armed men had been sent from the heavenly King against him. Nikon told his vision to his servant and foretold the terrible death that would shortly come upon Quintianus.

The next day the governor set him before the judgement seat and asked who he was and of what faith. He was let down from the torture, tied to wild horses to be dragged apart, and thrown bound with iron fetters from a high mountain into a deep valley; and he stood again before the tormentor, alive and whole, so that the governor was afraid. At last he ordered the saint's face to be broken with stones, his tongue drawn out with pincers and cut off, and the martyr taken to the place called Gigia, where he had lived with his disciples, and beheaded.

On the same day he passed sentence, Quintianus went to Panormus to seize the goods of the holy martyr Agatha, whom he had tortured a little before that time. Two men set upon him; one bit and tore his cheek with his teeth, the other trampled and struck him until he threw him into the river, and the wretch drowned, ending his evil life badly, as the saint had foretold. The martyr's body lay unburied at the place of execution until a shepherd troubled by an unclean spirit came upon it, was thrown to the ground and freed; and when he had told the miracle, the bishop of Messina came with his clergy, led by that shepherd. They found the body of the martyr, and the bodies of the hundred and ninety-nine, his disciples, whole in the bath-house and untouched by the fire, and buried them all together with their teacher, in a marked place, with honour.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same menologion day and gives the life doxologia's one line did not. He was born at Neapolis of a heathen father and a Christian mother, and was an officer of the Romans there, unbaptised, though his mother taught him the faith of Christ in secret from his father. Once, when he was setting out with his company to battle, she told him that if he came to extremity he should cross himself and call Christ to his help. And in the battle, when his company was surrounded and very near destruction, he crossed himself and cried out to Christ in his heart; and in that hour he was filled with an uncommon strength, rushed on his enemies, killed some and put the rest to flight. Going home he kept exclaiming in wonder that the God of the Christians was great.

Having gladdened his mother with the news of his victory by the cross of Christ, he sailed secretly to Asia, where Theodosius, bishop of Cyzicus, baptised him. After his baptism he shut himself in a monastery and gave himself to learning and to ascetic labour. But before his death the bishop had a vision in which he was commanded to ordain Nikon his successor; so the old man called him at once and ordained him deacon, then presbyter, then bishop. By God's providence Nikon soon came to Neapolis and found his mother still alive. After her death he withdrew with nine disciples, once his comrades in war, into Sicily, and gave himself there to preaching the Gospel.

But there was a terrible persecution of the Christians at that time, and the prince Quintian took Nikon with his companions and put them to great torments. A hundred and ninety of his disciples and companions were beheaded — the Prologue says nine went with him and a hundred and ninety died, and does not reconcile the two numbers, nor does this folder. Nikon himself the torturer tied to horses' tails, threw from a high rock into a ravine, beat and scraped, and he survived all of it; at the last he was beheaded with the sword. His body was left in a field for the birds to eat, but a shepherd boy with a raging spirit caught on it and fell upon the dead body of Christ's martyr and was healed at once; he told of the body, and the Christians came and buried it honourably. He suffered, the entry closes, in the time of the emperor Decius — the only date any page read for this folder gives.

The Russian calendar keeps him on the same menologion day and gives the fullest account the corpus has of how he came to the faith. He was born at Naples of a pagan father and a Christian mother and stayed a pagan himself, a soldier of uncommon courage and strength. Surrounded once with his company and in danger of death, he remembered his mother’s Christian teaching, signed himself with the cross and promised God that if he came out of it he would be baptised; he came out of it, and went home, and with her blessing set off to look for a priest, which in a time of persecution was not easy.

He reached the island of Chios by ship, climbed a high mountain there and spent eight days in fasting and prayer asking God to help him, and an angel showed him the way in a dream. He went to Mount Ganos, where many monks were in hiding under Theodosius, bishop of Cyzicus, and from that bishop he received baptism and the angelic habit, and lived in a cave church as an example to the whole brotherhood. After three years the bishop was told by an angel to ordain him bishop and to send him and all the monks to Sicily. Nine soldiers of his old company came to see him, believed, were baptised and went with him.

They settled in a desert place called Gigia near the river Asinos. Years later the persecution began again, and the governor of Sicily was told that a bishop was living nearby with a great brotherhood. All hundred and ninety-nine monks were seized and beheaded, and the bishop alone was kept alive to be tortured. They burnt him with fire and he was unharmed; they tied him by the feet to wild horses to drag him, and the horses would not move; they cut out his tongue, stoned him and at last beheaded him, and threw his body out to the beasts and the birds. A herdsman with an evil spirit came upon it and fell on his face, and the spirit went out of him crying, woe is me, where shall I flee from the face of Nikon; and the bishop of Messina came with his clergy and buried him and his disciples.
