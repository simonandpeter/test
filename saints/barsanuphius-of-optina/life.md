# Barsanuphius of Optina

Paul Ivanovich Plikhankov was born at Samara on 5 July 1845, the son of John and Natalia. His mother died while he was still an infant and his father married again so that the boy should have a mother; the stepmother was severe with him but was a true mother to him, and he loved her greatly. A descendant of the Orenburg Cossacks, he was enrolled in the cadet corps at Polotsk, finished his studies at the military school at Orenburg and became an officer, and afterwards completed the Cossack officers' school at Petersburg, took a post in the Kazan military district, and at last became a colonel.

Lying ill with pneumonia, and feeling he had not long to live, he asked the medical orderly to read to him from the Gospel, and lost consciousness. He saw the heavens opened and was afraid of that strong light; his whole life with all its sins passed before his eyes, and repentance took hold of him, and a voice told him to go to the monastery of Optina. The doctors did not believe he would recover. He did recover, and in August 1889 he went to Optina, where the elder was Saint Ambrose, who told him to put his worldly affairs in good order.

Two years later Ambrose blessed him to break every tie with the world and come to Optina within three months. It was not easy to leave the army in three months: he was offered promotion to general and asked to defer his retirement, and some tried to find him a wife and laughed at his resolve to become a monk. On the very last day of the three months his affairs were settled and he returned to Optina — and Ambrose was already lying in his coffin in the monastery church.

Saint Anatoly I, who followed Ambrose as elder, sent him to the hieromonk Nectarius as disciple and cell-mate. He was received as a novice in 1892 and tonsured rassophore a year later, and over the next ten years passed through the degrees of monastic life, being ordained deacon in 1902 and priest in 1903. He had already received the schema in secret in December 1900, in a grave illness; asked what name he wished, he answered that it did not matter, and was given the name Barsanuphius after Saint Barsanuphius of Tver and Kazan. He did not receive the mantle until December 1902.

From September 1903 he helped Father Joseph, the superior of the skete, in the spiritual guidance of the brothers and of the sisters of Shamordino; in 1904 he was sent to the east as a military priest to care for the wounded, and returned to Optina on 1 November 1905. When Joseph grew too old and weak, Barsanuphius was made superior in his place, and quickly restored order, discipline, the paying of debts and the repair of the buildings, joining strictness to a fatherly care for everyone under his hand.

Like all the Optina fathers he was given foresight and the healing of the body's and the soul's sicknesses. One of his spiritual sons, the monk Innocent Pavlov, remembered how frightened he was at his first confession to a father who knew his most hidden thoughts and reminded him of things and people he had long forgotten; the saint spoke gently, said God had allowed him to see them, and told him to say nothing of it to anyone while he lived. He loved spiritual books and above all the Lives of the Saints, and said that many of the questions of our life find their answer there, because the Lives teach how to get past hardship and how to keep a working faith; it grieved him that so many had not read them.

He kept many saints in his daily rule, and not by accident: each saint, he explained, had a part in his life, and if something of weight happened on a day he would look to see whose feast it was and commemorate them ever after. On 17 December 1891, the feast of the prophet Daniel and the three holy youths, he left Kazan and never returned; on that day he resolved to die to the world, and he felt that God had brought him out of a furnace of passions as the three youths were brought out of the fire, unharmed because he had not bowed to fornication, to pride, to greed or to the other idols.

The brothers who came later were mostly from easier surroundings and did not understand the holiness of the monastic life or what an elder is, and they began to demand reform, to seek posts of government and to press for the skete's closing; on their complaints Barsanuphius was moved to be superior of the Golutvin monastery. He found it in ruin, did not lose his zeal, and it soon recovered its spiritual breath and its finances with it. Early in 1913 he fell ill again and asked Metropolitan Macarius of Moscow to bless his retirement to Optina, and it was not to be. He departed on 1 April. His body lay in the church at Golutvino until 6 April, which was Lazarus Saturday; after the service the relics were sent by train to Optina, reached the station at Kozelsk on 8 April, and the coffin was carried by the clergy the rest of the way.
