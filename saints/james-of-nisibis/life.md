# James of Nisibis

Nisibis was a city that lay for a time on the border between the rule of Rome and the rule of Persia, and that paid tribute in those days to the Romans. James was born there, and chose the desert for his dwelling, and lived on the high mountains. In summer and autumn he stayed in the groves with the sky for a roof, and in winter he went into a cave against the frost. His food was nothing sown in a field and nothing worked for, but only what the ground brought forth of itself: fruit gathered from the forest trees, and herbs such as grow in gardens, and of those only enough to keep his days. He never needed fire to cook, for he ate his greens raw, and he needed no wool for clothing, for he covered himself with goatskins.

Mortifying the body so, he strengthened the soul with spiritual food and made his mind a mirror of the Holy Spirit, and looking on the glory of the Lord he climbed towards perfection. His boldness towards God grew daily, so that he asked and received whatever he needed; he foresaw and foretold things to come, for the gift of working wonders had been given him. Leaving the desert, he went into Persia to see the new shoot of the holy faith there and to help it grow as much as he could. Crossing a stream on the way, he came on girls washing clothes with their legs bared almost wholly and their heads uncovered, who looked the stranger over with bold eyes, ashamed neither of his dignity nor of their own nakedness.

Angered at their shamelessness, and wishing to show the power of God for their teaching, he cursed the spring from which the stream ran, and it dried up at once, so that not a drop was found in it; and he cursed the girls, that they should grow old all together, and the black hair of their heads went grey in a moment, like leaves when frost falls on them. They ran into the city and told what had happened, and the citizens came out to the great wonderworker and begged him to soften his anger and give the water back; and at his prayer the spring ran again and the stream filled. Then they asked him to give the girls their youth back too. He was willing, but when he asked where they were they were nowhere to be found and had not come to repent, so he left them as they were, for an unforgettable remembrance of the power of God. He had done it, the account says, not by striking with a rod as Moses did, but by the sign of the Cross.

For these wonders, and being loved and honoured by everyone, he was raised against his will to the bishopric of his own city, Nisibis, though he refused such an office and fled from it. When Arius, first blasphemer of the Son of God and of the Holy Spirit, sharpened his tongue against his Maker and troubled all Egypt, and the emperor Constantine gathered the first council of the whole world at Nicaea, James, bishop of Nisibis, stood in the midst of the holy fathers defending the right faith, and Arius was put out of the Church.

Nisibis was then under the rule of the emperors of Rome; and when Constantine died, Sapor, emperor of Persia, came with all the strength of his armies and surrounded the city to take it, and at the prayers of the bishop the city stood unconquered. Sapor dammed with stones and earth the river that ran through the city, and when much water had gathered he loosed it against the walls, and they fell. But in the night the whole people set to work at the bishop's urging and raised the walls again, so that neither horsemen nor footmen could get in without ladders. Then they begged James to go up on the walls and scatter the enemy's strength; and the Persian army was broken by gnats and flies, and when Sapor saw the man of God walking on the walls he took him for the emperor, in imperial purple with a shining crown on his head, and put to death his own men who had told him there was no emperor in the city, and fled back to his own land.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and puts him in the fourth century, born and bred in Nisibis of Mesopotamia. Loving the desert and stillness, saint.gr says, he left the city for the highest peaks of the region's mountains and lived there, facing the summer's excessive heat and the winter's frost with courage and endurance; his synaxarion says he ate wild herbs and a little water and had one plain tunic for clothing. The fruits of that life were boldness before God and, by the power of the Holy Spirit, foresight and wonders: once, passing a spring where some young women were at their work in a shameless state, he dried the spring up. For his virtues he became bishop of his own city, took part in the First Ecumenical Council at Nicaea in 325 which deposed Arius, and — the page has it — prayed, so that the blasphemer never reached the church he meant to serve in but died of the dissolution of his bowels. When the Persians besieged Nisibis he contributed most of all, by the strength of his faith and his moral weight, to driving off the enemy and breaking the siege; and he fell asleep, venerably, in peace at a very great age. His right hand, the page notes, is at the Armenian Patriarchate of Etchmiadzin.

The Russian calendar keeps him on 13 января ст. ст. and fills out his story. He was the son of a prince of Gefal in Armenia and was well educated, it says, but from his youth he loved solitude and lived a long while in the mountains near the city of Nisibis, on the frontier of Persia and the Roman empire, under the open sky, eating the fruit of trees and green herbs and clothed in goatskins. In the persecution of the emperor Maximian, whose reign it dates 305–311, he became famous for a brave confession of the faith, and for the strictness of his life the people of Nisibis chose him as their bishop, not later than the year 314.

He was known, it goes on, for his burning zeal for the Orthodox faith, for great wonders and for the gift of foresight; by his prayers Nisibis was saved from the invasion of Sapor, king of Persia; and among the fathers of the First Ecumenical Council he was one of the notable defenders of the faith. He founded a catechetical school in Nisibis and taught in it himself. The page's long account is taken from the «История боголюбцев» of Blessed Theodoret of Cyrus, which marvels that in his prayer against the Persian army he asked neither lightning nor fire, as a prophet of old had done, but only that the besiegers be stung by insects, so that they might learn godliness from the power of God; and it ends with the city's people carrying his body away with them when Nisibis was ceded to Persia.
