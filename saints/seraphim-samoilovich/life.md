# Seraphim (Samoilovich)

The Russian church calendar of the Сретенский monastery heads a company of seven with him on 22 октября ст. ст. and carries a life for him and for none of the other six. He was born Semyon Nikolayevich Samoilovich on 19 July 1881 at Mirgorod in Poltava province, finished the Poltava seminary in 1902, and took a teaching post at the two-class school of Unalaska in Alaska. In 1905 he held the same post at Sitka, and there he was tonsured by a bishop and a month later ordained hieromonk.

In 1906 he was superior of the Nuchek mission in North America, and in 1907 and 1908 taught in the Sitka seminary. In America, the calendar's life says, he was the fellow-labourer of the bishop who would become patriarch, who valued the zealous ascetic missionary highly. In 1908 he was assistant to the diocesan missionary of Vladikavkaz and in 1909 confessor of the Ardon seminary. In 1910 he was made deputy superior of the Brotherhood monastery at Mogilev and then, at that same bishop's wish, of the Tolga monastery at Yaroslavl.

At Tolga he wrote a serious study of the monastery's history for its six hundredth anniversary, opened a school of beekeeping for orphan boys, and when the war came set up a field hospital. From 1915 he was deputy superior of the Protection monastery at Uglich, and in February 1920 he was consecrated bishop of Uglich, vicar of the Yaroslavl diocese.

He belonged to the so-called Parallel Synod, which existed with the silent approval of the patriarch in the St Daniel monastery in Moscow and was founded by an archbishop and joined by several prominent hierarchs. Its members held that the patriarchal Synod, kept under close watch by the state police, was ineffective and had been forced into a conciliatory position; they conceived their own body as a standing pre-council conference whose chief aim was to prepare a secret local council able to settle the Church's problems without the state's pressure. Their two founding decisions were the decentralisation of church life as the most effective resistance to Bolshevik control, and the secret consecration of bishops and ordination of priests. In this, the calendar's life says, the Parallel Synod was the foundation stone on which the Catacomb Church came to be built.

From July 1922 he was in the Yaroslavl prison, and after his release in 1924 the patriarch raised him to archbishop; in 1925 he was temporary administrator of the Yaroslavl diocese. In that year one of the deputies of the patriarchal locum tenens, foreseeing his own arrest, named temporary deputies of his own, and he was one of them. On 16 December 1926, when that metropolitan was arrested, he issued an epistle announcing that he had taken up the office of deputy patriarchal locum tenens, and he governed the Church until April 1927, when he handed the administration to the metropolitan newly released from prison.

In 1927 he was summoned to the state police in Moscow and offered compromise terms for the Church's legalisation, with the membership of the Synod dictated to him. He refused, and proposed members of his own, among them a metropolitan then in prison. When the officers objected that the man was inside, he answered that since it was they who were holding him, they should let him out. For that answer he was arrested soon afterwards.

After the Declaration of 1927 he joined the Yaroslavl opposition, which the metropolitan of Yaroslavl led. In January 1928 he wrote with regret that he had handed over the deputy's rights hastily and without conditions, and begged the metropolitan to show courage and issue another declaration to correct the first, which, he wrote, turns men away from the service of truth, and God does not help a lie; he called on him to look to the senior hierarch of Yaroslavl and hand the deputy's rights to him. In April 1928 he was deprived of his see and forbidden to serve, and arrest followed, and then exile from Yaroslavl to a monastery at Mogilev.

In his epistle to the whole Church of January 1929 he went on charging the metropolitan's policy with the grave sin of drawing the faint-hearted and the weak among the brethren into a new Renovationism. He was arrested again in February 1929 and sentenced to five years in the camps, but the epistle had already gone round the country and was printed at Belgrade. On Solovki he was put to general labour; carrying bricks for a building he fell from the scaffolding and broke his ribs, which knitted badly and left him an invalid.

In March 1932 he was released from the camp and exiled for three years to the Northern Territory, where he led the secret Church, ordained priests and received monks. His spiritual children remembered that he grew weaker in body and stayed firm in spirit, and that he held that in a time of persecution there should be no centralised church administration and a bishop should govern his own diocese himself. About 1934 he wrote one more epistle, declaring the metropolitan forbidden to serve for his uncanonical acts from 1927 onward, and a small catacomb council met at Archangel to confirm it as a rule for all the exiled bishops and clergy.

In May 1934 he was arrested again at Archangel, charged with founding a new counter-revolutionary organisation of the adherents of the true Orthodox church, and sentenced to five years in the camps. He served the sentence in the Suslovo section of the Siberian camps, and he was shot in the camp on 22 October old style, 4 November 1937, which is the day the calendar keeps him on. He was numbered among the saints at the Jubilee Council of Bishops in August 2000.

Six more are named with him on the line — four priests and two archimandrites — and the calendar gives none of them a life.
