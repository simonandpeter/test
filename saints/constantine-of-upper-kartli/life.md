# Constantine of Upper Kartli

Constantine was a Georgian prince of Upper Kartli, and the calendar that keeps him remembers him first for what he did with what he had: he was known in his own country for his generosity to the poor and for his protection of churches and of the clergy. The article the same calendar links beside his life makes him a rich and powerful nobleman of the Georgian kingdom, famous through the whole state for his virtue and his pity for sinners, who gave lavishly to churches, monasteries and poor Christians on a pilgrimage to the holy places and came home resolved to live in unceasing prayer.

His was a time when Georgia was attacked again and again. He was eighty-five years old, the article says, when the Arabs came in, killed many Christians in the fighting and then set about a persecution, and he stood at the head of the Georgian army with his son. He was taken prisoner in a battle — the article puts it near the city of Гори — and brought to Tiflis, where he bore the hardships of prison gladly and wrote to monks asking them to pray not that he be given his freedom but that he be able to endure his sufferings.

Brought before the Arab commander Буга, who was boasting of his victories, he told him that those victories had been sent by God not as a reward to the commander but as a punishment to Christians for their sins. The calendar's own life page has Буга shut him in prison and then send him on to a king it calls Джепар, who pressed him to deny Christ and, when he confessed his faith firmly, had him thrown into a foul cell. The linked article has him sent in heavy chains to Samarra, the Abbasid capital, to a caliph it names аль-Мутаваккиль, who offered him honours and his life for his faith and was told that he had power over the body and none over a soul vowed to the Master of heaven and earth.

Two Georgian commanders who had been captured in the fighting and had renounced their faith were sent in to him to argue him into doing the same; he drove them out, said he would keep God's commandments to the end, and finished his prayer with psalms and singing while they mocked him. They were ordered to kill their countryman themselves and could not, and one of the ruler's servants beheaded him, bringing the head in on a dish. The head on a dish is the article's; the calendar's life page says only that he was beheaded by the king's order, in the eighty-fifth year of his life.

**The three pages read for him do not agree about the year.** The day's line and the calendar's life page say 842. The article the calendar links says 10 November 853, sets him under an empress whose reign it dates from 842, and dates the caliph who condemned him 847 to 861. azbyka.ru, asked once for this folder, carries the calendar's life word for word with one figure changed — «в 852 году» — and its own day line prints «(852)» and calls him a great martyr, «вмч. Константина-Кахи, кн. Грузинского», where its own page for him says martyr and where the calendar this folder cites says martyr too. All three years are recorded here and none is chosen, the grade is left as the cited line gives it, and the by-name Кахи is set down without being made part of the recorded form or of the English name. His body was hung up in the public square, the life page says, but Christians took it and carried it into Georgia, and the place of its burial is not known. Under the catholicos Иоанн II, who is dated 871 to 893, a feast was appointed in his honour, and a service for him was composed later by the catholicos Антоний.
