# Moses the Ethiopian

Moses was a bought slave of a rich landowner, hard and unruly, making trouble daily until his master lost patience and threw him out. He found refuge in a band of robbers and, with his enormous bodily strength, soon imposed himself and became their chief.

Hunted by the authorities for his many crimes, he went to hide deep in the desert where the most famous ascetics lived, and their company slowly tamed him; the grace of God overshadowed him, his heart softened, he repented truly and asked for redemption. The change was radical, and in a short time he reached the measure of the great fathers of the desert; after baptism he was even ordained priest. Two virtues adorned him above all, true repentance and deep humility: to his last breath he wept bitterly for his sins and held himself lower not only than men but than the dumb creation. Once four robbers, his old companions, broke into his hut not imagining whom they would find; he tied them up with ease, carried them to the assembly of the elders and asked what he should do with them — it is no longer fitting for me to punish a man — and they confessed, repented and became monks. On the day the patriarch of Alexandria ordained him, and said kindly as he vested him that he had become white as a dove, Moses asked whether he judged by the outside or the inside; the patriarch, to test his humility, had the clergy drive him from the sanctuary with insults, and he went at once without a word, reproaching himself. At seventy-five he left this life violently: pagan robbers broke into the cave where he was living and killed him with knives, and so the Lord’s word to Peter came true once more, that all who take the sword die by the sword.

The Russian and Serbian calendars keep him the same day, 28 August (Julian), with the troparion that calls him a desert-dweller, an angel in the body and a wonderworker. The Sretensky calendar has him an Ethiopian of the fourth century, black of face and so called the Moor; a slave of a great man in his youth, driven out by his master after a murder, he joined a band of robbers, who chose him leader for his harsh temper and great bodily strength, and with them did many murders and robberies, so that all feared even his name. After some years of such a life he repented by God’s great mercy, left the band and went to a desert monastery, where he wept long, begging to be received, and the monks, not believing his repentance, took him only when he would not stop pleading; there he was in full obedience to the abbot and brethren, shedding many tears for his sinful life — profiting much, the Prologue says, from the teaching of Macarius, Arsenius and Isidore — and after a time went to a solitary cell, in prayer and the strictest fasting. Once four robbers of his old band attacked his cell; he, who had not lost his huge strength, bound them all, carried them on his shoulders to the monastery and asked the elders what to do with the captives — who told him to let them go; learning they had fallen into the hands of their old chief, and that he had spared them, the robbers followed his example, repented and became monks, and when the rest of the band heard of his repentance they left robbery and became zealous monks too. He was not soon freed from the passions: he went often to the abbot, Abba Isidore, asking how to be rid of the passion of fornication, and the elder, experienced in the spiritual war, taught him never to eat his fill but to stay half-hungry in the strictest abstinence; when the passion would not leave him in his dreams Isidore taught him all-night vigils, and he stood whole nights at prayer without bending the knee, lest he sleep. From the long struggle he fell into despondency, and when the thought came to leave his cell Isidore strengthened him with a vision — many demons in the west preparing for battle, and in the east a still greater number of holy angels making ready too — assuring him that the angels’ power exceeds the demons’ and that the long fight with the passions was needed to cleanse him wholly of his old sins. He took up a new labour, going round the desert cells by night and bringing each brother water from the well, above all the elders who lived far from it and had not the strength to carry their own; once, bending over the well, he felt a heavy blow in the back and fell as if dead, and lay there till dawn — the demons’ revenge for his victory over them — and the brethren carried him to his cell in the morning, where he lay a whole year paralysed. Recovered, he told the abbot with firm resolve that he would go on striving; but the Lord himself set an end to those years of struggle: Isidore blessed his disciple, told him the passion had left him, and bade him receive the holy mysteries and go in peace to his cell, and from then Moses had power from the Lord over the demons. The fame of his labours spread among the monks and beyond the desert, and when the governor of the country wished to see him he left his cell to hide from visitors; on the road he met the governor’s servants, who asked the way to the hermit Moses’s cell — it is not worth going to that lying, worthless monk, he told them — and when they went back to the monastery and repeated the old man’s words, the brethren knew from their description that it was Moses himself. After many years of monastic labour he was ordained deacon; the bishop put the white vestment on him and said, Abba Moses is all white now — and he answered, master, what makes a man clean, the outside or the inside? In his humility he thought himself inwardly unworthy of the diaconate, and once the bishop, to test him, had the clergy drive the deacon from the altar, reviling him as an unworthy Ethiopian, and he took the dishonour with complete humility; having tried him the bishop ordained him presbyter, in which rank he laboured fifteen years and gathered seventy-five disciples. At seventy-five he warned his monks that robbers would soon fall on the skete and kill everyone in it, and blessed them to leave in good time to escape a violent death; they begged him to go with them, and he answered: for many years I have waited for the time when the word of my Master, the Lord Jesus Christ, would be fulfilled in me — all who take the sword shall perish by the sword. Seven brothers stayed with him, one of whom hid nearby as the robbers approached; the robbers killed Moses and the six monks who had stayed with him, about the year 400. Tormented by the demon of fornication, the Prologue adds, he confessed to Isidore, fasted more, kept night vigil standing, and made a habit of carrying water all night to the elders from a distant well, until after six years of fearful labour Isidore healed him miraculously of the thoughts, fancies and dreams the demons brought; ordained priest in old age, he founded his own monastery, had seventy-five disciples and lived seventy-five years, and foreseeing his death told his disciples to flee because barbarians would fall on the monastery — he must die by violence, he said, having once done violence, as it is written, he who takes the sword shall perish by the sword; he stayed with six brethren, the barbarians came and cut them down, and a brother hidden nearby saw seven bright crowns come down on the seven martyrs. Its verses remember his answer to those who asked whether others’ sins did not grieve him: he who has a dead man in his house does not weep over another’s dead, but over his own.
