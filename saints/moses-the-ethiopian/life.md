# Moses the Ethiopian

Moses was a bought slave of a rich landowner, hard and unruly, making trouble daily until his master lost patience and threw him out. He found refuge in a band of robbers and, with his enormous bodily strength, soon imposed himself and became their chief.

Hunted by the authorities for his many crimes, he went to hide deep in the desert where the most famous ascetics lived, and their company slowly tamed him; the grace of God overshadowed him, his heart softened, he repented truly and asked for redemption. The change was radical, and in a short time he reached the measure of the great fathers of the desert; after baptism he was even ordained priest. Two virtues adorned him above all, true repentance and deep humility: to his last breath he wept bitterly for his sins and held himself lower not only than men but than the dumb creation. Once four robbers, his old companions, broke into his hut not imagining whom they would find; he tied them up with ease, carried them to the assembly of the elders and asked what he should do with them — it is no longer fitting for me to punish a man — and they confessed, repented and became monks. On the day the patriarch of Alexandria ordained him, and said kindly as he vested him that he had become white as a dove, Moses asked whether he judged by the outside or the inside; the patriarch, to test his humility, had the clergy drive him from the sanctuary with insults, and he went at once without a word, reproaching himself. At seventy-five he left this life violently: pagan robbers broke into the cave where he was living and killed him with knives, and so the Lord’s word to Peter came true once more, that all who take the sword die by the sword.

The Russian and Serbian calendars keep him the same day, 28 August (Julian), with the troparion that calls him a desert-dweller, an angel in the body and a wonderworker. The Sretensky calendar has him an Ethiopian of the fourth century, black of face and so called the Moor; a slave of a great man in his youth, driven out by his master after a murder, he joined a band of robbers, who chose him leader for his harsh temper and great bodily strength, and with them did many murders and robberies, so that all feared even his name. After some years of such a life he repented by God’s great mercy, left the band and went to a desert monastery, where he wept long, begging to be received, and the monks, not believing his repentance, took him only when he would not stop pleading; there he was in full obedience to the abbot and brethren, shedding many tears for his sinful life — profiting much, the Prologue says, from the teaching of Macarius, Arsenius and Isidore — and after a time went to a solitary cell, in prayer and the strictest fasting. Once four robbers of his old band attacked his cell; he, who had not lost his huge strength, bound them all, carried them on his shoulders to the monastery and asked the elders what to do with the captives — who told him to let them go; learning they had fallen into the hands of their old chief — the Sretensky calendar’s life runs on past the point read here. Tormented by the demon of fornication, the Prologue adds, he confessed to Isidore, fasted more, kept night vigil standing, and made a habit of carrying water all night to the elders from a distant well, until after six years of fearful labour Isidore healed him miraculously of the thoughts, fancies and dreams the demons brought; ordained priest in old age, he founded his own monastery, had seventy-five disciples and lived seventy-five years, and foreseeing his death told his disciples to flee because barbarians would fall on the monastery — he must die by violence, he said, having once done violence, as it is written, he who takes the sword shall perish by the sword; he stayed with six brethren, the barbarians came and cut them down, and a brother hidden nearby saw seven bright crowns come down on the seven martyrs. Its verses remember his answer to those who asked whether others’ sins did not grieve him: he who has a dead man in his house does not weep over another’s dead, but over his own.
