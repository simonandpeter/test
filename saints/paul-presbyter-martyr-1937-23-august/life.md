# Paul (Gaidai)

Paul Ignatievich Gaidai was born on 15 January 1886 in Bessarabia, at
Izmail — the case files disagree, some naming Galați in Romania, where the
fuller life has him born in 1896, but his relatives at Izmail have
documents that he was baptised in the Edinoverie church there; he lived
in Moldavia and finished the seminary. In his youth he had the spiritual
counsel of John of Kronstadt, who charged him to pass after his death
under the guidance of the Odessa archpriest Jonah (Atamansky), John’s
friend and spiritual intimate: go to the archpriest Jonah at Odessa and
follow in his steps. After John’s death he did so, came to Odessa and
served as psalmist in a town church under Fr Jonah, meaning to become a
monk; but Fr Jonah did not bless him for monasticism and found him a
bride, a devout maiden of noble family, Kapitolina Dmitrievna, then about
fifty while Paul was little over twenty, married them and blessed them to
live as brother and sister — which he never regretted, thanking Fr Jonah
and telling his spiritual children, love and pity Matushka; she is my
cover — and they took in two girls, Stesha and Vera. Soon he was ordained
deacon and priest and began to serve at the church of Peter and Paul in
Odessa, faithful to Patriarch Tikhon through the schisms, and drew the
hearts of the Orthodox at once by his frequent services, his fine
preaching and above all what marked his teachers, the righteous John and
Jonah — a warm living faith, boundless love for people and tireless
labour, giving himself in sacrifice for the flock of Christ; people
streamed to the young priest made wise by the Holy Spirit. The
authorities noticed it, and exiled him from Odessa to a distant farmstead
and then to the village of Kapaklievka in the Taras Shevchenko district of
the Odessa region, where he celebrated the Liturgy daily, served molebens
for the sick and exorcised the possessed, and the sick and the troubled by
unclean spirits came from all the towns and villages around, filled the
church and were healed by his prayers, Kapitolina his faithful companion.
On 30 April 1929 the Odessa GPU arrested him for practising healing, deceit
to rouse superstition for gain and counter-revolutionary activity, and on
30 June 1929 the special board of the Ukrainian OGPU exiled him
administratively for three years to Turukhansk in the Krasnoyarsk
country, where Kapitolina, the two girls and some twenty spiritual
children, whole families among them, followed him. Turukhansk was hunger
and mosquitoes; his little house stood under the bluff on the Yenisei
bank, and the community lived by gathering and selling wild onion and
burning lime, the men hiring themselves to fell timber and the women to
wash linen so as to help him and survive, sometimes eating nothing for
weeks, and the Lord kept them all by his prayers. Freed in 1933 he moved
to Leningrad and served at the church of St George, but was soon arrested
again “for belonging to the Union of Michael the Archangel” and put in
the Kresty prison, where a violent madman, a possessed man, was put into
his solitary cell as a torment — and, entering, grew calm when the priest
prayed for him; at interrogation he denied belonging to any political
party, and in the same year the Leningrad NKVD sentenced him under article
58-10 to three more years in the Turukhansk country, but he fell gravely
ill and in 1934 was moved to Akmolinsk, where Kapitolina, the girls and
the spiritual children followed him again and built a low house of adobe
to gather in for prayer. On 17 October 1935 he was arrested again and
held for a time in the Akmolinsk prison, where he fell ill and lay in the
infirmary, and when the Nativity fast came and he tried to keep it he was
mocked and forced to eat forbidden food; charged under articles 58-10 and
11 and denying it, he went on hunger strike to win release, and searches
of his house and his spiritual children’s turned up a great quantity of
tea — three hundred and four slabs of 250 grams and a hundred and
ninety-five of 50 — which served to accuse him and the whole community of
organising journeys to Odessa and Krasnoyarsk for tea to sell in the
Akmolinsk market, that is of speculation; some of the community were
arrested and soon released for want of proof, and he denied the charge.
On 22 January 1936 the Akmolinsk people’s court sentenced him under
article 107 to ten years in the camps, and he was sent to the Karlag. The
case file shows that on 14 April 1937 he escaped from the fifth, Nura,
section and was caught on the 15th at Dzhaila — because, he explained,
recidivists who had robbed him threatened to kill him for reporting it —
and was put for five months in the punishment cell, where he went on
hunger strike to get out of it and meant not to stop until his demand
was met; a search of his cell found “under the hunger-striker Gaidai’s
bunk a clay jug of port of half a litre, a teacup full of cherry jam,
sushki and sweets, one kilogram in all”, of which he said he had not
touched them and had even demanded a doctor to certify whether he had
eaten. Sent on 31 July 1937 to the Karagan work party, he could no longer
go out to work for illness, and the camp authorities, citing witnesses,
accused him of raising his temperature artificially to be excused work —
taking a little bread and a big dose of salt, swallowing it and rubbing it
under his arms; he demanded to be confronted with those who said so. In
August 1937 he was arrested again, charged with systematically carrying on
counter-revolutionary agitation among the prisoners, speaking against the
leaders of the party and government, and raising his temperature to avoid
work, and admitted no guilt; on 31 August 1937 the troika of the Karaganda
NKVD sentenced him to be shot, and the sentence was carried out on 5
September 1937 — 23 August of the old style, the day the Russian calendar
keeps him. His grave is unknown; he was rehabilitated under the law of
the Republic of Kazakhstan of 14 April 1993.
