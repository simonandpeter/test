# Anastasius the Sinaite

Anastasius of Sinai was a monk of the seventh century who left the world and everything in it, and the page reckons him among the fathers of the Church. It is careful to say at once that he is not to be confused with Anastasius the patriarch of Antioch, who was also called the Sinaite and is kept on this same 20 April; the two are separate men, and this corpus keeps them as two. Besides today, he is commemorated with the assembly of the venerable fathers of Sinai on Bright Tuesday.

He was born at Alexandria in Egypt in the first half of the seventh century. While still young he took up his cross according to the Lord's commandment, received the monastic tonsure, and followed the Lord willingly. Desiring a harder ascetic life, he went first to Jerusalem to venerate the holy places, and came from there to Mount Sinai, where he found a company of monks living in severe asceticism and stayed to serve them.

So he came to a deep humility, and received from God great gifts of knowledge and of wisdom. He wrote lives of the holy fathers and composed a number of homilies, and in the end became abbot of the whole monastic community of Mount Sinai. He departed to the Lord in peace, in great old age, some time after the year 700.

Of his counsels the page gives one, on those who receive the holy Mysteries: that if a man has some small human sin, quickly forgiven — a theft of the mind, a sin of the tongue or the hearing or the sight, or one of vainglory or sorrow or anger — then once he has reproached himself for it and confessed it before the Lord he may receive; and that for such men the receiving is unto the forgiveness of sins.

The Greek calendar keeps him on 21 April — the day after the Anastasius of Antioch with whom the Romanian calendar shares his name — and saint.gr's notice is mostly a bibliography. He lived in the seventh century, it says, and came of a noble family; but he soon left the world and the things of this life and was tonsured a monk. He went to Jerusalem, and having venerated the Holy Places he ended at mount Sinai, in the monastery of St Catherine, of which he became abbot. There he found ascetic monks and stayed beside them as their subordinate and their servant; and because he had much humility he received from God the gift of knowledge and of much wisdom, with which he wrote lives of holy fathers and composed discourses profitable to the soul. Having reached deep old age he died peacefully at the beginning of the eighth century.

The page names six of his works: the «Οδηγός», the Guide, in twenty-four chapters, so called because it was meant to serve as a guide in support of Orthodoxy against Monophysitism; the «Ερωτήσεις και αποκρίσεις», Questions and answers on various heads and various persons, in which — following the method of Maximus the Confessor — he settles dogmatic, practical and ecclesiastical matters; a discourse on the holy synaxis and on not judging and not bearing grudges, which treats of the Eucharist; the «Θεωρίαι αναγωγικαί εις την εξαήμερον», anagogical contemplations on the six days, in twelve books; two discourses on the creation of man according to the image of God; and a collection of patristic passages against the Arians. From the third of these the page quotes him: when you see your brother sinning, do not judge him, and least of all when he is a priest; remember that there is one judge, God, who will render to each according to his works. You saw your brother's sin — but do you know all his good deeds? It may even be that the very sin you saw he has already washed away with the tears of his repentance.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović sets his beginning under a teacher the other calendars read for this folder do not name. He was long a monk under the famous abbot [John of the Ladder](/saints/john-of-the-ladder), the entry says, and after that man's death became abbot of Mount Sinai himself. Besides being a great ascetic he was an eloquent writer of the lives of the saints and of other instructive works.

It adds one controversy as well: he waged a fierce struggle against the heretics called акефалита, the Acephali or headless, who denied the Fourth Ecumenical Council at Chalcedon. He died, this book says, in deep old age in 685, and went to the Lord whom he had faithfully served — where doxologia.ro's life puts his repose some time after the year 700. Neither reading is chosen here and neither is displaced; the interval was widened to take both in.
