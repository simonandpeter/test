# Olav of Norway

The life of Olav Haraldsson — Harald's son — was written by the Norwegian poet and historian Snorri Sturluson (1179–1241) in his chronicle of the kings of Norway. Olav was born in 995 and while still young became a viking warrior, soon marked out as a leader and a strategist. He succeeded his father in the rule, holding wide parts of Norway. While he was still in his teens he went on a viking raid in the Baltic lands, and then turned to England, where with the Norwegian raiders he took Canterbury in 1011 and carried off the archbishop prisoner; King Ethelred was to pay ransom for him but delayed too long, and the archbishop was killed.

The Romanian page sets that in its context: when the monasteries of Lindisfarne, Jarrow and Wearmouth on the eastern coast of England were plundered and profaned by Norwegian vikings, Alcuin said that grief filled the whole of Europe, writing to King Ethelred that they and their forefathers had dwelt in that holy land nearly three hundred years, but had never seen such destruction in Britain as had now come upon them from the pagans, nor could anyone have believed that such an attack could come from over the sea; the painting of the church of Saint Cuthbert was fouled with the blood of Christian priests and stripped of its adornments, and the holiest place in Britain had fallen prey to the heathen.

Olav entered at last the service of Duke Richard II of Normandy. Afterwards King Cnut of Denmark and England laid claim to Norway, drew the pagan Norwegian rebels to his side and raised an army against him, and in 1028 the holy king had to leave Norway, fleeing through Sweden to Novgorod in Rus, where he was well received by the Grand Prince Yaroslav the Wise. The Russian prince was married to a viking princess, Irene, the daughter of King Olav of Sweden, who in old age became a nun with the name Anna and was numbered by the Church among the venerable women of the Orthodox calendar.

At Novgorod Olav lived an ascetic life of fasting and prayer and planned a pilgrimage to Jerusalem, but after a vision he decided to return to Norway, and reached it in 1030. On 29 July 1030 he and his army, outnumbered, fought at Stiklestad near Trondheim against the Danish troops joined with the Norwegian rebels. Shortly before the battle he fell asleep with his head on the breast of his most faithful friend, Finn, and dreamed that he climbed a ladder reaching to heaven; and when he woke and told the dream, his friend said, I think it is a sign that you will die. The king told his soldiers that if they lost everything in this battle, God would give them a greater reward than passing goods. In the fighting he fell under the axe-blow of a pagan rebel, saying: O Lord, save! The axe, as the sign of Saint Olav's martyrdom, is worn to this day on the uniform of the Norwegian royal army.

Many wonders were done at his grave, and his holy relics were then taken up incorrupt and sweet-smelling and moved into the church of Saint Clement at Trondheim, which became the cathedral of Nidaros. Because of the miracles at his relics that place became one of the most important pilgrimages of northern Europe. After the Protestant Reformation it is no longer known, unhappily, where the relics of Saint Olav were hidden; a particle of them is kept today in the Roman Catholic church of Saint Olav in Oslo. On the spot where King Olav was martyred, at Stiklestad near Trondheim, a stone church was raised.
