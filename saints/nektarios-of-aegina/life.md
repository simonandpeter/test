# Nektarios of Aegina

Nektarios — Anastasius Kephalas in the world — was born on 1 October 1846 at
Selymbria in Eastern Thrace, the fifth of the six children of the pious and
poor Demosthenes and Maria. His grandmother taught him the fiftieth psalm as
a small boy, and at the verse “I will teach transgressors thy ways” he would
repeat it to himself again and again; he built little pulpits and preached
Christ to the children of his age. After the schools of his town, with his
parents’ blessing, he went at fourteen to Constantinople, since the family
could not keep all its members and Selymbria had no secondary school. The
first years there were hard: he worked for a tobacco merchant who underpaid
him, went in broken shoes and wretched clothes, and had only prayer for
comfort; seeing his master write letters and receive answers, the boy with
his guileless heart wanted to write one too, and had no one to write to but
Christ — my Christ, I have no shoes and no clothes; send them to me, please;
you know how much I love you; Anastasius — addressed to the Lord Jesus
Christ in paradise. A neighbouring shopkeeper who was fond of the boy met
him on the way to the post, offered to post the letter, read it out of
curiosity, and, moved to tears, sent him money anonymously; when the boy
appeared better dressed his master beat him as a thief, and the shopkeeper,
seeing it, took him into his own furniture shop, where the hours were fewer
and he could go to church, read the Scriptures and go to school, and soon
his family joined him in the City. After seven years there, at twenty, he
left without finishing his schooling to teach at Lithi on Chios, where
everyone who knew him loved him for his evident beauty of soul, and where
his great benefactor John Choremis, a rich notable whose nephew had once
helped the penniless boy board the ship for Constantinople, took him under
his protection. He had resolved to leave the world: after three years’ trial
at the New Monastery of Chios he was tonsured on 7 November 1876, at thirty,
with the name Lazarus, and on 15 January 1877 Metropolitan Gregory of Chios
ordained him deacon and named him Nektarios — his bent was to the monastic
life above all, and the pressure of his gifts of speech and learning turned
him to the secular clergy, though he never forgot monasticism to the end of
his life. He attended the gymnasium on Chios until the great earthquake of
1881 sent him to Athens, where he finished at the Varvakeion, and the same
year went to Alexandria to Patriarch Sophronius, who, impressed and on good
recommendations, sent him back to the theological faculty of Athens, where
he distinguished himself and won the Papadakis scholarship — a great relief,
Choremis having died and left him in want. With his degree in 1885 he
returned to Alexandria, was ordained priest at once, was patriarchal
secretary and archimandrite within five months and preacher and patriarchal
commissioner within two more, and on 15 January 1889 was consecrated bishop
of Pentapolis in Libya, the record of the election surviving. He took the
episcopate with great humility, asking the Lord why he had raised him to so
great a dignity when from his youth he had asked only to be a plain worker
of the divine word, and praying for humility and the seed of the other
virtues, and to live all his days by Paul’s word, it is no longer I that
live, but Christ that lives in me.

Sophronius was near ninety and the succession was open; the people, who had
been served by his manifold charitable, pastoral and anti-heretical work,
wanted Nektarios, and with the patriarch’s favour he was the first choice.
His rivals, knowing it, set him aside by accusing him of plotting to
overthrow Sophronius and by vague charges against his morals, backed by
clergy who feared that a bishop’s policy of austerity and poverty for the
Church would leave the patriarchate without the means to resist political
and national pressure. Sophronius believed it and dismissed him at once,
without the synodal hearing the canons required; Nektarios would not push
the matter to extremes and left Alexandria, while his enemies, bent on his
moral and financial ruin, blackened his name in Athens and Constantinople so
that he could find no work, and withheld his stipends. He rented a small
room on the edge of Athens and could not pay for it or for food; the
archbishop Germanus liked him but under pressure from the synod could not
help, and the minister of education could not, since he was not a Greek
subject. At last a member of the government named Melas, who had known him
in Alexandria, got him appointed preacher at Chalcis, where the rumours
followed him and people from Athens and locals alike jeered at his sermons.
In 1891, two years after the charges, while attempts to remove him were
still being made in the government, the plot was laid bare: it came out that
he had drawn no salary and served without pay as bishop, that he was still
by right bishop of Pentapolis, illegally driven out, and his name was
cleared of every moral scandal and every intrigue against the patriarch;
after the harshness he had borne, the people of Chalcis took him to their
hearts, he preached with new ease, his fame spread past Chalcis, and when
the local see fell vacant they all but demanded him for it. In 1892 and 1893
he was preacher in Laconia and in Phthiotis and Boeotia, touring the towns
and villages, while friends worked to move him to the Rizarios school in
Athens, and in March 1894, over renewed whispers, he was made its director.
He worked with the zeal of God to plant the sacred zeal of the priesthood in
his students and to see to their livelihood, reformed the curriculum,
improved even the food and the sport, secured four scholarships a year for
boys from Asia Minor, made the school’s church of St George its centre of
worship and the school a spiritual foundation with scholars invited to
lecture, and was himself the living example: when students came to blows he
punished not them but himself, with three days’ fasting, as the one to
blame; he addressed his pupils barefoot once, having given his shoes at the
door to a poor man who had none; and when the overseers quarrelled over who
should clean the latrines he settled it by cleaning them. He kept up a
liturgical, preaching, confessing and charitable work beside it, was close
to Father Planas and joined the vigils at the little church of St Elisha
where Papadiamantis and Moraitidis sang; he wrote much and gave much of it
free to people and theologians who could not buy it, never sought profit or
notice, and when accused never disputed but stayed meek and said God would
vindicate the truth; when Sophronius died and he was asked to succeed him,
he refused. He directed the Rizarios school fourteen years, to 1908, when he
left it for his health.

His wish for the monastic life never left him, and grew after his visit to
Athos and his friendship with the elder
[Daniel](/saints/daniel-katounakiotis) in 1898; he looked for a place for a
monastery, an ecclesiastical Parthenon as he called it, for the end of his
life, and when four lonely women under his direction asked to be nuns under
him he found an old abandoned monastery at Xantos on Aegina and settled them
there with three nuns already on the island; it began again in 1904 under
his guidance while he was still at the school. His coming to Aegina was
marked by two things that made him loved at once: he healed a possessed
youth, and when the villagers, three years without rain, asked him to serve
and pray, he served with the whole island present and the rain came the same
day, and the Aeginetans held him a saint in his lifetime. Retiring to the
monastery in 1908, old and ill, he never stopped working, in spirit or with
his hands — building cells, opening roads to the monastery, gardening,
holding no work a shame but a blessing; the gifts that came grew the house
to fifteen nuns in four years and went mostly to the island’s poor, and the
crowds who came for his blessing helped the islanders breathe; through the
Balkan wars and the First World War he was to them brother, helper, guide
and companion, and went on writing books that the press and the learned
foundations of the day acknowledged. Bitterness was not lacking:
Metropolitan Theocletus of Athens, despite his first consent, refused for
more than ten years to recognise the monastery, which therefore had no legal
person and could not receive the legacies left to it; and in 1918 a nun’s
mother accused him of immorality, a lie the Athens prosecutor’s inquiry
exposed, the girl having gone to the monastery of her own will, while clergy
on the island who envied him slandered him behind his back — meek, calm and
patient under every accusation and humiliation, faithful to the Gospel, to
Christ’s example and to the writings of Nicodemus the Hagiorite, trusting
wholly in God’s justice, he never saw the monastery recognised. His end was
painful, a chronic disease of the prostate on top of age and hardship; he
still planned a school; in 1920 he was taken into the Aretaieion hospital in
Athens, where cancer was found, and on 9 November 1920 he died. His body
gave out fragrance and myrrh flowed from his face; it went the same day to
Aegina and was buried at his little monastery by a crowd of clergy and
people, and the news shook Greece from end to end; a paralysed man in the
next bed, touched with the dead saint’s vest, was healed. The grave was
opened repeatedly in the years that followed and for more than twenty years
the body was whole and incorrupt, breathing the fragrance of holiness, until
it dissolved by judgements God knows, as many incorrupt relics have; on 3
September 1953 Metropolitan Procopius of Hydra translated the grace-flowing
relics before clergy, monastics and a great crowd, and an ineffable
fragrance filled the place, and in 1961 the Ecumenical Patriarchate formally
recognised him a saint. The room where he died is a small church on the
second floor of the Aretaieion, hung with icons and the votive offerings of
the sick’s relatives. The Greek and Romanian calendars keep the translation
of 1953 on 3 September, which is the line this entry stands under, and his
feast on 9 November.
