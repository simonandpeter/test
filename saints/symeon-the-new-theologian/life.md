# Symeon the New Theologian

Symeon the New Theologian was born in the year 949 at Galatea in Paphlagonia, and studied at Constantinople. His father prepared him for a career in the law, and for a short while the young man held a high position at the imperial court. At fourteen he met the well-known father Symeon the Pious at the monastery of Stoudios, who was to mark his spiritual growth deeply. He stayed in the world several years more, preparing for the monastic life under that father's obedience, and entered the monastery when he was twenty-seven.

Symeon the Pious told him to read the writings of Mark the Ascetic along with other spiritual writers. He read them carefully and put into practice what he read, and three points in Mark's book On the Spiritual Law struck him especially: that a man must listen to his conscience and do what it tells him, if he wishes to heal his soul; that only by keeping the commandments can he draw the grace of the Holy Spirit upon himself; and that whoever prays with the body alone, without spiritual knowledge, is like the blind man who cried out, “Son of David, have mercy on me.”

He was wounded with love for spiritual beauty and set himself to gain it. Besides the rule his father had given him, his conscience told him to add some psalms and prostrations, repeating constantly, “Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me”; and of course he obeyed it. By day he helped the needy people who lived in the house of Patricius, and by night his prayers ran on past midnight. Once as he prayed a divine light, very bright, came down upon him and filled the room; he saw nothing but the light about him and no longer felt the ground beneath him, and it seemed to him that he had himself been turned into light.

Then his mind went up to the heavens and he saw a second ray, brighter than the first, at the end of which Symeon the Pious seemed to stand — the man who had given him the writings of Mark the Ascetic to read. Seven years after that vision he entered the monastery, where he made his fasting and his vigils harder and learned self-denial. The enemy of our salvation raised the brothers against him, though he was indifferent to the praise and the reproach of others; and because of the discontent he was sent to the monastery of Saint Mamas in Constantinople, where he was tonsured into the monastic habit.

About the year 980 he was made abbot of Saint Mamas, and held that office for twenty-five years. He repaired and restored the monastery, which had suffered from the brothers' neglect, and brought order into the monks' life. The strict monastic discipline he fought to keep raised much discontent among them, and once, after the Liturgy, some of them attacked him; when the patriarch of Constantinople put them out of the monastery and meant to hand them over to the civil authorities, Symeon asked that they be treated gently and left to live in the world.

About 1005 he resigned the abbacy in favour of Arsenius and settled somewhere near the monastery, in quiet; and there he made his theological works, of which some fragments appear in the Philokalia. Their chief theme is the hidden work of spiritual perfection and the struggle with the passions and with evil thoughts. He wrote instructions for monks — the Theological and Practical Chapters, a treatise on the three methods of prayer, and a treatise on faith — and he was besides a true Christian poet, writing the Hymns of Divine Love, about seventy poems full of deep religious meditation.

His priceless teaching on the mysteries of the prayer of the mind and on the spiritual struggle brought him the name of the New Theologian. That teaching was not his own creation, the life says, but had simply been forgotten with time; some of it seemed strange and unacceptable to his contemporaries, which led to conflicts with the clergy of Constantinople, and he ended by being exiled from the city. He crossed the strait of the Bosphorus and settled at the old monastery of Saint Macrina, and in 1021 he fell asleep in peace in the Lord.

In his lifetime he received the gift of working wonders, and after his death many wonders happened, among them the miraculous finding of his icon. His life was written by his disciple Saint Nicetas Stethatos.
