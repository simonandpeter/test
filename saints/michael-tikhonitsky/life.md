# Michael Tikhonitsky

Michael Tikhonitsky was born in 1846 in the village of Oshet in the Nolinsk
district of Vyatka province, the son of Peter Tikhonitsky, psalmist of the
Saviour church, and his wife Anfisa; an obedient, inquisitive boy, he heard
the Gospel from childhood read in church by his grandfather the deacon John
Yankevsky and helped his father read and sing in the choir, went to the
church school at Vyatka — a pupil of quite good abilities, zealous diligence
and satisfactory progress, one teacher wrote — and then to the seminary,
firm in his faith and resolved to serve the Church and carry on the old
priestly line of the Tikhonitskys. Finishing in 1868 he was ordained deacon,
and in 1870 priest, for the little church of Elijah at the Izhevsk works,
lately given to the co-religionists of the old rite, where at twenty-four he
set himself to preach and to bring parishioners back from the schism, and
kept many from error and returned many to the Church. He was moved with his
family to Bystritsa — his wife Apollinaria and his mother Anfisa keeping a
house that had to be a pattern for the parish, and he finding time beside
the church and the village school to bring up the children, of whom Benjamin
and Vladimir became priests and Elpidiphorus and the daughters Maria, Julia
and Vera teachers — and two years later to Podrelye, where he taught in the
zemstvo school, and the dean wrote of him that he was zealous in preaching
the word of God and explaining the Church’s teaching, drew the parishioners
infected with the schism to her, and deserved the diocese’s notice; the
parishioners hurried to church every Sunday and feast to hear him, and his
pupils loved him as sincerely. Compassion and mercy were always his marks —
he joined the Red Cross to help the sick and needy and received its badge of
the second class — and his first care was the house of God: at the
parishioners’ request he headed the parish trusteeship that built the stone
wall that still surrounds the Trinity church, and at Bystritsa taught the
law of God in the village school so simply, the teachers said, that every
child understood, and questioned the whole class rather than one. In 1881 he
was moved to the Trinity church of the town of Orlov in Vyatka province,
content in every parish with little, living in the church house without a
salary, on what the parishioners gave freely for the offices, his modesty
and humility an example to them; on 11 December 1881 his wife died, the
loved companion and best friend who had shared every hardship of parish
life. From 1898 he served the little Ascension church in the old town
cemetery of Orlov, where the peasants of the villages round came; his son
remembered him there on Easter night, framed in silver hair and a silver
vestment, shining like a saint from an icon, the procession moving round the
church under the bells with its unquenched candles, the old priest’s tenor
and the psalmist’s young bass singing that the angels sing in heaven, the
paschal service begun before the closed doors, his young, ringing “Christ is
risen!” and the crowd’s thunder in answer, and then the old man stepping
down the porch and turning to the dark and the scattered graves with a voice
of peculiar tenderness — and you who are in the tombs, Christ is risen! —
while the crowd froze and strained to hear, and the sighs of the spring
night and the waking earth came back like the answer of the departed. In
1913, in the tercentenary of the Romanovs, the loved and respected priest
was appointed to the Kazan cathedral of Orlov, and at sixty-seven became
confessor of the town’s clergy and dean of its churches while still teaching
at the girls’ gymnasium.

When Patriarch Tikhon, sorrowing for the people’s calamity, issued the
message that anathematised the makers of bloody reprisals against the
innocent and called the pastors to summon their children to the defence of
the Church’s trampled rights, Father Michael understood it, as he wrote
afterwards, as a call to peace among people and nothing else, and on 15
February 1918 read it at the liturgy in the Kazan cathedral; that evening he
was put under house arrest, and two days later the case went to the
revolutionary tribunal. The indictment was full of hatred for the Church and
had not a word against him but the reading; he did not admit guilt; his
defenders pointed to his character and to the fact that he was no politician
and had spoken not against the Soviet power but for the Church and the
faith, and the prosecution’s own witnesses defended him, telling of his
generosity to the poor and his disinterestedness. On 28 February 1918 the
tribunal of the Orlov soviet found him guilty of a counter-revolutionary act
but, in view of his great age and the two weeks of house arrest already
served, gave him a public censure for the first time and a warning of
severer punishment. Half a year passed; he served on, his health broken by
the confinement — the close air and the want of movement had been murderous
to an old man’s exhausted strength, his daughter said — visiting the sick,
helping the poor, caring for prisoners. On 5 September 1918 the Cheka
searched the Tikhonitskys’ flat and found nothing; he was apparently told to
leave town and did, unaware of the trap; the next day, 7 September, a
warrant was issued for the search and arrest of the priest Tikhonitsky
“living in the village of Rusanovo”, to be taken at the bearer’s discretion
in the villages of Levino volost or on the road, so that flight from justice
could be laid to him, and on 8 September he was arrested in the village of
Nizhnyaya Yedoma in the house of Prokopy Fokin, the search “without result”,
and taken with Fokin and a Catherine Khalturina on the abstract charge of
counter-revolution. I do not understand the present struggle, he wrote in
the record, but I would wish there were no such struggle among a Christian
people; I looked on your commission’s arrests as a needful thing; I looked
on my own as an arrest for inquiry, for I knew I would not be punished
without trial and was sure of release, since I had always taught the people
only good. On 14 September a daughter petitioned the Orlov commission to
examine the case, be convinced of his complete innocence and free him, that
the old man might spend the remnant of his days preparing in peace for
death; it was not considered. He was questioned on 19 September, and on 20
September 1918 the investigator Yurevits resolved: for spreading appeals
against the power, the priest Tikhonitsky to be shot, and asked the chairman
of the Orlov Cheka the same day to have it carried out. At nine that evening
it was. Led to execution he sang the paschal canon and comforted those with
him, prayed for his executioners on the road, took off his cassock and threw
it to a parishioner he knew, to be sent home, crossed himself and blessed
the almshouse as they passed its church, and at the cemetery was made to dig
his own grave, hurried, since the guards feared a rescue; he dug shallow
with his last strength, was told it was enough, asked leave to pray, prayed,
turned to the guards and waited, would not have his hands tied or his eyes
bound, and stood straight, almost stern, until the shots; he fell into the
grave and they filled it quickly, while some schoolgirls watched from the
ground, afraid to breathe. His son Bishop Vladimir, a member of the Council
in Moscow, learned of it there, and the Patriarch himself came in a cab to
condole with him. Three of his sons served the Church — Vladimir, a monk
early and in emigration Metropolitan and Exarch of Western Europe; Benjamin,
a priest at Vyatka for many years and from 1942 a monk and Archbishop of
Kirov who did much to raise the diocese; Elpidiphorus, a gifted teacher and
deep believer who died in Stalin’s camps — and his daughters taught long at
Orlov and humbly tended their father’s grave, which became a place of
reverent veneration; the path to the martyrs’ grave never grew over,
Elpidiphorus wrote in 1926, and on Easter night he sent his father the
greeting, Christ is risen, you who are in the tomb and who live in my heart.
On 8 September 2008 his relics were found in the Orlov cemetery at the place
tradition had kept, and rest in the parish church of the Nativity of the
Mother of God; the Holy Synod numbered him among the new martyrs, and his
glorification was kept in 2003. The Russian calendar keeps him on 7
September of the old style, 20 September, and in the synaxes of the Udmurt
and the Vyatka saints.
