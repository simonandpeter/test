# John Koukouzelis

The Greek synaxarion calls John a fine musician with a beautiful voice. He came from Dyrrachium, lived in the years of the Komnenoi, and lost his father young; his devout mother had him taught sacred letters, and because he was clever and sweet-voiced everyone called him angel-voiced. When he was of age he went to Mount Athos and lived as a monk in the cell of the Archangels belonging to the Great Lavra, where he chanted with [Gregory the Domestikos](/saints/gregory-the-domestikos), who is kept on the same day. He died in peace at the beginning of the fifteenth century, the synaxarion says, and was buried in his cell; his head and other relics are at the Great Lavra, and a fragment at the monastery of Saint Anne at Lygaria near Lamia. It keeps him with an apolytikion and a kontakion of his own, and with a second apolytikion and kontakion shared with Gregory.

The Romanian life, which has no hymns for him, makes him Bulgarian by origin, from Dyrrachium, orphaned young, and a pupil of the court school at Constantinople, where the emperor John II Komnenos (1118–1143) loved him and made him first chanter. Asked once what he had eaten, he answered beans and peas — koukouzelis — and the nickname stayed. The court did not content him, and when he learned the emperor meant to marry him he followed the abbot of the Lavra, who had been at the palace, to Athos, and told the porter he was a shepherd who wished to become a monk. He was sent out with the monastery’s goats; a hermit heard him singing alone and the goats stopping their grazing to listen, and told the abbot, who knew him for the young man of the palace. The abbot went to Constantinople, won the emperor’s promise of help and told him everything, and the emperor let John stay. He built a kathisma of the Archangels near the monastery, kept silence there through the week and came to chant in the right-hand choir on Sundays and feasts. Once, dozing in the choir during the Akathist, he saw the Theotokos, who told him to sing to her and put a gold coin in his hand; he found it there on waking and hung it on her icon, afterwards called Koukouzelissa, and icon and coin became wonderworking. Another time she healed his leg, rotted from long standing. He wrote books on the order of the services and the art of chant, and hymns of his own. He knew beforehand the hour of his death and asked to be buried in the chapel of the Archangels. He came to Athos at nineteen, and how many years he lived there is not known; the life calls him the patron of church chanters and gives no year.

The two pages disagree on his time — the Greek puts him in the years of the Komnenoi but his death at the start of the fifteenth century, the Romanian at the court of John II Komnenos — and the Greek synaxarion tells the story of the Theotokos’s gold coin of Gregory the Domestikos, though its own apolytikion for John has her reward him with a golden coin.
