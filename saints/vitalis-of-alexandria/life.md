# Vitalis of Alexandria

The Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović gives him a whole page and sets him in a single reign: in the time of the patriarch [John the Merciful](/saints/john-the-merciful-of-alexandria), it says, a certain young monk appeared at Alexandria who, as soon as he came, drew up a list of all the harlots in the city. His ascetic labour was singular, and the Prologue says so plainly before describing it. By day he hired himself out for the heaviest work there was; by night he went to the houses of ill fame, gave the money he had earned to one of the women, and shut himself in a room with her until morning.

As soon as the door was shut he would beg the woman to lie down and sleep, and he would spend the whole night in the corner of the room praying to God for that sinner. So he kept her from sin for at least one night. The next night he went to another, the third to a third, and so on in order until he had been to them all, and then began again with the first. By his counsels many of them left their filthy trade: some married, some went into a monastery, some turned to honest work and honest earnings. And he forbade every one of these women to make known why he came to them.

For that silence he became a scandal to the whole of Alexandria. Men reviled him in the street, spat on him and even beat him; and he bore it all patiently, showing his virtue to God and hiding it from men. Only when he died was the whole of it learned. Many wonderworking healings began to happen at his grave, and people brought their sick to it from various parts. Spat upon by men, the Prologue ends, he was and remained glorified by the all-seeing God.

The Prologue’s entry gives no year, only the patriarchate it sets him in; it names no monastery and no birthplace, and Alexandria is the city he worked in rather than a place this folder locates him at. The Prologue is a book of selected lives and prints no hymn for any of its saints; the Romanian and Greek calendars were not read for him, and those two rows of his attestation say so.

The Russian calendar tells the same labour with a different set of particulars, and with the reckoning in it. He was a monk of the monastery of Serid, and he came to Alexandria in the days of the patriarch [John the Merciful](/saints/john-the-merciful-of-alexandria); he was sixty years old when he dared to take this work on himself, and he began by writing all the harlots of the city into his book of remembrance and praying for them by name. He worked from morning to evening and earned twelve copper coins a day. In the evening he bought himself one bean, which he did not eat before sunset, and gave the rest of the money to one of the women, saying: «Умоляю тебя, за эти деньги соблюди себя в чистоте эту ночь, не греши ни с кем» — I beg thee, for this money keep thyself pure this night and sin with no one. Then he shut himself in with her, and while she slept he stood the whole night at the psalms and went quietly away in the morning.

He bound each of them by an oath to keep the purpose of his visits secret, and when one of them broke it to defend him she fell into possession, after which the city no longer doubted that he was a sinner. Clergy who were scandalised denounced him to the patriarch, who would not believe them and shamed them with the story of the First Council of Nicaea, where the emperor burned the written accusations the bishops and clergy had brought against one another without reading them, saying that if he saw a bishop or a priest or a monk sinning with his own eyes he would cover him with his own garment so that no one should see his sin. Coming out of a brothel one day the saint was struck on the cheek by a young man going in, who shouted that the monk was shaming the name of Christ; and he answered that for him, a humble man, that youth would receive such a blow on the cheek that the whole of Alexandria would run together at his cry.

He settled at last in a small cell and died there in the night, and in that same hour a dreadful demon appeared to the young man, struck him on the cheek and cried: «Вот тебе удар от монаха Виталия» — here is thy blow from the monk Vitalis. The youth was possessed, rolled on the ground and tore his clothes and cried so loud that a crowd gathered; when he came to himself he ran to the cell begging mercy, knocked and got no answer, and when the door was broken in they found the saint fallen asleep on his knees before an icon with a scroll in his hands: «Мужи александрийские, не осуждайте прежде времени, пока не придет Господь, Праведный Судия» — men of Alexandria, judge not before the time, until the Lord come, the righteous Judge. The possessed woman touched his body and was healed and told the people everything; the women he had saved came and spoke of his mercy; and the patriarch, glad that he had not believed the slanderers, carried the body in state through the whole city and buried it with honour. From that time, the page ends, many Alexandrians made a covenant with themselves to judge nobody.
