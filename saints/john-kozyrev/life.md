# John (Kozyrev)

The Russian church calendar of the Сретенский monastery names him seventh of fifteen on 21 октября ст. ст., in a line of eleven priests, two deacons and two monks of 1937, and the name link behind his place and behind the eighth lead to one and the same life page: he and [Basil (Kozyrev)](/saints/basil-kozyrev) were brothers, and the calendar tells their story once. He was born on 16 January 1885 in the village of Pozharye in the Sandovo uyezd of Tver province, where his father Alexei Kozyrev was priest.

He finished the Tver seminary in 1904 and taught at the theological school of Krasny Kholm until 1910, then went home to Pozharye as a teacher in the village school. His father died in 1911, and on 9 October of that year he was ordained priest to the church of his own village. In 1929, when the authorities began taking inventories in the churches before the next seizure, he hid a chalice, a diskos, a communion spoon, two small plates and an altar Gospel in a silver binding; none of it could be kept, for they were found and taken. He was arrested the same year, charged with collecting donations for the church, and fined three hundred roubles.

In January 1934 the state police arrested a priest of the Entry church in Bezhetsk and showed him the informers' denunciations of himself and of his brother priests; what he was threatened with or promised cannot now be established, but he believed the investigators and confirmed what was put to him. On 16 February the two Kozyrev brothers were arrested, and something was evidently promised to them too if they would admit guilt. Fr John agreed with his investigator, and on 22 February signed the protocol: that he had been one of the active members of a counter-revolutionary group of Bezhetsk churchmen which for years had carried on activity against the existing order.

The protocol gave as its characteristic example an illegal gathering of 24 September 1933, when the clergy came together in the St Nicholas watch-hut after a solemn service for the tenth anniversary of the brothers' third brother, who had been made bishop of Bezhetsk in 1929. There, it said, the talk turned to the shortages in the country and the collective farms' failure to feed it; one of the company said the collective farmers lived so badly they would do better to run from the farms without looking back; and Fr John said that he knew the farmers of the village of Kucheli and that many of them were sitting without bread and in need of it. The bishop, the protocol has it, answered that if life was hard it must be looked on as God's chastisement, and the people called in sermons to patience and submission to Providence.

Fr Basil admitted nothing. The next day a doctor examined the two: Fr John had been gravely ill with pneumonia just before his arrest, the pleurisy had stayed with him, and anaemia and emaciation had set in because the prison hardly fed them; his brother was ill as well. But priests were arrested in order never to come back, and the court's medical expert wrote on each man's papers that he was fit for heavy physical labour. Two days later the troika sentenced both brothers and the priest who had confessed against them to three years in a corrective labour camp.

They were to go with the first convoy on 13 March, but the prison wagon did not arrive on the appointed day and they were sent back to Bezhetsk prison to wait for the next; from Vladivostok they were shipped to Kolyma, where the work and the life were such that the two brothers barely survived and the third priest died in the camp. At the end of October 1936 they came back to Bezhetsk and went into their church again, and at home they found their brother the bishop, who questioned them closely about the camp and the interrogation and learned that the investigator had made him the head of the alleged organisation. In July 1937 the bishop was appointed to the see of Barnaul; on the seventeenth the brothers, nearly all the clergy and many of the laity saw him onto his train, he blessed his flock for the last time, and he was arrested as soon as he arrived.

In September 1937 both brothers were arrested. The experience of 1934 had taught them that the investigation rests on lies, that nothing will be scrupled at, and that not a word of it can be believed, so that the only way left was to be as brave as they could, to admit and sign nothing false, and to pray for strength to bear it. Fr John was questioned on 21 September and asked why no correspondence had been found in his house; he said he had burned it, and when pressed, that his fears had begun when his brother the bishop was arrested the year before and he himself was called in, and that what he burned was the letters between him and his wife while he was in the camp.

He denied preaching sermons with counter-revolutionary commentary, saying they had been religious and nothing else, and denied spreading rumours of war. He did say, and would not take back, that he had explained the arrests of priests to the faithful as the Soviet power's wish to remove people it found undesirable from the elections to the Supreme Soviet, and that he explained his own arrest the same way; told that this was nothing but slander on the Stalin constitution, he agreed that by that definition it was. At the last interrogation, on 1 October, he said finally that he did not belong to any counter-revolutionary group and had carried on no agitation. On 1 November 1937 the NKVD troika for the Kalinin region sentenced both brothers to be shot, and they were shot on 3 November 1937. They were numbered among the saints at the Jubilee Council of Bishops in August 2000.
