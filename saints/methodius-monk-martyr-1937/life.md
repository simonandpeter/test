# Methodius (Ivanov)

Methodius — Nicholas Mikhailovich Ivanov in the world — was born in 1899
in the city of Simbirsk, a priest’s son. He finished the parish school and
the church school at Alatyr and entered the Simbirsk seminary, which he
had no time to finish because of the coup in Russia, completing his
education in a Soviet school; from 1920 he taught at Saransk, from 1921 at
the art and technical school at Penza, and the same year moved to Moscow
to teach in one of the higher schools. But teaching in a Soviet school on
a programme founded on godless ideology was incompatible with the
religious convictions of a deeply believing man, and in 1923 he left the
school and entered the Protection monastery in Moscow as a novice, where
the same year he was tonsured with the name Methodius and ordained
hierodeacon. There he came to know Gurias (Stepanov), bishop of Alatyr,
vicar of Simbirsk, once the monastery’s superior and now living in it
because he was forbidden to leave Moscow, with whom he kept close all his
life. In 1925 he was ordained hieromonk; in 1929 the monastery was closed
and he moved to the church of the Jerusalem icon of the Mother of God
beyond the Protection gate, and the same year the authorities arrested him
for counter-revolutionary activity. Asked his attitude to the Soviet
power, he answered: I am dissatisfied with the Soviet power because it has
closed the theological schools, forbidden spiritual literature, closed the
churches and in general treats the clergy and the faithful harshly. The
special board of the OGPU gave him three years’ exile, served in the
Pinega district of the Archangel region. Back in 1933, forbidden to live
in Moscow, he settled at Kashira and then in the village of Sukovo in the
Kashira district, where he served in the church and a small monastic
community gathered about him. In 1936 he was made igumen, and wrote of it
to Bishop Gurias, then in exile, who congratulated him and added: but
since, for want of monastic brethren, you have no one to be igumen over,
you will have to take up an intensified igumenship over yourself — that
is, sobriety over your heart and your whole life — which I wish you
sincerely and with prayer. Beginning the new merciless persecution,
Stalin ordered on 3 July 1937 that the heads of the regions send him
within five days lists of all to be arrested and shot “by administrative
handling of their cases through the troikas”; the clergy were among the
first marked for destruction, and on 26 July the Moscow regional NKVD
ordered the Kashira district office to arrest the igumen Methodius at
once, his counter-revolutionary activity and his ties with “the
anti-Soviet element among the churchmen” of the Kashira district and
Moscow to be proved only in the course of the inquiry. Arrested on 28
July, he was questioned about his acquaintances and named the nuns he had
met in exile who now served at the church, the nun with whom he had lived
for a time on his return, and a deacon serving at Kashira, and denied any
anti-Soviet talk. On 10 August the nun who owned the house at Kashira
testified that he had set up monastic order in her house, several nuns
living there and being required to keep the monastic rule; and the deacon
said that travelling with him to Moscow by train on 2 July and asking how
he lived, he had been told that Methodius was very well placed, the place
being remote and the people very believing, and that he was on very good
terms with the chairwoman of the village soviet, who had been wary at
first, thinking him a red, but had seen he was a true servant of God and
become one of them, no longer hiding that she was a believer herself, did
not like this power, and had to play her part for the time being — after
which the witness went on to the igumen’s counter-revolutionary and
anti-Soviet remarks. Questioned again about the anti-Soviet activity of
the group of nuns he knew — I know nothing of it, he answered. The
interrogations went on a month; at the last, on 31 August: you are
convicted by the inquiry of showing a counter-revolutionary, hostile
attitude to the leaders of the party and the members of the Soviet
government; do you admit it? — no, I do not; do you admit that until
recently you were of terrorist disposition? — no, that too I do not admit.
On 8 September 1937 the NKVD troika sentenced him to be shot, he was taken
to an NKVD prison in Moscow, and on 9 September 1937 — 27 August of the
old style, the day the Russian calendar keeps him — the igumen Methodius
was shot and buried in an unknown common grave at the Butovo range. The
life was compiled by the igumen Damascene (Orlovsky); the Russian
calendar keeps him also in the synaxis of the saints of Moscow and of the
new martyrs of Butovo.
