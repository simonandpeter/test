# Basiliscus of Amasea

Basiliscus was born in Amasea and was the nephew of Theodore Tiron. When Theodore had finished his passion in that city, his companions were left in prison for Christ: [Eutropius](/saints/eutropius-of-cappadocia) and [Cleonicus](/saints/cleonicus-of-cappadocia), brothers by the same mother and Cappadocians, and Basiliscus with them; and for love's sake all three called one another brothers. The governor Publius, who had tortured Theodore, had perished, and in his place came Asclepiodotus, a Phrygian, cruel in habit and godless in mind.

He had authority from the emperor Maximian to force the Christians to sacrifice and to destroy with torments those who would not. He had the record of Theodore's trial read out, learned that the three were still in prison, and — seeing the burnt temple of the goddess they called Hera, mother of the gods — sent soldiers to bring them. They stood before him with shining faces, and Basiliscus and Cleonicus refused the sacrifice as Eutropius had.

The three were stretched out by soldiers and beaten with rods, until an earthquake shook the court and the men doing the torturing begged to be spared the work. Back in the prison they prayed together, and there was thunder and an earthquake that shook the foundation of the pagan temple. They were tortured again with iron claws and with mustard mixed with salt upon their wounds, and at midnight the Lord appeared to them and promised them their rest.

In the morning, when the sentence was given, Basiliscus called out to the governor to pass sentence of death on him also; but he was not granted it. Eutropius and Cleonicus were crucified outside the city and gave up their souls on the third day of March, while Basiliscus was left shut in the prison and passed a long time in chains. Afterwards, when Asclepiodotus had perished and Agrippa was set in his place, Basiliscus too was put to the sword, on the twenty-second day of May, the day on which, the life says, his memory is honoured.

The Greek calendar keeps the three of them together on the same day, on one page, and tells the martyrdom at length. saint.gr puts them at Amasea of Pontus — not Cappadocia, which is the epithet this folder carries from doxologia — makes them soldiers and kinsmen of [Theodore the Recruit](/saints/theodore-the-recruit), and sets them in the years of the emperor Maximian, under the governor Asklepiodotos, who arrested them and tortured them hard; but the Lord and the great-martyr Theodore appeared to them and they were made whole. In prison the three young men lost neither their courage nor their faith, and by their preaching and their bearing they brought many of their fellow prisoners to the true faith, so that Asklepiodotos had them brought before him again. He began to flatter Cleonicus with promises of gifts and honours; the saint laughed, mocked the sickness of the idols, and while a sacrifice was being performed brought down the idol of Artemis by prayer.

They were scourged for it, and the Lord appeared again, with the great-martyr Theodore at his side, in the sight of the crowd of idolaters watching; the three were healed of their wounds on the spot and many who saw it were baptised, whom Asklepiodotos ordered beheaded. Blind with rage, he had boiling pitch poured over the three, and it fell instead on the executioners and burnt them, while the martyrs were untouched. Then he sentenced Eutropius and Cleonicus to the cross and Basiliscus to prison; the two spent their last night in prayer, and the Lord appeared to them once more to give them heart. On the third of March, the page says in the year 308, Eutropius and Cleonicus were crowned by their death on the cross, while Basiliscus stayed shut in prison and died there some years later — and for him saint.gr sends the reader to the twenty-second of May, where it keeps his memory again.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day with his companions, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović differs from both the other calendars about his end. It tells the three as one story: companions of [Theodore the Recruit](/saints/theodore-the-recruit), left in prison behind him, long unsentenced because the emperor's governor in Amasea was replaced, and brought out when a new and more inhuman governor arrived. All three were young men — [Eutropius](/saints/eutropius-of-cappadocia) and [Cleonicus](/saints/cleonicus-of-cappadocia) brothers born of one mother, and Basiliscus a kinsman of Theodore — and they answered the governor that as the Holy Trinity is indivisible, so were they indivisible in faith and inseparable in love.

The difference is in the sentence. The Prologue has the first two condemned to the cross and Basiliscus to the sword, and has the sword fall in the same breath: the two brothers crucified on two crosses, the third beheaded, and all three gone into the kingdom of joy where Theodore was waiting for them. It puts the whole of it in the year 308. Both of the other calendars read for him leave him shut in prison after that sentence and put his death later, on the twenty-second of May, which is the second day already noted on his rows here. The Prologue's year lies outside the interval recorded here as well, so neither the day nor the year was moved and the disagreement is what this paragraph records.

The same book keeps him a second time, on the Julian 22 May, in an entry of his own — and that entry tells his end as doxologia and the Greek page tell it, not as its own 3 March entry does. When the two brothers were crucified and gave up their spirits on the third of March, it says, Basiliscus was taken back to prison; and there, through a change of the emperor’s governors, he lay a long time. With tears he begged God not to deny him a martyr’s death, and after long prayer the Lord Jesus Himself appeared to him, promised to grant his wish, and sent him home to his village to greet his mother and his brothers.

Then the new governor Agrippa came and sent for him from the village at once; and on the road from the village to the city of Amasea the Lord worked great wonders through His martyr, so that many of the people believed in Christ. Agrippa ordered him to sacrifice to Apollo. Apollo means destroyer, Basiliscus answered, and by fervent prayer he crushed the idol to dust and burned the temple with fire from heaven. Frightened, Agrippa put it all down to sorcery, and gave the order that Basiliscus be cut down with the sword.

At that Agrippa went mad; and in his madness he went out to the place of execution, found a little of the martyr’s blood in the dust, bound it under his belt, and by it was made whole. Coming to himself, he was baptised. Afterwards a certain Marin, a citizen of Komani — which, the Prologue says, was the name of the place where Basiliscus was put to death — raised a church upon the martyr’s relics, in which many sick people found healing. The 22 May entry gives no year, so the 308 of the 3 March entry is left standing as the disagreement it is.

The Сретенский monastery’s calendar keeps the three together on the Julian 3 March — «Мчч. Евтропия , Клеоника и Василиска (ок. 308).» — and its register line for the day calls them «Мучеников Евтропия и дружины его, Клеоника и Василиска», Eutropius and his company. Its life is much the fullest of the four read for them. It puts the suffering at Amasea in Pontus in Asia Minor about 308, makes [Eutropius](/saints/eutropius-of-cappadocia) and [Cleonicus](/saints/cleonicus-of-cappadocia) brothers, and makes Basiliscus the nephew of the great martyr Theodore the soldier, whose memory it puts on 17 February; the three were friends.

After Theodore’s martyrdom they were in prison, and by their preaching brought many of the pagans shut up with them to the faith. Publius, who had tortured Theodore, perished ingloriously, struck down by the wrath of God, and Asclepiodotus was made governor in his place, no gentler than his predecessor. Learning that the friends of the martyr were still in the prison, he had them brought before him, and before the new governor they confessed Christ as firmly as before. They were beaten without mercy until their bodies were one wound.

Under the torture Eutropius prayed aloud: «Даждь, Господи, нам терпения совершенного в ранах сих ради венца мученического» — give us, Lord, perfect patience in these wounds for the martyr’s crown, and come to our help as you came to your servant Theodore. The Lord himself appeared to them with angels, and Theodore with them, saying that the Saviour had come to their help so that they might be assured of eternal life. The soldiers and many of the bystanders were counted worthy to see it too, and began begging the governor to stop the torture; seeing the crowd stirred and ready to believe, he had the martyrs taken away.

Then he invited Eutropius to dinner and proposed that he should sacrifice to the gods in public and stay a Christian in his soul, and Eutropius refused him. Next day they were brought into the pagan temple to be forced to sacrifice, and Eutropius prayed that the whole frenzy of paganism might be destroyed and that on that spot Christians might offer the bloodless Sacrifice to the true God. The last word of the prayer had barely sounded when an earthquake began; the walls of the temple fell and the image of Artemis broke with them, and above the noise a voice came: your prayer is heard, and on this place there shall be a house of prayer for Christians.

When it was over the governor, hardly recovered from his fright, had tall wooden stakes sunk in the ground, the martyrs tied to them and boiling pitch poured over them; and the pitch ran off their bodies as water off marble and scalded the torturers instead. The onlookers were appalled, and the governor in his obstinacy ordered their bodies torn with iron hooks and their wounds galled with mustard mixed with salt and vinegar, and they bore that too with a wonderful steadiness. The last night before the execution they spent in prayer, and the Lord appeared to them again and strengthened them.

On the morning of 3 March [Eutropius](/saints/eutropius-of-cappadocia) and [Cleonicus](/saints/cleonicus-of-cappadocia) were crucified, and Basiliscus was left in prison; on 22 May he was beheaded in the town of Comana and his body thrown into the river, and the Christians found his remains and buried them in a ploughed field, and a church was raised in his name at Comana afterwards. That second day is this calendar’s own and this corpus holds the one the day page gave. The year is where the hosts part and both readings are now carried: doxologia’s reign of 286 to 305 and this calendar’s «ок. 308». The day prints no hymn under any of the three names, and the register line is all it adds besides.
