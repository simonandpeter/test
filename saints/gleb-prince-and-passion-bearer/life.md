# Gleb

Gleb, David in holy baptism, is one of the first Russian martyrs, the
passion-bearers; he suffered with his brother prince Boris, Roman in
baptism. After Boris’s murder Svyatopolk the Accursed sent a messenger to
the younger brother Gleb with a false report that their father, the grand
prince Vladimir — already dead — was ill, meaning to kill by treachery a
possible claimant to the throne of Kyiv. Deceived, Gleb hurried towards
Kyiv with a small retinue; a warning from his brother Yaroslav, which
reached him near Smolensk, did not stop the saint, who expected no such
villainy from his brother.

Not far from Smolensk the murderers overtook Gleb’s boat; he did not
resist, only meekly begged them to spare his still young life, and at the
murderers’ order his own cook cut his throat. The prince’s body was buried
in a lonely place near Smolensk, between two logs, in a plain wooden
coffin, in 1015. In 1019–1020 his brother Yaroslav sought out the grave,
and the body, found incorrupt, was carried to Vyshgorod near Kyiv and
buried beside Boris; later the brothers’ relics were translated to the
church of Basil the Great, where many wonders were worked at their tomb,
and Metropolitan John of Kyiv composed their service and set their joint
feast on 24 July, kept since the first half of the eleventh century.

The Russian Church has honoured the passion-bearing brothers from of old as
constant helpers of their native land in its hardest trials: before the
battle of the Neva in 1240 Boris and Gleb appeared in a vision to one of
the soldiers of Alexander Nevsky and helped the Russians in the fighting,
the chronicles are full of tales of the graces witnessed at their tomb and
of the victories won with their help, and many churches and monasteries in
every corner of Russia have been raised in their honour. The Russian
calendar keeps Gleb alone on 5 September (Julian), the day of his death,
with a troparion and kontakion, and the brothers together on 2 May, the
translation, and 24 July.

The Greek calendar keeps him on 24 July, and keeps him with his brother. saint.gr's line for the day reads «Άγιοι Μπορίσος και Γλιέβος οι Μάρτυρες», transcribing both Slavonic names into Greek, and its whole notice says that they were sons of Saint [Vladimir](/saints/vladimir-the-great), whom it sends the reader to look up on 15 July, and that by holy baptism they were renamed Romanos and David — the second being the baptismal name this folder already carries from the Russian calendar. We have no further information about the life of these saints, the page adds, and it gives no year, no place, no manner of death and no hymn; it calls the two of them martyrs and not princes. His brother [Boris](/saints/boris-son-of-vladimir) comes into the corpus from this same Greek line, the Russian calendar having kept Gleb alone on 5 September.

The Serbian church keeps the two brothers on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović gives them in six sentences. They were sons of the great prince [Vladimir](/saints/vladimir-the-great), the baptiser of the Russian people; before his own baptism Vladimir had many wives and children by them, and Boris and Gleb were brothers by one mother. Before his death Vladimir divided the state among all his sons.

But Svyatopolk, his eldest son, prince of Kyiv, wanted to take the portions meant for Boris and Gleb as well, and so sent men who killed Boris in one place and Gleb in another. The two brothers were uncommonly devout and in everything pleasing to God, and they met their death with prayer and with the lifting up of their hearts to God. Their bodies remained incorrupt and fragrant and were buried in the city of Vyshgorod, from which to this day there comes a grace-filled power that heals men of various sicknesses and torments.

**The Prologue gives no year for either of them**, and none was taken; it calls them both мученици, martyrs, which is the rank the Serbian row records.
