# John (Karabanov)

John was born in 1885 in the village of Gerasimovo in the Shablykino
district of the Karachev county of Oryol province, the son of the peasant
Theodore Karabanov. Finishing the Karachev teachers’ seminary in 1905 he
became a people’s teacher, teacher and head of the school of the ministry
of education in the village of Znamenskoye in the Bolkhov district;
mobilised in 1915 he served in the 203rd infantry regiment, was sent on
1 January 1916 to the Alexander military school and on finishing it, on
1 May 1916, made a platoon commander, then sent as a company commander to
the front near Dvinsk. Demobilised in 1917 he returned home and taught;
in 1918 he was sent to Oryol to the courses training teachers for the
higher primary schools, in 1919 called into the Red Army, where he
commanded a platoon and a company of the 2nd engineer labour battalion at
Oryol, Nikolaev and Ochakov, and demobilised in 1921 taught again. In
1922 he was ordained priest for the church of the village of Gnezdilovo
in the Znamenskoye district of Oryol province, and after a time sent to
the church of Khotkovo in the Shablykino district. At the end of the
twenties the authorities resolved to close the churches on the pretext
that they hindered the founding of collective farms, and the OGPU
explained the need for repression thus: the Bryansk operative sector had
information that at Khotkovo the priest Karabanov and the kulaks had
organised around themselves a group of persons socially alien to the
Soviet state — kulaks and former traders — who, gathering at the priest’s
flat, criticised the measures of the Soviet power and planned their
actions to wreck them, carrying on systematic counter-revolutionary
activity; the group had set itself the task of irreconcilable struggle
with the “godless Soviet power” and of bringing the mass of the
peasantry, the middle and poor peasants, under its influence by using
religious prejudice, and its concrete counter-revolutionary activity
came down to stirring discontent and resistance to the Soviet power’s
measures, especially on religious questions. On 6 January 1932 the OGPU
arrested him and the members of the church council. Questioned the next
day he said: throughout my life under the existing Soviet power — under
which as a people’s teacher and as a platoon and company commander in the
Red Army I defended the interests of the proletariat with mind, word and
deed, with breast and bayonet, in the wars of 1919–1921 against Denikin
and Wrangel — and in my present position too I serve the people and their
welfare and further their prosperity in the strengthening and extension
of the socialist order of the Soviet power; living among a farming people
whose interests are bound up with the individual use of land and the
individual farm, I have explained as I could the advantage of
collectivisation, but the people, not yet seeing well-run collective
farms, do not venture in the mass at Khotkovo to join one — the absence to
this day of well-run collective farms round Khotkovo is the main brake;
middle and poor peasants alike want to see with their own eyes a well-run
collective farm about them, and then, the Khotkovo people say, we shall
all go into it; and those who will not join say, first, that there is no
organised farm at Khotkovo yet and they see no good order in the farms
round about, and second, let those who want to organise a collective farm
show by experience a good well-ordered life for the working peasant, and
then we shall join. At the second and last interrogation, on 21 January,
he said: to the charge of heading a counter-revolutionary church-kulak
group and carrying on systematic agitation against the economic and
political campaigns I admit myself guilty in part, and I will add in what
exactly. Sometimes in the church lodge, my flat, we gathered to discuss
the repair of the church and the fence and other economic needs of the
church — I, the warden, the chairman of the church council, the psalmist,
a nun and I do not now remember who else; we never held a sitting of the
church council; at such gatherings we sometimes discussed the tax on the
church and political questions, such as collective farming, and they
were held in my flat, in the warden’s house and in the church after the
service, though I cannot now name the times and places. On the repairs we
said that materials were hard to get now, there were almost none anywhere
and what there was cost very dear, and a way out must be found or the
church might be closed; what was said on the collective-farm question I
cannot at all recall. Our parish takes in the villages of Meshkovo,
Okolenki, Bashkarevo and Khotkovo; on the religious feasts I went round
them with molebens, with the warden, the psalmist, the nun and the
chairman of the council, and in the houses we touched no political talk
and spoke nowhere of collective farming or other measures of the Soviet
power. In church after the service the warden more than once announced
the receipt of new taxes — I do not remember his exact words; I myself
said nothing about the tax. Sometimes believers asked me about joining the
collective farm, in the houses where I was with the molebens and in the
lodge, and I answered them: you should join. The concrete anti-Soviet
talk put to me did not happen on my part; of the opinions and talk of
those arrested with me about the power’s measures I can say nothing. On
28 February 1932 the OGPU troika sentenced him to eight years in a
concentration camp, and he was sent to the Temnikov labour camp in
Mordovia. In 1937 new cases were opened against the clergy in the camps:
on 25 August at the settlement of Yavas, seat of the third section of the
Temnikov camps, the NKVD drew up a memorandum on him from the testimony of
false witnesses, without calling the accused to a single interrogation —
only before the shooting was he told what he had been sentenced to. On 30
August the NKVD troika sentenced him to be shot, and on 5 September 1937 —
23 August of the old style, the day the Russian calendar keeps him — he
was shot and buried in an unknown common grave. The life was compiled by
the igumen Damascene (Orlovsky); the Russian calendar keeps him also in
the synaxis of the saints of Oryol.
