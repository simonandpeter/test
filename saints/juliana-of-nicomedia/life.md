# Juliana of Nicomedia

Nicomedia, the great city of Bithynia, was much given to the service of idols in the days when Diocletian reigned in the west and Maximian in the east. There lived in it a rich and honoured man named African, a strong adherent of the Greek religion, whose daughter Juliana grew up beautiful, wise and well-mannered; he betrothed her to one Eleusius of the imperial retinue, and put off the wedding to a better time. Hearing the preaching of the Gospel, she believed in secret, and learned to know the Creator from his creation.

When the wedding drew near she sent word to Eleusius that she would not go to him unless he were first made prefect of that country, thinking the office out of his reach and the marriage so avoided. He got it from the emperor Maximian, at length and at great cost, and sent to tell her to be ready. Then she said openly what she had long hidden: that unless he worshipped her God and served the Lord Jesus Christ, he must look for another wife, for she would not have an unbelieving husband.

Her father questioned her first gently and then in fury, swearing by Apollo and Artemis to give her body to the beasts and the dogs; she asked why he delayed. He beat her, dragged her by the hair and trampled her, and then handed her over to Eleusius to do as he liked. Sitting in judgement, the prefect was softened at the sight of her and promised that if she would only marry him she need not sacrifice; she answered that neither words nor torments nor death would join her to him until he were baptised.

She was stretched between four posts and beaten by six soldiers, hung up by the hair until the skin of her head was lifted, burned in the sides with red-hot irons and pierced through with a sharpened iron, and thrown half-dead into prison. There, praying, she was visited by the enemy in the shape of an angel of light, who urged her to sacrifice; a voice from heaven gave her power over him, her chains fell off, and she seized him and made him say who he was and what he had done from Eve and Cain onwards, and beat him through the night.

In the morning she came out whole and radiant, dragged the demon after her and threw him on a rubbish heap. Told of the everlasting fire, the tyrant heated a furnace and cast her in; she stood unharmed, and her few tears became two rivers that put the fire out. At that five hundred men and a hundred and thirty women of Nicomedia confessed Christ, and were all beheaded on the spot. She was thrown naked into a cauldron of molten lead, which became a warm bath to her, while the fire consumed the torturers standing round and the cauldron broke.

The prefect, shamed by a girl, tore his hair and reviled his own gods, and condemned her to the sword. She went to it as to a wedding, and her head was struck off. A Roman woman named Sophia, who happened to be in Nicomedia, carried the body back to Rome, laid it in her house and in time built a fine church for it. Eleusius was shipwrecked, cast on an island and eaten by beasts. The life closes with her years: betrothed in her ninth, she became Christ's bride by her own blood in her eighteenth.
