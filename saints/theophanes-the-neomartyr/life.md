# Theophanes of Zapanti

saint.gr says he was born in the village of Zapanti, now Kalovryse, near Kalamata in Messenia, of devout parents, Nicholas and Kyro. As a young man in Constantinople, learning the tailor's trade under a hard master, Theodore — as he was called in the world — who was remarkable for his bodily beauty, was led astray, denied his Christian faith and embraced Islam. The Muslims were glad of his change of religion and cultivated him with flatteries and honours, teaching him Turkish and Arabic in the royal palaces for six years. His much reading brought him to Christ: he remembered the faith he had denied, and with remorse and tears resolved to return.

He wandered a long while looking for a man who could help him to forgiveness, and came to Venice, where the wise and holy metropolitan of Philadelphia, Gabriel Severos, held the see. That hierarch taught him, admonished him, upheld him and tonsured him a monk under the name Theophanes, and urged him towards martyrdom, telling him that the completing of his repentance would be to shed his blood for the Christ he had denied. Strengthened, he returned to his own country to bear witness.

He wandered much again before he reached the martyrdom he wanted. From Constantinople, where he could not manage it, he went to Athens and after three days' prayer presented himself boldly to the judge there, confessing his faith publicly and his earlier wrong denial of it; he achieved nothing and the judge drove him off. Then he went to Euboea and to Larissa, provoking the judges to send him to his martyrdom; at Larissa the hard and savage judge had him scourged six hundred times, and out of his wounds the saint thanked God, reckoning that another suffered and not he. He was healed by prayer and left for the Holy Mountain.

On Athos he met virtuous monks and spiritual fathers who strengthened and armed him with their prayers, and stayed at Vatopedi, where he met the learned hierodeacon Synesios, who was to be his biographer and hymnographer. Fortified, he went back to Constantinople for the fulfilling of his purpose, and there his meeting with a new spiritual trainer, Euthymios, was important: that man prepared him so that he should not flinch or give out. He received the immaculate mysteries, and after a night of warm and tearful prayer went boldly to the court to confess the crucified and risen Christ as the only true God.

He confessed Christ with the whole strength of his soul and declared his repentance for his turning aside to the Muslim religion. The angry judge had him imprisoned to think out a better punishment, and the gaolers mocked him and beat him on the way. Called up again, in case he had been drunk or out of his mind, the saint argued with abundant reason and eloquence that he had sinned dreadfully in being led away by impiety, that he rejoiced at having returned to Christ, and that no punishment dismayed him; the judge ordered seven hundred lashes and close keeping in prison.

In that second imprisonment his keepers tortured him continually and mocked him, challenging him to show them a wonder. The saint prayed undisturbed for three hours to Christ the sun of righteousness and to the Mother of God, and when he said the amen of his prayer there was a great earthquake: the foundations of the prison were shaken, and though it was night the whole place shone with a light from above heaven. The saint stood praying, loosed from his chains, shining and glad, glorifying God; and his tormentors, turned from wild beasts into tame sheep, begged his forgiveness and kissed his feet, and some of them, hearing his teaching, believed in Christ.

When the rulers heard of it they called him to court again, and he came without fear and held to the Christian faith as the only true one, undoing the unwise arguments of the judges. So they resolved to torture him dreadfully and put him to death. They impaled him, flayed strips from his chest and his back, set him on a mule and paraded him through the streets of Constantinople for a show, then threw him onto hooks that went through his body and tore him to pieces.

Through all of it the saint stayed undisturbed and prayed warmly, thanking the Holy Trinity for granting him what he longed for and asking that a sign be shown to the unbelieving. After his prayer a wholly white bird like a dove came down out of heaven, at the sight of which the saint was filled with joy while all who were there were astonished; the Turks said to each other that Christ, preached and glorified by the martyr, is truly God. The bird stayed three hours and went away. As it grew dark the saint cried out, as Christ did on the cross, I thirst; and his executioners seized on it to taunt him — become as we are and we shall give you water — and he answered that Christ refreshed him and that he thirsted only for his own salvation.

In the night that followed came heavy thunder and lightning, and a heavenly light wrapped the worn-out body of the glorious neomartyr; and the Turks standing by declared that the Christians' piety alone is purely true.

Then the executioners, afraid the people would be converted, took up whatever sharp thing they found and began to flay his face and put out his eyes; and the saint breathed out and gave up his spirit to his Maker. Christians then gave money and gifts to the executioners and took the martyr's relics and gathered his blood with reverence; and these became a source of wonders to the lame, the leprous, the possessed and the long and incurably sick. Of the executioners who had led the butchery, some were blinded, some went mad and drowned themselves in the sea, some had their hands wither; others repented, called on the saint's help, and became witnesses and heralds of his wonders.
