# Acacius of Melitene

Melitene, the city of Armenia, raised him. His parents were faithful people and childless, and they asked God for him with prayer and fasting; when he was born and had been taught his letters, they brought him to the bishop of that city and gave him over to the service of God, as they had promised when they asked for him. The bishop then was Otreius, one of the hundred and fifty fathers of the second Ecumenical Council, who had stood there against Macedonius, the adversary of the Holy Spirit, together with [Gregory the Theologian](/saints/gregory-the-theologian) and the other defenders of the faith.

Otreius saw the gift of God in the child and made him a cleric, and Acacius went from strength to strength, until he was a teacher of old men as well as of boys, in word and in the manner of his life; doxologia says that Euthymius the Great learned his letters from him in his childhood. When Otreius departed to the Lord, Acacius was chosen for his seat with one voice, and was set, the page says, like a bright lamp upon a golden stand.

Most of the life is a life of miracles. In a year of drought and famine he went out with the hungry people to the church of the Great Martyr Eustathius beyond the walls, built an altar in the open field, and, beginning the bloodless offering, did not mix water into the cup as the custom is, but asked God to mix it himself from above; rain fell at once, and watered the dry ground as well as the chalice. A river that had broken its banks and was drowning the villages he shut back within them, laying down a stone and forbidding the water to pass it.

He set himself to cleanse the pagan place called Miasini and began a church there to the Mother of God, against the hard resistance of the servants of the idols. When the roof of another church began to fall during the liturgy, it hung in the air until he had finished and led his clergy out, and fell only after, hurting no one. Swallows whose noise drowned his preaching he silenced, and they left their nests. He made his own house before the city into a hospice for the poor and the sick, served them himself, and drove the flies from their sores with a prayer; he gave a spring out of dry rock, and quieted the frogs of a lake and afterwards loosed them again, but low.

At the third Ecumenical Council at Ephesus, in the reign of Theodosius the Younger, he stood with Cyril patriarch of Alexandria and the other fathers against Nestorius and put him under anathema. The page says he was loved by the fathers and much honoured by the emperor, that he shepherded the church of Christ well and worked many miracles, and that when he departed he was laid beside the tomb of the martyr [Polyeuctus](/saints/polyeuctus-of-melitene). It gives no year for his birth or for his death.

The Greek calendar keeps a Confessor Acacius, bishop of Melitene, on 31 March rather than the Romanian 17 April, and gives him a controversialist's portrait. He lived in the fifth century, saint.gr says, and was distinguished greatly for his virtues, his learning and his Orthodox zeal. When the heresy of Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople, troubled the Church, Acacius was marked out for his careful and systematic work in keeping his flock clear of that heretical error; and wishing to strike at it more widely and to help towards the Church's general rejection of it, he wrote against Nestorius. He was also a most able speaker and a teacher of the people, and one homily of his survives, which was delivered at Ephesus. The page's own year for his birth, «περί το 431 μ.Χ.», about 431, cannot be reconciled with a homily at Ephesus, where the council met in that same year; the figure is recorded as the page's with the contradiction stated, and no year is invented to replace it. The page closes with a caution of its own: S. Eustratiades, it says, claims that there was also an Acacius II, bishop of Melitene, and puts his feast on 18 April.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Romanian calendar's day, 17 April, and gives him a fuller notice than either of the other two pages. He struggled, it says, in the city in which he was also born, Melitene in Armenia. Blessed Otrius, bishop of that city, who had taken part in the Second Ecumenical Council, ordained him presbyter, and on Otrius's death Acacius became bishop in his place. He took part in the Third Ecumenical Council at Ephesus, which condemned Nestorius's blasphemy against the Mother of God, and there, together with [Cyril of Alexandria](/saints/cyril-of-alexandria), he was greatly zealous for the purity of the Orthodox faith. He had great grace from God and worked many wonders; and after a long and zealous service of God he reposed peacefully in the year 435.

That year will not stand beside the year this folder already held. saint.gr says he was born about 431, which the Greek page itself cannot reconcile with the council of 431 it has him preach at, and a death in 435 makes the difficulty plainer rather than mending it. Both figures are kept as the pages print them and nothing is chosen between them: the Prologue's 435 is recorded as the death, the Greek page's about 431 stays as the birth, and the note on each says what the other says.

**The Russian calendar's life is the fullest read for this folder, and almost all of it is new here.** He was born at the Armenian city of Melitene to a devout family, it says; his parents were long childless and promised, praying for a child, to give him to God, and so the boy was handed to Otrius, the bishop of Melitene, for the service of the Church. Otrius was a firm champion of Orthodoxy, who at the Second Ecumenical Council in 381, when the heresy of Macedonius arose, defended the teaching that the Holy Spirit is the third person of the consubstantial and undivided Trinity. He brought Acacius up with love, made him a reader, then ordained him deacon and priest; and Acacius taught grown people and children the holy Scriptures and the Orthodox confession of the faith. Among his pupils was [Euthymius the Great](/saints/euthymius-the-great).

After Otrius died he was raised to the see of Melitene by common consent, and governed his diocese wisely; and by firm faith, humility and ascetic labour he received the gift of wonderworking. Once, in a rainless summer, he was serving the divine Liturgy in the open field and the wine in the holy chalice was mixed with rain that fell suddenly and watered the whole earth. At his prayer a mountain river in flood went no further than a stone he had laid on the bank. On one of the islands of the river Azar he built a church in honour of the Mother of God against the opposition of the pagans; and the builders, from carelessness or from malice, set the vault up unsoundly, so that during the Liturgy it sagged and was about to fall. The people rushed out of the church in terror, but he stopped them with a cry — the Lord is the defender of my life, of whom shall I be afraid? — and the vault halted and hung as it were in the air, and fell only when the service was over and he had been the last to leave, hurting nobody; and afterwards the church was built again.

He took part in the Third Ecumenical Council in 431 and defended the Orthodox confession of the two natures of the Saviour, the divine and the human, and of his seedless birth from the holy Virgin Mother of God; and he departed to the Lord in peace about the year 435.
