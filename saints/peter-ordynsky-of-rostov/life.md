# Peter Ordynsky of Rostov

The Greek calendar keeps him on 30 Ιουνίου and knows nothing about him. saint.gr's heading is «Άγιος Πέτρος ο πρίγκιπας του Ορντύνσκϊυ, Ροστώβ (; - 1290)» — Peter the prince of the Horde, of Rostov, died 1290 — and the body of the page is the one sentence «Δεν έχουμε λεπτομέρειες για τον βίο του Αγίου», we have no details of the saint's life. The year of death and the two place-words are therefore everything this source gives.

What those words carry is a Russian saint read into a Greek calendar: Rostov is the city on the lake north-east of Moscow, and «Ορντύνσκϊυ» is the Russian epithet of the Horde, borne by a prince who came out of the Tatar Horde to the Russian church. The page neither tells that story nor vouches for it, so nothing of it is recorded here beyond the words it prints. His Russian calendar is the source that has his life, and it is read further down.

The Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović has the life this folder was waiting for, and it is in a Serbian book rather than the Russian one. He was a Tatar by origin, it says, and a nephew of the Tatar khan Berkai. He heard words about salvation from [Cyril II of Rostov](/saints/cyril-ii-of-rostov), the bishop of that city, and those words stuck fast to his heart; and when he further saw the wonderful healing of Berkai’s son by the bishop’s prayer, he left the Golden Horde in secret and fled to Rostov, where he was baptised and gave himself with all his soul and heart to the ascetic life and to the study of the devout faith.

Once he spent a night on the shore of a lake, and in his sleep the apostles Peter and Paul appeared to him and ordered him to build a church in their name on that very spot, and gave him besides the money needed for it. Peter did indeed build a beautiful temple there, and in it he was himself tonsured in his old age, after the death of his wife. He ended peacefully in deep old age on the twenty-ninth of June 1290, and his church became and remained a monastery, the one called Petrovsky.

The year is the one the Greek heading already gave this folder. The day is not: this book puts his repose on 29 June and keeps his memory on 30 June, as the Greek calendar does, and the corpus records the feast and not the day of a death.

The Russian calendar has now been read, and it tells how he came. He was a nephew of Bergai, khan of the Golden Horde. In 1253 [Cyril](/saints/cyril-ii-of-rostov), bishop of Rostov, went to the Horde on his diocese’s business and spoke before the khan of the wonders worked at the relics of an earlier hierarch of his city, and the khan’s young nephew was among those who heard him. When the khan’s own son fell ill soon after, he called the bishop back, and the boy was healed at his prayers; the khan sent him home richly gifted, and on the road the nephew overtook him and begged to be taken to Rostov with him.

At Rostov he was baptised and married, and he loved silence, the thought of God and prayer. The church he built by Lake Nero after the apostles appeared to him is the one the Serbian account above describes, and this page adds that he took the monastic habit in it after his wife’s death, not long before his own end in deep old age in 1290. It gives no day for that death, where the Serbian book gives 29 June. His veneration began in the fourteenth century, and the Council of 1547 appointed it to be kept everywhere.
