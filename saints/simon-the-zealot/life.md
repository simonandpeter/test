# Simon the Zealot

This apostle was of Cana of Galilee by descent, known to our Lord Jesus Christ and to his most pure Mother, since Cana is not far from Nazareth. When Simon held his lawful wedding, Jesus was called there with his disciples, and when the wine ran short the Lord turned the water into wine. Seeing such a wonder, the bridegroom at once believed that the Lord was the true God, and leaving his wedding and his house he followed him eagerly; for which he was called "the Zealot", that is, the ardent one.

He was kindled with so great a divine love for Christ, the life says, that he overlooked his bride and all worldly things for the love of God, and made his own soul a bride to the deathless Bridegroom, being numbered among the disciples of Christ and being one of the twelve apostles.

After he had received the Most Holy Spirit with the other apostles, who came down in the form of tongues of fire, he went over into Mauritania and into Africa, preaching Christ and enduring for him with a burning zeal. Afterwards, being in Britain and enlightening many by the word of the Gospel, he was crucified by the unbelievers and ended his life by a suffering of one form with Christ's. The page gives no year.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and adds where he went. saint.gr says he was one of the twelve disciples of the Lord and the brother of Jude Lebbaeus, and that he was called the Zealot because of the warmth and the zeal with which he preached the word of the Gospel. He came of Cana of Galilee, the page says, and is known as Simon the Cananite; and then it adds, in its own parenthesis, «ή Ναθαναήλ ή Βαρθολομαίος - υιός του Θολομαίου», or Nathanael, or Bartholomew, son of Tholomaios. Those are the page's identifications and they are set down here as its words and not as findings: this corpus keeps Bartholomew and Nathanael in folders of their own, and whether the three names are one man is not a thing a calendar's parenthesis settles.

After the Ascension of Christ and the grace of Pentecost, the page goes on, Simon gave himself to a wide apostolic work. He taught the Gospel in Persia, where, having pulled down the myth of the many gods, he made plain to a multitude of people the light of the truth of the Gospel. Then he went over to Africa, where he preached the life-giving Word of the true God and Saviour from Egypt as far as Mauretania. The last place of his wonderful apostolic labour was Britain; and there, after teaching the Christian faith to the people, he was seized by the idolaters, tortured, and put to death on a cross. The page gives no year for any of it, and adds that he is commemorated on the twenty-second of April as well.

The Ohrid Prologue, read for the Julian 10 May, calls him one of the twelve great apostles and makes him by birth a man of Cana of Galilee — and then says something no other book read for this folder says. The Lord Jesus came to his wedding, with his Mother and with the disciples; when the wine ran out the Lord turned the water into wine; and seeing that wonder Simon the bridegroom left his house, and his parents, and his bride, and went after Christ. The epithet it glosses in passing: Zealot means the zealous one, and he was called the Zealous for his great and fiery zeal towards the Saviour and his Gospel, which is the reason the Greek page gives too.

After receiving the Holy Spirit, this book says, he went to preach the Gospel in Mauretania in Africa; and having brought many to the faith of Christ he was tortured and at the last crucified on a cross like his Lord, who had prepared for him a crown of glory in the immortal kingdom. The Greek page runs the journey further — Persia first, then Africa from Egypt to Mauretania, and last Britain, where it puts the arrest and the cross. The two books agree on the preaching in Mauretania and on the manner of his death and disagree about the country he died in, and neither reading is chosen here. Neither gives a year for anything.
