# Zenobius (Mazhuga)

Zenobius — Zachary Ioakimovich Mazhuga — metropolitan of the Georgian
Church, began his spiritual life at the Glinsk hermitage, near which he
was born, at Glukhov, on 14 September 1896. He lost his father at three
and his mother at eleven and lived with relatives, poor, but keeping the
piety his mother had planted; his married sister soon placed him in the
tailors’ workshop at the hermitage, to his great joy, for he had run to
the monastery before and knew monks who pitied the orphan, and at sixteen
he was entered as a novice. Newcomers were put first in the guesthouse for
half a year, to learn to serve people, then in the laundry for a year or
two, to fear no labour, and then, by their diligence, in the kitchen, the
bakery, the bindery or the tailors’ shop; Zachary did well at the first
obediences and badly at the trades, his superiors complained of him to
the abbot, he was moved from place to place and prayed with all his might
for fear of being sent home, and only the strict schemamonk Gerasim, to
whom he was entrusted, pitied him — testing him by driving him off and
threatening a beating, until the boy declared he would not leave whatever
the elder did to him; from then he was Gerasim’s spiritual son, taught
long prayer, strict fasting and restraint of speech, and put to mind the
horses, which he feared and then came to love. In 1916 he was taken for
the army; the elder saw him off, foretelling that he would return to the
hermitage and that they would not meet again, and told him to pray and
trust God’s will in everything. He begged God for a post where he need
kill no one, and was put in an escort company and never fought; holding
the line in the Pinsk marshes gave him the eczema and the thrombophlebitis
that tormented him all his life. Demobilised, he returned to Glinsk and
was tonsured on the Annunciation of 1917 with the name Zenobius, for the
hieromartyr Zenobius of Aegae — Gerasim had died, as he foretold — and
given to the hieroschemamonk Nicholas; the abbot Nectarius sent him with
grain to the monastery mill at Putivl, forty kilometres off on the Seim,
and back with flour, through the robberies and killings of the civil war,
and the carts came through whole, the abbot praying for him and he for
himself. Once he was called to the abbot, sat down and had his hair cut
without a word; sent to wait; called in the evening, had his monastic
clothes taken off and a suit fitted, all in silence; told to harness the
horses, and then that he was trusted with a hard thing — to carry a man
away secretly, hence the change of look, along roads where every band
robbed. The man was a bishop, who said at parting: you have saved a
bishop, so you will be a bishop — fulfilled thirty-five years later.

When the hermitage was closed in 1922 and soon wrecked he took one of its
antimensia, on which the Liturgy was later served under the open sky, and
went to Georgia, where it was quieter: he was ordained hierodeacon at the
Dormition monastery of Dranda and hieromonk in 1925 by Bishop Nicon of
Sukhumi; when that monastery too was closed in 1927 he served at the
church of St George and then of St Nicholas in Sukhumi, tried a small
skete in the hills, and, driven from there too, lived among the Greek
villages, moving from place to place, sewing for the villagers for
nothing and fed and lodged by them, and learning to speak Greek freely.
One night, warned of a check, he went into the forest so as not to
compromise his host, made a fire and prayed to St Nicholas, whose spring
feast it was, and heard heavy steps crashing through the bushes — a bear;
he fed the fire and prayed harder and at last fell asleep, and the host’s
boy woke him in the morning with milk; the bushes were trampled in a ring
round his shelter, and the host, a hunter, who had not believed him, saw
the tracks and said, yes, Father Zenobius, St Nicholas saved you — a
Himalayan bear never gives up its den. Finding no way to serve, he went to
Rostov-on-Don, to the church of St Sophia, and in 1936 was arrested. In
the remand prison he caught malaria, which was God’s mercy: a young doctor
argued that such a patient could not be sent to Central Asia, so he went
to the Urals while fourteen clergy went to Tashkent — and he grieved to be
left out, until he learned, on the White Sea canal, that all who went to
Tashkent had died; at the Rostov transit prison he had the comfort of a
brief meeting with the future Glinsk elders
[Seraphim (Romantsov)](/saints/seraphim-romantsov) and
[Andronicus (Lukash)](/saints/andronicus-lukash). Arriving at the camp he
gave away all he had, and so did a companion; a mocker asked what they
would eat tomorrow, and that evening their things lay on their bunks while
the mocker’s, mattress and all, were stolen. Priests were put with the
criminals; one night a young man was brought in with nowhere to sleep, and
he and his companion cut their blankets and gave him half — in the morning
he said, Father, while I am here no one will touch you, and, a chief of
the criminal world, he protected them. He knew the common services by
heart, confessed all who asked and prayed with the willing, though he loved
best to pray alone; once, marching back in fifty degrees of frost, last in
the column as the shortest, he saw a freshly picked bunch of grapes in the
snow, picked it up and gave every prisoner in the barrack a grape, as God’s
plain wonder and comfort. Working like everyone, weak and with his bad
legs, he ruptured himself loading sacks and was moved to the tailors’
shop, where the master tried to do him down to get his own man the place,
until the master was changed and the new one restored the falsified
work-sheets, so that he stood among the leading workers and was freed
early, after four years and eight months of five, on the Dormition, and
rehabilitated.

Sukhumi would not register him; in Tbilisi his money and papers were
stolen and he was taken to the militia as a man without a passport, where
the chief, finding a prayer-rope on him, told his men they were supposed
to catch criminals, and this was a monk. Waiting for new papers he went to
the Sioni cathedral, where Patriarch Kallistratos saw him and made him a
supernumerary priest of the cathedral, and honoured him so that on his
deathbed he asked his successor to have him made a bishop — no easy
thing, since the Georgians did not want a Russian among their bishops. In
the war years, 1942–1945, he served at Sioni and was confessor of the Olga
convent at Mtskheta; in 1945 he was moved to Kirovo in the Stepanavan
district of Armenia, and in 1947–1950 was rector of the Holy Spirit church
at Batumi, and the parishes he served revived. The patriarch made him dean
of the Russian parishes of Georgia and Armenia, once under Moscow, and his
appointment eased their friendly passing to the Georgian Patriarchate; he
loved Georgia, knew its history and honoured its saints, and made no
difference between men of different nations, honouring the image of God
in each. As archimandrite and member of the Georgian Synod in the fifties
he stood in a line of greeting for the visiting patriarch of Alexandria,
accompanied by Bishop Pimen (Izvekov), the future Patriarch of Moscow, and
insistently asked the patriarch to change places with him — acting, he
said later, not by his own will and knowing how unseemly it looked; a
little later an icon fell from an upper tier of the iconostasis exactly on
his head, tearing his klobuk, and he came off with a faint, and the
guests’ annoyance turned to gratitude: no one there doubted that God’s
providence and his guardian angel had kept the head of the Alexandrian
Church through him. In 1956 he was consecrated bishop, a year later made
vicar of Patriarch Melchizedek III as bishop of Stepanavan, in 1960 under
Patriarch Ephraim bishop of Tetritskaro, and in 1972 metropolitan; that
year he fell gravely ill and a consultation gave him two days, and he lay
praying to the Lord and to the Mother of God before her icon the Healer,
told his friends afterwards that she had visited and blessed him, and
outlived all the doctors who had foretold his death.

Those who knew him saw in him a hierarch’s greatness joined to a monk’s
humility: after so much hardship and illness he was warm, welcoming and
hospitable, wanting to comfort and strengthen everyone; he said little,
listened first, sometimes gave his view in general talk for the one it
concerned to draw his own conclusion, and many took his attention and
kindliness for clairvoyance, which those who have it hide. A meeting with
him changed lives: the surgeon Gziriashvili, unbaptised at thirty, was
baptised in December 1957 with the metropolitan as godfather, became
another man, and owed to him, he said, his understanding of the human soul
and of a sick man’s suffering. Valery Lyalin, in Glinsk in Tbilisi, tells
how he asked at the cathedral railing where to find the metropolitan, and
was answered by the dry old monk he had asked — worn skufia, grey
threadbare cassock, bast shoes, leaning on a staff, advising an old woman
about her drunken son-in-law — that the metropolitan Zenobius was he, and
thought of the capital’s metropolitans in silk and diamonds handed out of
black cars. Patriarch Ilia II called him a great hierarch of Orthodoxy, a
bearer of divine grace whose warmth, not earthly but heavenly, gathered so
much clergy and so many faithful round him. All his thirty-five years at
the Alexander Nevsky church he lived in a modest little house beside it,
one room with a table, a bed and two bookcases, and refused the residence
Patriarch David V offered; he came to the services morning and evening
unless ill, taught above all that all should live in peace, remember the
Lord always and never think themselves anything — without God, as the
proverb says, not even to the threshold — read constantly so as to be all
things to all men, led people not to himself but to God, rebuked those
who leaned on him instead of their own effort with “put not your trust in
princes, nor in sons of men”, and demanded obedience of those who asked
his blessing: if you ask, then obey; if you will not do it, why come. Worn
by the crowds and the hours of talk, he prayed only at night, rising at
one or two and asking only not to be stopped. Once he forbade communion to
Angelina, abbess of the Olga convent, for judging priests, and a twisted
report reached Patriarch Alexis I that he meant to close the Russian
convent; the patriarch, regretting the misunderstanding, later gave the
Alexander Nevsky church an icon in a silver riza. He loved Ilia II and told
Fr Vitaly (Sidorenko) and Mother Seraphima before his death that the
patriarch was his spiritual son and a good man, and their confessor
henceforth; and tonsuring Michael Potapov, the future archimandrite
Modestus, shortly before his death, said: with Modestus all that is mine is
finished. He took the schema with the name Seraphim, for Seraphim of Sarov,
served his last Liturgy on that saint’s day, 1 August 1984, feeling his
legs fail, came to church for the last time on St Nicholas’s day, 19
December, and prayed thereafter in his cell day and night; on his last
night he told his cell-attendant, I am leaving you, asked the igumens
Philaret and Benjamin to stay, was anointed and given communion, and
during the canon for the departure of the soul died quietly, in his
eighty-seventh year, on 8 March 1985. Patriarch Ilia II led the bishops
and clergy at his monastic burial, two choirs sang, Russian and Georgian,
and the patriarch said he believed the departed would not leave them out of
his prayers; many have found help at his tomb — I am leaving, he had told
his cell-attendant, pointing upward, but there I will pray for you. The
Synod of the Ukrainian Church numbered the schema-metropolitan Seraphim
among the locally venerated saints on 25 March 2009, and on 30 November
2017 the Council of Bishops of the Russian Church glorified him for the
whole church, with his memory on 22 September — 9 September of the old
style — in the synaxis of the Glinsk fathers; the calendar keeps 8 March as
well.
