# Jacob the Hermit

The Romanian page gives Jacob in a single paragraph, and it is a paragraph about a fall. After fifteen years as a hermit he had been granted the gift of wonderful healings; and after that, the page says plainly, he fell into sin and committed a murder. In despair he left his cave and went out into the world.

God set an old man in his way, who reminded him of David's example and David's prayer — have mercy on me, O God, according to thy great mercy, and according to the multitude of thy compassions blot out my transgression — and urged him to repentance. Jacob turned back from the road to perdition and spent many years in a tomb, in tears and prayers.

God gave him a sign of hope: there was a drought in that country, and at the hermit's prayer the heavens opened and the rain came. He kept the canon of his repentance to the end of his life, the page says, hoping in the mercy of God for the sin he had done. No year, no country and no name of a companion is given, and nothing is added here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same twenty-eighth of January, and saint.gr's page fills in what the Romanian notice left bare. His cave was near the small town of Porphyreone, and he spent fifteen years in it. Before the fall, the page says, some lewd and envious men brought a harlot to him, who got by a trick into his cell and urged him to sin; he reminded her instead of the punishment of the fire to come, and she repented and changed her life. Then a man of rank brought him a daughter possessed by a demon; Jacob prayed and the demon left her, and the father, fearing its return, left the girl in the cave with her younger brother for company. It was there that Jacob was overcome by desire and ruined the girl, and then, so that the foul deed should not be known, killed both her and her brother and threw their bodies into the river nearby.

The Serbian church keeps him on the fourth of March, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović tells the same fall in the same order, with a century, one scene the other two calendars do not have, and one difference in the count of the dead. It puts him in the sixth century. He had so perfected himself in the life pleasing to God, it says, that he healed the heaviest sick by his prayer; and the enemy of the human race brought strong temptations upon him for it.

The scene the other pages do not have is what he did with his hand. Mockers sent a loose woman to him, who pretended before him and wept while she was drawing him towards sin; and seeing that he was about to fall, Jacob put his left hand into the fire and held it there until the whole of it was scorched. The woman was filled with fear and horror at the sight, repented, and mended her life.

On another occasion, the entry goes on, he did not withstand the temptation. Parents brought him a girl out of her mind, to be healed of her madness; he healed her indeed, and then sinned with her, and then, to hide the sin, killed her and threw her into the river — from fornication to murder, the Prologue remarks, is no long way. He spent ten years after it as a penitent living in a grave, and knew that God had forgiven him when rain fell at his prayer in a great drought that was killing men and cattle. The entry draws the lesson itself, and names David's as the like case: how wicked the evil demon is, how by God's permission he can throw down the greatest of spiritual giants, and how God in His mercy forgives sincere penitents even the heaviest sins and does not punish them when they have punished themselves.

The Russian church keeps him on this same day, which is the Serbian day too and not the twenty-eighth of January the Greek and Romanian calendars give him, and its line sets him in the sixth century with no year: «Прп. Иакова постника (VI).» Its life is a fourth telling of the fall, closest to the Serbian in order, and it differs from all three in the place and in the count of the years. The cave was «недалеко от Финикийского города Порфириона», and he was fifteen years in it and received the gift of wonders there; and when the fame of him spread, so as not to fall into the snare of it he went away and found a second cave and lived thirty years in that. The second cave and its thirty years are in no other telling read for him.

The fall it tells without the killing. He healed a girl of possession, it says, and fell with her into sin — «Иаков исцелил отроковицу от беснования, но впал с нею в грех» — and the horror of it threw him into despair; he wandered the desert a long while with no shelter and no rest, gnawed by his conscience, and had all but settled on leaving the monastic life and going back into the world. There is no murder in this telling at all, where the Greek page has him kill the girl and her younger brother and throw both bodies into the river, and the Serbian has him kill her and throw her in. What stopped him, the page says, was the measureless mercy of God, which the sins of the whole world cannot overcome and which wills that all men be saved.

Wandering, he came on a monastery and confessed his sin before the abbot and the brethren; the abbot urged him to stay with them for fear he should fall into final despair, and he would not, and went wandering again. At last Providence set in his way a hermit full of grace and wisdom, who received his repentance and offered him a place beside him; he did not take that either, but went away strengthened and comforted by the conversation, shut himself in a cave, and for ten years brought God his repentance there, weeping and crying out and asking forgiveness for what he had done. The Lord heard him and gave him back the gift of wonders, and he stayed in that cave until his death and was buried in it. The drought and the rain that the other three pages make the sign of his forgiveness are not in this telling.
