# Pelagia of Tarsus

In the persecution Diocletian raised against the Christians, many fled to the mountains, while those stronger in faith stayed in the churches praying to be made victors. At Tarsus in Cilicia there was a bishop named Clinon, who had brought many pagans to the true God and baptised them, and the emperor, being then in Cilicia, ordered the gates of the city shut and the bishop hunted. Warned by a revelation, Clinon left the city secretly, because his hour had not yet come, and hid in the mountains; the emperor turned his anger instead on those the bishop had baptised and threw them into prison.

In that city lived a maiden named Pelagia, of a good and rich family, wonderful in beauty, wise and full of the fear of God. Hearing the Christians speak of Jesus Christ she was set on fire with love of Him, resolved to marry no mortal and corruptible man but to be the bride of the deathless Bridegroom, and longed for baptism; her mother was a pagan widow, from whom she hid her faith. When the emperor's son, hearing of her beauty, sent honourable men to ask for her in marriage — to her mother's great joy — she crossed herself and answered without fear: «Eu m-am logodit cu Fiul lui Dumnezeu, cu Împăratul cel fără de moarte» — I am betrothed to the Son of God, the deathless King.

She asked leave to visit her old nurse, who lived in a village outside the city, hoping to find the bishop; her mother refused, and that night the Lord appeared to her in Clinon's likeness, saying nothing, only showing her his face. She asked again and her mother gave way, dressing her in imperial purple with gold and precious stones as the betrothed of the emperor's son, and sending her out with carriages, horses and servants. Ten stades from the city, at a wooded mountain, her servant Longinus recognised the man coming down the path, and Pelagia stopped the carriage and went to meet him on foot, sending her people to rest among the trees.

She asked him what was most needed by those who would be joined to his God, and he answered: baptism, for the forgiveness of sins and for eternal life. She fell at his feet and begged it, renouncing Satan and the idols; the bishop raised his hands and prayed, and a spring of living water rose out of the ground before them. He hesitated, ashamed to baptise a virgin; she told him she could see two shining youths at the spring holding a bright cloth, and he too saw the angels, and baptised her. Afterwards she gave him her purple and her jewels to sell for the poor, saying that no one can serve two masters.

Her servants had been struck blind while she spoke with the bishop; when their sight returned they asked who the woman was who had stood between them wearing two crowns with a cross shining above them, and she told them to be silent and began to teach them the faith. Her nurse found her changed — humble where she had been proud, silent where she had been talkative, fasting and keeping vigil — and sent her away quickly for fear of the emperor's son. Her mother, learning of the baptism, went to the emperor for soldiers to hunt the bishop; Pelagia slipped out of the house with the servants who had believed and hid across the river Cydnus, and the soldiers, blinded, looked at her and did not see her.

Then, taking counsel of the Holy Spirit, she went home and reproached her mother for making war on the God of heaven, and got nothing from her; her mother sent word to the emperor's son that his betrothed had married the God of heaven. The young man reasoned that torture would not turn her, that she would die gladly and leave him shamed and bereaved, and killed himself with his own sword. His mother-in-law to be, fearing Diocletian's revenge on her whole house, bound her own daughter and brought her to the emperor as the cause of his son's death.

But Diocletian, seeing a beauty greater than any of his own women, turned from revenge to lust; he sent her mother home free with a hundred talents, and the next day offered Pelagia the imperial crown and the succession for a son of hers if she would deny Christ. She answered that she was sick at the thought of his marriage, that she had Christ for bridegroom, and that three incorruptible crowns were prepared for her — for faith, for purity, and for martyrdom. He had a brazen ox heated until it threw off sparks like a live coal, and when she would not be moved he ordered her stripped, to feed his eyes; she reminded him of his own wife and daughters, and then, crossing herself, undressed herself and threw the clothes down before him rather than be touched by unclean hands.

Crossing herself again she ran to the glowing ox without waiting to be thrown in. Her hands melted like wax at the touch of it; she put her head through the opening and went in whole, glorifying God aloud for strengthening her, and there gave up her soul. Her body melted like butter and poured out like myrrh, and the whole city was filled with the fragrance. The emperor had her bones thrown out of the city on a mountain called Linaton, where four lions came out of the desert and sat by them, keeping off the beasts and the carrion birds.

God revealed her end and the place of her bones to Bishop Clinon, who went to the mountain and found the lions beside them; seeing the man of God they came and bowed to him and went back into the desert. He carried the bones higher up the mountain and laid them in a rock, over which, in the reign of Constantine, a church was built, and he wrote her name on the stone: the holy virgin Pelagia, who gave herself to God and struggled for the truth to the end, rests here with her relics, and her soul reigns in heaven with the angels in the glory of Christ.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, and saint.gr's notice gives the whole story that the Romanian line did not. She was born at Tarsus, it says, and lived at Rome in the years of the emperor Diocletian. She grew up among idolaters, but while still young she saw the bishop of Rome in a vision urging her to be baptised; and when she woke and came to herself she asked her mother's leave, and on the pretext of going to her nurse, who lived in another city, she went to the bishop, and he baptised her a Christian. Her joy was great.

She handed her costly clothes to the bishop to sell and spend the money on the poor and the helpless, put on the robe of her baptism, and went on to her nurse; and the nurse, seeing her dressed so, flew into a rage and drove her out. She decided then to go back to her mother, hoping for a mother's understanding and affection; but her mother too was thrown into confusion at the sight of the dress, and falling at her feet begged her to come back to her first faith. Pelagia was disappointed and grieved, but told her that her decision was final and could not be recalled.

When the son of Diocletian heard of it, who was Pelagia's betrothed, he killed himself out of grief. The emperor discovered the cause of his son's death, called Pelagia, and ordered her to sacrifice to the idols; and she refused outright, telling him that she had come to know the true God. Beside himself, Diocletian ordered a brazen ox to be heated and her placed inside it, and so gave her the glory of heaven. Fragments of her relics are kept at the Great Lavra on the Holy Mountain, at Prousos of Eurytania, at Ntaou Pentelis, and in the church of Saint George of the Greeks in Venice.
