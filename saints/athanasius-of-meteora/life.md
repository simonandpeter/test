# Athanasius of Meteora

The whole of what doxologia.ro prints for him is four sentences. This venerable Athanasius was born in the year 1310. He lived a life of monastic struggle on the Holy Mountain of Athos. He founded the famous monastery of Meteora in Thessaly. He was endowed by God with the great gifts of foresight and of working miracles.

The day's line names him without an epithet, «Sfântul Cuvios Atanasie», and it is his own page that says where he belongs: its icons are titled «Cuviosul Atanasie de la Meteor», and the icons on the page of [Joasaph](/saints/joasaph-of-meteora), whom the calendar keeps on the same day, are titled «Sfinții Cuvioși Atanasie și Ioasaf de la Meteora». That pairing is the calendar's own, and is why the two folders name each other.

No year is given for his death, nothing of his parents or his country, and no troparion is printed. What is written above is all that was read.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr's account is the fuller one. Athanasius, Andronikos in the world, was born at New Patras — the present Ypati, near mount Molyvion — of eminent and wealthy parents; the page dates his birth to 1305, or to 1303 according to other sources. Left fatherless while small, he was given into an uncle's care; but when the Franks took his homeland in 1319 he went away to Thessalonica and from there to the Holy Mountain.

On the Mountain they kept a reverent custom of not receiving beardless youths, for fear of scandalising the fathers and to let them ripen fully; so Athanasius left in disappointment and wandered many places. He went to Constantinople, where he came to know the famous Gregory the Sinaite, and later crossed to Crete; and when he had come of age, at thirty, he went back to stay on the Mountain, living in a region belonging to the monastery of Iviron. From there the fathers Gregory the Constantinopolitan and Moses called him and gave him the monastic and angelic habit.

In those years the Turks made many raids on the Holy Mountain, and many monks felt unsafe and withdrew inland for protection; the same happened at about the same time to Gregory Palamas. Among those who chose that exile were Gregory the Constantinopolitan with his disciples Athanasius and Gabriel, and the three of them went west, passed Thessalonica and Veroia, and went up in reverence to the Skete — the whole valley of the Aliakmon, which counted many monasteries — where they stayed, tradition says, three years, arriving in 1340, four or five years after Gregory Palamas left.

Athanasius, his elder testified, loved stillness and turned away from the tumult of the town, and his heart was set on absolute solitude. So it leapt when he heard from Jacob, the bishop of Servia, that further south there were rocks so high that not even eagles nested on them, and that ascetics had been seen there who spoke to nobody and hid themselves as far off as they could. Almost at once he took leave of the brethren, left the Skete of Veroia and went to Stagoi, which is Kalambaka; and there, up on the towering rocks and on one stone in particular called the Pillar, he settled with two disciples and built a church. That stone he named Meteoron; and because those who came to him for the ascetic life multiplied, he founded a coenobium with a church in the name of the Transfiguration of Christ the Saviour. There he passed his years in asceticism, and having lived seventy-eight years he died peacefully in 1383, or by another reckoning in 1373.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović gives him four short sentences that agree with the fuller account this folder already carries. He was born in 1310. He ascetised on the Holy Mountain. He founded the renowned monastery of Meteor in Thessaly. And he had a great gift of insight into what is hidden, and of wonderworking.

The year agrees exactly with the 1310 the folder already held, so nothing was changed by it; this book gives no year for his repose, where the Greek page prints two and settles neither. Its Thessaly is the same country the Greek page reaches by way of Stagoi and Kalambaka, and its one sentence about the monastery is the whole of what it says about the house.
