# Augustine of Canterbury

The Saxons, the Angles, the Jutes and the pagan Germans who began settling in Britain from 454, saint.gr says, having pushed the Britons by fire and sword into the mountainous places and the islands, went on living in the darkness of error and idolatry. Gregory the Great, bishop of Rome, fulfilling an old and burning wish of his own to make Christians of the Anglo-Saxons, sent Augustine there — a monk of the monastery of Saint Andrew at Rome — with forty monks for company, to take up that work.

After many adventures they reached Britain and landed on the island of Thanet in 596, and at once sent word to Ethelbert, king of Kent, of their arrival in his country and of their purpose. A few days later the king went out to the island himself to meet them; and having heard them, he agreed to give them what they needed, without promising or binding himself to believe in Christ. He granted them a dwelling at Canterbury and the church of Saint Martin, which the old Britons had left, and which queen Bertha — a Christian from Gaul, with a bishop from Paris for her own chaplain — had been using.

With that church as their base the missionary monks began to preach, to baptise and to celebrate the Liturgy, and many idolaters were drawn by them to the Orthodox faith. After a year Augustine went to Arles, where he was consecrated bishop by the metropolitan Virgil; and having come back he sent Laurence and Peter to Rome to ask for more missionaries to strengthen the work. Gregory sent many chosen disciples of his own, among them Mellitus, first bishop of London, Justus, first bishop of Rochester, Paulinus, first archbishop of York, and Rufinianus, third abbot of the monastery of Saints Peter and Paul at Canterbury. Bede records that Gregory sent by these missionaries many sacred vessels for the worship, coverings for the altar, vestments for the priests and relics of saints to the Church of Britain.
