# Maura, wife of Timothy

Maura was seventeen years old and twenty days married when her husband [Timothy](/saints/timothy-husband-of-maura), a reader of the Church in the village of Pirapei, was taken as a Christian before Arian, governor of the Thebaid under Diocletian, and blinded and hung head downward for refusing to sacrifice and to hand over his books. She was sent to him scented and adorned, to draw him back to the world, and she pleaded with him: barely twenty days married, she said, she did not yet know his ways nor the rooms of his house, and it wounded her to see him suffer and to be left a widow so young.

He told her to let go of the short and empty things of this world and come with him to that beautiful struggle, for which the Saviour would give them crowns. She answered that she had come with her heart full of worldly attachment, but that once he began to speak the Spirit of God had entered into her, and that she now desired the same things he loved. When he told her to go and rebuke the governor she said she was afraid she would not be able to endure the sight of the torments, being only seventeen; and he prayed over her that God would not let them be parted in the contest.

She went and stood before Arian and told him that no gold of his would buy her soul, and that she had put on the armour of her Lord Jesus Christ. He offered to marry her to the richest of his centurions; she answered that she was joined to the heavenly Bridegroom. He had the hair torn from her head, and she thanked him, saying that she had dressed it at his own crooked advice and so given her husband offence. He had her fingers cut off and thrown away, and she said it was a second sin taken from her, the fingers with which she had put on her deceiving ornaments; watching them cut, she told Timothy's father, it was like seeing a man pull herbs up in a garden and throw them aside, and she felt no pain.

Twelve soldiers filled a great cauldron and boiled it until it roared like thunder, and she was thrown in and stood in the middle unhurt, thanking the governor for washing her clean of the sins she had committed in the world, and telling him the water was so cold she felt no heat in it. Suspecting his soldiers of pity, he came down from his seat to test it; she poured it over his hands and the skin shrivelled with the burning, and he cried out that blessed is the Lord God of Maura, for there is no other.

He ordered her released, and then turned on her again and threatened to fill her mouth with live coals. She answered with the seraph who took a coal from the altar with tongs and touched Isaiah's lips, and asked that not her mouth only but her face and head and whole body be covered with coals and burned, that she might be a sweet savour of Christ. When the crowd cried out to him to stop inventing new torments for so young a girl, she turned to them and said that each should mind their own work — the men the things of men, the women theirs with purity and gentleness — and that she needed no help from them, since God was her support. **There doxologia's account stops.** The page prints no more: not her death, not Timothy's, and no year.
