# Michael (Voskresensky), with 28 martyrs

Michael Grigorievich Voskresensky was born in 1883 in the village of
Tyoply Stan, the son of the district police officer Gregory Dmitrievich
Voskresensky; his grandfather Father Demetrius was rector of the church at
Poretskoye and taught the Law of God in the school, where his future
father-in-law Ivan Danilovich studied — to whom Father Demetrius once
appeared after his death, which he took as a special sign, so that when
years later the grandson Michael asked for his daughter Maria he agreed
without hesitation. At Bortsurmany he served from 1910, loved by his
parishioners for kindness, piety and blameless fulfilment of his pastoral
duties; then came 1917 and the open persecution of the Church, and in
half a year of cruel rule the Bolsheviks set the people against them and
risings broke out everywhere. In the summer of 1918 a group of Kolchak’s
troops was retreating down the Sura, and the people of the town of
Kurmysh rose, to free themselves from the Bolshevik captivity or join the
retreating troops; led by the director of the local bank, Sovernin, the
townspeople disarmed the Red Army company in the town and locked the
soldiers up, ordering two men of Bortsurmany who happened to be there,
Nicholas Migunov and Nicholas Nebasov, to guard them and give them neither
food nor drink — and the two fed and watered them and hardly constrained
them at all. The punitive detachment sent to crush the resistance was
almost all Latvians, led by one Garin, a nobleman of Nizhny Novgorod
province, and wherever he went priests were tortured and killed. The
rumour ran through Bortsurmany that the punishers would destroy everyone,
and the voice of the bell went out like a summons, like a funeral knell —
rung by a peasant nicknamed Yelenya, who could ring and loved to; and the
bell was a special one, the peasants’ own, cast at their expense and
hauled there by themselves, harnessed in turns so that all should have a
share in drawing the sounding preacher. Yelenya stood on the bell-tower
and rang and rang, a strong tocsin heard all round, and people listened
in alarm to the untimely ringing. Fr Michael was not in the village; he
had gone to neighbouring Kozlovka to give an old man communion. The
detachment camped on the hill facing the village, heard the bell, felt
that it could not be speaking for them, foreigners and godless, and set a
gun against the village meaning to burn Bortsurmany — as would probably
have happened had a postman not fallen into their hands: is Bortsurmany
entrenched? asked Garin; there are no trenches, said the postman; you lie,
it is entrenched — no, there are no trenches at all. Two scouts were sent
at last, met a peasant peacefully ploughing at the very entrance to the
village, and knowing how to win him over, having been peasants
themselves, one harnessed his horse to the plough and ploughed while the
other asked who lived where and how to get there, until a whole list was
made; that night the punishers rode into the village and began the
arrests, bringing the prisoners to the volost office. Late that night
Fr Michael was coming home, and at the edge of the village the punishers
barred his way: who goes there? — a priest; that was enough — let us kill
him, he heard; there is time yet, said another, and they let him go home.
At the same time other punishers had come to his house to arrest him,
found him out and left; coming in, he understood the lot prepared for him
and did not flee. Soon they came for him, and his wife went after them to
hand him a coat for warmth — he will be warm enough without a coat, they
answered. The decision was already taken to execute all the prisoners;
they were beaten all night, Fr Michael with especial cruelty, and having
no guilt to charge him with his tormentors accused him of ordering the
bell rung and awaiting Kolchak’s detachment with joy. Arrested with the
priest was the reader Eulampius Pavlovich Nikolaev, from Ilyina Gora, once
a clerk in a neighbouring village and a relative of Fr Michael, whom the
priest had once invited to Bortsurmany as church reader and who now
shared his martyrdom; among the peasants arrested were Nicholas Migunov
and Nicholas Nebasov. To forestall an outcry the executioners announced
that all the prisoners would be sent to Kurmysh for trial, but the
passion-bearers knew the fate prepared for them and made ready for death,
repenting and confessing. Before noon on 27 August (9 September) a column
of thirty under guard set out along the Kurmysh road, Fr Michael in front
singing the funeral hymns aloud and his parishioners with him; so they
went five kilometres to a ravine called Stepanikha, where all were ordered
into one line with the executioners facing them. Fr Michael sank to his
knees and prayed to God with lifted hands, and not one of the sixteen
bullets that struck him could end his life — a plain sign of a wonder —
until one of the executioners came up and stabbed the hieromartyr in the
heart with a bayonet. Of the thirty only one survived, Ivan Petrovich
Kurepin, who told the details of the martyrdom of the priest, the reader
and the twenty-seven peasants. After the killing the punishers sent a
local man to Bortsurmany to say that the bodies were to be taken away or
buried there, all by evening; the peasants came with carts and took them
all, setting an inscribed cross at the place, and that evening the dead
were buried in five common graves. Three had no relatives in the village
and there was no time to make them coffins — the hieromartyr Michael, the
reader Eulampius and the volost clerk — and the priest’s coffin was the
coffins of his parishioners, on which he was laid and by which he was
surrounded: Nicholas Migunov, Nicholas Nebasov, another Nicholas Migunov,
and a servant of God whose name is unknown. The priest’s house was
plundered; soon after, his widow wrote to the authorities in Moscow
asking why her husband the priest had been killed, and the answer came
from Moscow that he had suffered innocently. But the killers were not
sated: the priest Stephen Nemkov, Fr Michael’s friend, served at Deyanovo
nearby, where Garin’s men chose victims though no one there had taken
part in the peasant resistance. The day before his martyrdom two Red Army
men came to his house, and he received them with love and fed them; over
dinner they urged him — father, hide somewhere, or they will shoot you
all — and nothing wavered in him: with a bright face he rose from the
table, pointed with a wide gesture to the five-domed church of the Holy
Trinity and said, there, you see, is the Trinity; I shall not go anywhere
from it; our Lord Jesus Christ did not hide and skulk, and neither shall
I. By evening he was arrested with eighteen peasants — his wife Anna
packed a bag for the road, and he took nothing — held in the school and
long beaten, the priest most cruelly mocked, his hair shorn before the
execution; on Sunday afternoon they were led out towards Maltsevo, lined
up at a ravine and shot with a machine-gun, Fr Stephen separately with a
shot to the head, which did not kill him, so that the killer bayoneted
him. All were buried in common graves except the priest, buried apart in
the middle of the cemetery; on the third day Evgenia Khorina gathered the
believing girls and with a box they went to the place to collect the
martyrs’ blood and remains, buried the box in a pit dug on the spot, and
a cross was set there later and panikhidas served; the commander Garin
was soon killed by his own punishers. The page goes on with the parish’s
later confessors — the priest Vladimir Karpinsky, who came to Deyanovo
after Fr Stephen and was shot dead at the doors of the church by a local
communist as he cried “Christ is risen” at the Easter procession of 1923;
Fr Sergius, the last priest of Deyanovo before the church was closed in
1937, driven from house and lodge, arrested and dead in prison with his
psalmist John Tarutanov; the warden of Bortsurmany, Alexis Migunov, and
his wife Irene, who preached the word of God in the fiercest years and
both died in prison; and Maria Shipilova, a landowner’s daughter now among
the village’s poorest, who sold the cow that fed her and her daughter to
pay the three hundred roubles the village soviet demanded for permission
to serve Easter in the mid-thirties, was exiled to Karaganda for it and
soon died there. The Russian calendar keeps Michael with his twenty-eight
on 24 August (Julian) and in the synaxis of the saints of Nizhny Novgorod.
