# John Cassian

doxologia's life calls him a father of proto-Romanian stock, born about the year 365 in Dobrogea, then called Lesser Scythia, at a place that has kept his name to this day — the Casian plateau and Casian's cave — while the village of Casimcea in the county of Tulcea carries it too. He came of a distinguished family and went through the schools of his time; but, set alight by a burning thirst for spiritual perfection, he gave up while still young the empty and deceiving attractions of worldly life and left for the Holy Land with his friend [Germanus](/saints/germanus-of-dobrogea), a brother not by birth but in the spirit.

So the two of them became monks in a monastery at Bethlehem. When they had settled properly into the rules of the coenobitic life, after the pattern of the monks of Palestine, Mesopotamia and Cappadocia, and felt in themselves the longing for a greater perfection, they decided to go to the hermitages of Egypt, to the anchorites whose manner of life they had heard of. They reached the communities of the Nile delta and went further and further into the desert from there.

Everywhere they passed they sought out the holy solitaries in earnest, to honour in them the brightness of grace and the wealth of their fruits, and to ask them counsel for the saving of the soul. Those questions and answers were to be left to us as a holy inheritance in the book of the *Conferences*, with which Cassian endowed the Church.

Gathering what they could of that heavenly teaching, they stayed seven years in Egypt on the advice of Abba Joseph, and then went on, visiting these teachers of the spiritual work from place to place, until they came to the famous desert of Scetis, where Macarius had founded and made the shining desert worthy of the honour of all. A great number of monks laboured there in hard asceticism, among whom the fathers Moses, Serapion, Theonas and Isaac shone especially, and the priest Paphnutius, their teacher, who told them that it is not enough for a monk to renounce the world and leave behind worldly goods and honours.

Against the objections that were raised long afterwards, Cassian stood firm in the right faith of the Church, far from noise and quarrels, schooled in the depth of divine contemplation, in the mystery of an unbroken and gentle peace and a serene stillness. He entrusted his soul to God in peace about the year 435. Known for a saint by those of his own time, he is honoured by all the monks of the Western Church as their father and one of their greatest teachers, and his holy relics are kept to this day in the monastery of Saint Victor at Marseille.

The Greek calendar keeps him on 28 February, where the Romanian keeps him on 29, and calls him simply Cassian the Roman. saint.gr says he was born at Rome of devout and eminent parents who saw to his upbringing in the instruction and admonition of the Lord, and that his acquaintance and company with holy men from childhood worked well on the forming of his character and his whole way of life; he studied the science of philosophy and of astronomy, and made a particular study of the writings of the Fathers and of Holy Scripture. He took up the monastic life in a skete and visited the monasteries of Egypt and of the Thebaid, of Nitria, of Asia and of Cappadocia. St Nikodemos the Hagiorite, the page quotes, writes that he went to various places and met holy and most discerning ascetics and gathered the virtues of all of them into himself, like another industrious bee, so that he too became a type and an example to others of every kind of virtue.

The first volume of the Philokalia, it adds, carries two of his discourses — to Castor the bishop, on the eight thoughts of wickedness, gluttony, fornication, avarice, anger, sadness, listlessness, vainglory and pride; and to Leontius the abbot, on the holy fathers of the Skete, with a discourse on discernment — which show the purity of his life and his Orthodox mind and are of great profit; and [John of the Ladder](/saints/john-of-the-ladder) weaves a just encomium of Cassian into his discourse on obedience. He fell asleep in peace. The page's heading dates him «(360 - 435)», a little earlier than the year doxologia's life gives for his birth, and no date in this folder is changed on its account; its couplets say that Cassian, gone from here to the world of minds, breathes out divine cassia spiritually, and that he died about the twenty-ninth.
