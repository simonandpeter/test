# Anastasius of Antioch

In the thirty-fifth year of the reign of Justinian the Great, when Domninus the younger, patriarch of Antioch, had died, Anastasius came after him — called the Sinaite, because he was taken from Mount Sinai to the episcopate. The calendar keeps him on the same day as the monk Anastasius of Sinai, and both pages begin by saying that the two are not to be confused.

In those years a wrong belief about the body of Christ arose in the Church: that while he lived among men, before the voluntary Passion, it was incorruptible and not subject to the natural needs of the flesh, and that he ate after the Resurrection in the same manner as before it. The opinion began at Constantinople. The emperor Justinian had attached himself to it and wished to set it among the dogmas of the faith, and Eutychius, patriarch of Constantinople, was driven from his seat for opposing it.

Many would have been carried away, the page says, had they not looked to see how Anastasius of Antioch judged the truth; for he was a man deeply learned in the Scriptures, firm in the dogmas of the right faith, and holy in his life. The heretics worked on him with every art, hoping that if he yielded all those parts would follow; they set the emperor against him; and they gained nothing. He wrote to the emperor boldly, reproving the error, and wrote through all Syria to the clergy and the monks, and preached daily in Antioch the word of the apostle Paul: that if anyone preach to you more than what you have received, though he were an angel from heaven, let him be anathema.

Justinian was angered and meant to drive him from his throne as he had driven Eutychius, but his own end came first; at his death he repented of the heresy and left an ordinance that Eutychius should return from exile to his see, and he died in repentance and is numbered with the Orthodox emperors. Then Justin the Younger, his nephew, came to the empire, and at the urging of quarrelsome men drove Anastasius from the see of Antioch — not for the faith, the page insists, but on unjust pretexts: that he did not spare the church's treasury but spent it vainly, and that he had spoken ill of the emperor.

It was said of him that when someone asked why he did not spare the treasury he answered, «so that it may not be taken by Justin, the destroyer of all the world». Some say too that when Anastasius was raised to the see of Antioch, Justin, then a noble of the palace under his uncle, asked a gift of gold from him and got nothing, Anastasius telling him that it was unjust to give gold for a spiritual office, which is not sold for gold but given by the gift of the Holy Spirit; and that from that time Justin sought occasions against him.

In his place, and against his own will, Gregory, abbot of the lavra of Pharan, was set over the patriarchate of Antioch, a man likewise adorned with word, with life and with right belief. When Gregory died, Anastasius came back to the throne of Antioch after twenty-three years of banishment, in the reign of Maurice.

The pope of Rome then was Gregory called the Dialogist, who lived with Anastasius in great spiritual love and wrote to him. Hearing of his return he sent at once, rejoicing: «Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will among men; for the great river which had once left the stones of Antioch dry has now returned to its own bed and waters the valleys about it». In another letter he answers Anastasius's wish to speak without paper and reed, and says that they are not divided by places who are joined by the bond of love; that the wings of the golden dove are the love of God and of the neighbour, and that Anastasius had flown to him upon them; and he asks his prayers among the perils of the Lombards who had fallen upon Rome.

From such a letter, the page says, the love of the two men in the Holy Spirit is plain, and the holiness of Anastasius is witnessed. He lived six years more after his return to the throne, and departed to the Lord in the fourteenth year of the reign of Maurice, in the year 599. After him another Anastasius came to the patriarchate, without the name of Sinaite, who was killed by the Jews in the reign of Phocas the tyrant and honoured by the faithful as a martyr of Christ; he is not this man.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr begins by saying how little it has: «Δυστυχώς για τον Άγιο Αναστάσιο έχουμε πολύ λίγες πληροφορίες για τη ζωή του». What it has is this. He lived as an ascetic on mount Sinai at the beginning of the sixth century, and from Sinai he went to Antioch, where he became the representative of the Church of Alexandria. When Domnus, the bishop of Antioch, died, clergy and people raised him to the episcopal throne of Antioch, in 559.

The emperor Justin, on the pretext that he had squandered the property of the Church, exiled him in 570 to Jerusalem, where he remained studying and writing until 593, when he returned to his throne; and he died about 599. About the end of his life the page declines to follow its own calendar: «Τώρα όσον άφορα το τέλος του, που οι Συναξαριστές σημειώνουν μαρτυρικό, ότι δηλαδή μαρτύρησε δια ξίφους, θετικές πληροφορίες δεν έχουμε» — as for his end, which the synaxarists note as a martyr's, that is, that he was martyred by the sword, we have no positive information.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović tells the whole arc of his two patriarchates in one paragraph. A monk of Mount Sinai, he was chosen patriarch of Antioch in the reign of the emperor Justinian, raised to that place by his virtue, the purity of his life, his great spiritual learning and his firm faith. But the emperor fell into the Docetist heresy, against which both [Eutychius of Constantinople](/saints/eutychius-of-constantinople) and this blessed Anastasius sharply rose up; Eutychius was driven out, and the emperor wished to drive Anastasius out too, but could find nothing in his life to charge him with.

When Justinian died, having first repented and restored Eutychius to his throne, it was Justin his successor who managed to banish Anastasius, on the strength of some slanders. He spent twenty-three years in exile, was brought back to the throne of Antioch in the reign of the emperor Maurice, governed the Church of God six years more, and ended his earthly journey in 599. The year agrees exactly with the one this folder already held, and the twenty-three years agree with the exile from 570 to 593 the Greek page gives it; the man set in his place while he was away is [Gregory of Antioch](/saints/gregory-of-antioch), whom this book prints as the next entry of the same day.
