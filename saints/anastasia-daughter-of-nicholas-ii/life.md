# Anastasia, daughter of Nicholas II

The Romanovs had reigned in Russia since 1613. Nicholas II, eldest son of the Tsar Alexander III, was born at Tsarskoe Selo — the town now called Pushkin — on 6/18 May 1868, on the feast of the holy and righteous Job, as though his long patience and unshaken faith were foretold in it. He was a deeply believing man, and though he was held to be a dreamer, too peaceable for a future tsar, history set him on the glorious throne of his forebears.

On 13 March 1881, after the murder of his grandfather Alexander II, Nicholas became tsarevich, and his father Alexander III became sovereign of holy Russia. Alexander III died early, on the arms of his skilful confessor Saint John of Kronstadt at Livadia, barely forty-seven years old, and his early death left the heir without a thorough training for the rule of the great empire.

In 1894 Alix Victoria Helena Louise Beatrice, daughter of Ludwig IV, grand duke of Hesse and Darmstadt, became the wife of Nicholas II; at her Orthodox baptism Alix was named [Alexandra](/saints/alexandra-wife-of-nicholas-ii). The new family had five children, raised in great reverence for the values of Orthodoxy: [Olga](/saints/olga-daughter-of-nicholas-ii), [Tatiana](/saints/tatiana-daughter-of-nicholas-ii), [Maria](/saints/maria-daughter-of-nicholas-ii), Anastasia and [Alexei](/saints/alexei-son-of-nicholas-ii).

The reign of [Nicholas](/saints/nicholas-ii) ran against a rising tide of revolutionary, nationalist and fanatical movements and the repressive answers of the Russian state. Defeat in the Russo-Japanese war of 1904–1905 and the revolution of 1905–1907 badly weakened tsarist Russia; then the largest country in the world entered the First World War beside the Entente powers. The defeats and losses at the front and the economic and moral ruin of the country brought the tsar's authority and the dynasty's standing down sharply, among the masses and among the great Russian aristocracy alike.

The bourgeois-democratic revolution of February 1917 forced Nicholas II to abdicate on 2/15 March 1917, and with that act of renunciation a glorious history of more than three centuries came to an end. On 8/21 March the tsar was arrested with his family and confined at Tsarskoe Selo, the summer residence of the tsars; from there they were sent by force to Tobolsk, a very isolated place in Siberia, where they lived in a building in which the rules of hygiene could not be kept. After the revolution of October 1917 they were taken to Yekaterinburg, their last residence, and there they were shot, in the night of 16–17 July 1918.

The Bolsheviks under Lenin had made a treacherous plan for the murder of the tsar and his whole family, and the firing squad was led by Yakov Yurovsky. Afterwards the bodies were carried out of the city into the middle of a wood, to a mine shaped like a well called the Ganina pit, where they were burned; what the fire could not destroy was doused with sulphuric acid and buried, earth was thrown in over the pit, brushwood was laid on it, and a military vehicle packed the ground down so that the place should not be known. The Romanian page's account of the execution itself is left here in this summary.

In that same year the Russian Orthodox Church had instituted the feast of All the Saints of Russia, which the tsar had received with much gratitude and reverence, not knowing that he and those dear to him would be honoured along with the multitude of God's saints formed on Russian soil. The remains lay in forgetfulness until July 1991, when their exhumation was approved and the work of identification began, lasting until 1994.

On 17 July 1998, eighty years after the killing, the remains of the Romanovs were buried in an impressive service at the cathedral of Saints Peter and Paul in Saint Petersburg, in the crypt of the Russian royal house, before bishops, priests, monks and a crowd of the faithful. A painted icon of the Romanov family was carried solemnly in the procession, and according to the central newspaper Русский Вестник, the Russian Messenger, red stains like blood appeared on the faces of the martyrs shown on it; under the eyes of thousands of pilgrims the colours grew stronger and the icon gave off a sweet fragrance. On 4 August 2000 the tsar and his family were canonised by the Russian Orthodox Church and entered in the calendar as passion-bearers.

Anastasia is named among the children in the calendar's line for the day and in the troparion. The page gives her no year of birth and tells nothing of her apart from the family's story.
