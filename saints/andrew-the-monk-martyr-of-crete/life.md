# Andrew the Monk-Martyr of Crete

When the persecutions were over and the Church stood at peace, the page says, the devil raised the war against the icons — not openly as before, but by craft, under the appearance of zeal for God — so that men, rejecting the holy icon of Christ, should reject Christ Himself, since the honour paid to the icon rises to Him whose face is figured on it. He stirred up the emperor Constantine, not the one who was equal to the apostles, but another of the same name, called Copronymus; and that emperor sent an order through all the lands of his rule that the icons be thrown out of the houses of the Lord and that nobody dare keep an icon at home, threatening frightful torments and death to all who would not obey.

There was great distress in the Church of God. Many of the faithful fled and hid for fear, the cities were emptied and the people fled into the wilderness; of those who stayed, some submitted and renounced the veneration of the icons, and others held fast to the teaching of the Holy Fathers and resisted. At that time there was on the island of Crete, which is now called Candia, a man named Andrew, who had feared God from his childhood. He had left this world and its glory, taken up his cross and lived a strait life that led to heaven, drawing many others after him into it.

Hearing that at Constantinople the lawless Copronymus was tormenting many saints for the new heretical teaching against the icons, Andrew was kindled with zeal for the faith and with love for Christ, and rose and went to Constantinople, fearing nothing. There he taught the people openly, with courage and great boldness, not to be deceived by the emperor’s words. Seeing what the confessors of Christ endured — some cruelly beaten with ox sinews, some burned with fire, some with their eyes put out and their tongues cut, others with their hands and feet cut off — he could bear it no longer.

He ran into the church of the holy martyr Mamas, where the proud emperor was with all his nobles and courtiers, and passing through the great assembly of the people he came before him and cried aloud: why, O emperor, do you call yourself a Christian and a servant of Christ, while you trample His icons underfoot and do much evil to His servants? The emperor had him seized, and the servants caught him by the head, by the hands, by the shoulders and by his clothes, threw him to the ground, beat him, dragged him and trod on him, until the emperor, wishing to seem merciful, told them to stop.

Brought before him, Andrew said that he had not come out of madness, nor out of a private opinion, nor to be noticed. The emperor answered that it was the madness of their empty minds to give to wood and paints and stone the glory of the eternal God, against what He had commanded through Moses, and that he would torment him not for truth or for Christ but only for his shameless insolence. Andrew answered that he who suffers for Christ’s icons suffers for Christ, and that the dishonour done to the icons passes to Him whose they are; and when he would have explained which images Moses forbade — those a man would hold in place of God — the emperor would not let him speak.

He was stripped and beaten without mercy; and when he bore the beating manfully, the emperor tried enticements. Andrew lifted his eyes to heaven and prayed God to save him from denying Christ, who is painted on His holy icon, and told the emperor it would have profited him more to busy himself with the affairs of the army and the ordering of his people than to drive out Christ and His servants. He was beaten again until the ground was red with his blood, and men of the emperor’s mind threw stones at him and broke his mouth and crushed his ribs.

After long torments he was thrown into prison, where for a time he strengthened in the right faith the faithful who came to him. Then he was brought out and tormented again, his first wounds opened afresh until the flesh fell from him; and at last he was bound by the feet and dragged through the city towards the place where malefactors were put to death. As he was dragged through the market a heretic who had brought fish there to sell, hearing why he was enduring it and moved by the devil, caught up an axe and cut off the martyr’s feet, so that his tormented course was cut short, for he died at once of that pain.

His body was thrown into a ravine among the bodies of robbers, and lay there more than twelve weeks. Then, by God’s command, men tormented by unclean spirits gathered from various parts, took the saint’s body out of the ravine and out of the carrion and laid it in a consecrated place, and in reward received healing at once; and many others were healed at his tomb by his prayers.
