# Dius of Antioch

Dius's homeland was Antioch of Syria. He was born of Christian parents and, growing up in the right faith, entered from his youth on the ascetic struggle of the desert, guided by men inspired by God into the better monastic way. He had much fighting with the devil, the unseen enemy, and with the adversary of his own household, his body, which desires against the spirit; and he overcame the devil with unceasing prayer and subdued the body with fasting, with sleeplessness and with many other labours, putting his earthly members to death in every way and making the body a slave to the spirit.

He took very little food, and not every day but on the second or the third day, and often passed a whole week without food and without sleep. By such abstinence he put himself to death and overcame the adversary, and rose to the height of the life without passions and became a pure dwelling of the Holy Spirit, for the grace of God settled in him; and he became a man powerful in deed and in word, working wonders and guiding many to salvation, so that the heavenly Father was glorified through him.

When he had lived many years at Antioch, a command came to him from God in a vision, that he should go to Constantinople for the profit of many. At first he would not believe the vision, fearing some contrivance of the adversary, since the devil knows how at times to make himself an angel of light. But when the same vision and command of the Lord came to him a second time — and it was even shown him what that city was like, which he had never seen, for in the vision he saw the whole of Constantinople — he submitted to the will of God, who orders all things to our profit; and leaving Antioch he went to the imperial city.

The Romanian page's account of his years there is long and the middle of it is left here unsummarised. At the end, having been told the hour of his death by a man who appeared to him, he bowed to that man and thanked God for such a warning, called the brethren, spoke them his last word of teaching, and gave orders about his burial: that they should not let the people of the city take his body, but should bury it in his own monastery.

Then, setting in order for himself all that belongs to a good Christian end, praying at length for them all and for himself and giving everyone the last kiss, he saw the angel of God standing before him, and gave up his honoured soul to him with joy. The emperor and the patriarch wanted his honoured body brought into the city for the city's defence, but the monks told them his ordinance, that his body should not be buried anywhere but in the monastery's tomb; and, not daring to go against the elder's ordinance, they did as he had commanded and buried him in his own house with an honourable burial.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 19 July. saint.gr tells the same Antioch and the same monastery rather more briefly, and adds a few things doxologia's life does not. Dios came from Antioch of Syria, it says, and lived in the years of Theodosius the Great; he was distinguished for his theological learning and for an unfeigned piety and self-restraint, and by the time he was thirty-five the fame of his virtue had spread so far that people came from everywhere to hear his valuable counsels. After some years the Patriarch Atticus, whose patriarchate the page dates 406 to 425, invited him to Constantinople and ordained him priest. His priestly duties he carried out with much zeal and exactness, and to his parishioners he was the pattern of a holy and tender spiritual father and a counsellor of their households. In Constantinople he also founded a monastery. At one time he fell gravely ill, as far as the doors of death, but was saved by a miracle. In the end he died in peace, having kept to his last breath the fire of his piety and of his affection for the souls of his flock.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 19 July and tells his end at a length neither of this folder’s other two books reaches. He was of Antioch in Syria, of Christian parents, and in his youth was set by God-inspired men on the monastic life and the ascetic struggle. Having endured a long and laborious fight with the devil and with the lust of the flesh, there was given him of God a great gift of wonderworking; and in his prayers, the entry says, he turned most often to the Holy Trinity.

He worked great and terrible wonders by the power of his prayer: he made a dry staff grow green, he filled a dry well with water, and he put an unbelieving man to death and raised him again. After a twofold heavenly vision he left Antioch and moved to Constantinople, where he continued his struggle near the city. The fame of him spread so quickly that the emperor himself came to take counsel of him, and the patriarch [Atticus of Constantinople](/saints/atticus-of-constantinople) prevailed on him and ordained him presbyter.

Having lived many years he made ready for death: he communicated, taught the brethren, lay down on his bed, and in the sight of all was dead. News of his death drew a great crowd, and Atticus came to it, together with Alexander the patriarch of Antioch. But when they would have buried him he rose up all at once, as though waking out of sleep, and said that God had granted him fifteen more years of this life.

And he lived exactly fifteen years more, and set many on the way of salvation, healed many, helped many in various straits and troubles, and at last gave up his soul to the Lord whom he had served faithfully his whole life. He reposed in the year 430, in a deep old age.

The two books name two different emperors, and this one names the later of them. saint.gr has him living in the years of [Theodosius the Great](/saints/theodosius-the-great), who died in 395; the Prologue has [Theodosius the Younger](/saints/theodosius-the-younger) come to him for counsel, and then puts his repose in 430. Both statements are kept as the books make them.
