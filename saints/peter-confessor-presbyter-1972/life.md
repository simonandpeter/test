# Peter (Cheltsov)

Peter Alekseevich Cheltsov was born on 20 August 1888 (old style) in the
village of Shekhmino in the Rybnoye district of the Ryazan region, the
son of a psalmist who later became a priest. In 1904 he finished the
Ryazan church school, then the Ryazan seminary as its first pupil, and as
first pupil was sent at the state’s expense to the Kyiv Academy; at home
a bride remained, Maria Ivanovna Starodubrovskaya, whose father was a
priest and whose mother baked the prosphora at the local church. When he
came home after his first year his intended mother-in-law said, well, our
Petenka has flown high; we shall never see him now — and said it so often
that Peter married, breaking the Academy’s rule against marriage before
graduation. Going back, he met on the stairs the new first pupil, Sergius
Pravdolyubov, who said, congratulations on your lawful marriage; Peter
went cold — the Academy knows — and wrote at once asking to be
discharged, which he was, though it turned out the authorities had known
nothing of it. On 10 October 1911 he was appointed to the priest’s place
at the St George church of Ulyakhino-Yurievo Gorodishche in the Kasimov
district of Ryazan province, ordained on 16 October by Bishop Demetrius
(Sperovsky), and on 11 November made catechist of the Ulyakhino parish
school and the Sivtsevo school of letters. The inspector of parish
schools, the archpriest Anatoly Pravdolyubov, a future hieromartyr, came
to his lessons, stayed for tea, told him how his sons Vladimir and Sergius
were doing, and said, you were wrong to drop the Academy; you should have
finished — and since married priests were now allowed to study there, in
August 1912 he entered the second year, studying beside Sergius
Pravdolyubov, a future hieroconfessor. He finished in 1915 as a candidate
of theology with the right to the master’s degree without further oral
examination, and was made teacher of the Old Testament at the Smolensk
seminary and catechist and inspector of the Smolensk diocesan girls’
school; Bishop Theodosius gave him the nabedrennik in December 1915 and
the skufia in May 1916, made him deputy chairman of the brotherhood of
Abraham of Smolensk in July 1916, and from August he sat on the diocesan
committee for the victims of the war, buying Gospels and religious books
for the hospitals, exploring an edition of the lives of the Smolensk
saints, and lecturing at the brotherhood’s public readings — on 21
November 1916 “on the meaning of suffering”; from July 1916 he edited the
Smolensk diocesan gazette, confirmed in the post by the Synod in
September. He took part in the all-Russian congress of teachers of the
church schools in Moscow in May–June 1917, was elected by the clergy of
the Smolensk diocese to the Holy Council of 1917–1918, and received the
kamilavka in February 1917 and the gold pectoral cross in December. When
the church schools were closed in 1918 he was called up as a soldier to
the rear militia as a priest without a parish, until the parish meeting
of the Elijah church at Smolensk chose him its priest; made archpriest on
18 April 1921, he taught homiletics and liturgics at the pastoral courses
the diocese set up that year and examined candidates for deacon and
priest. On 6 April 1922 he was arrested on suspicion of resisting the
seizure of church valuables — the first step of the confessor’s road of
the future saint of Velikodvorye — held two months and released for want
of a case. His wife Maria was a worthy companion of particular piety; they
lived as brother and sister and raised an adopted daughter, Maria. She
went constantly to the elders and urged him to, and often they were at
Optina with the elder Nectarius, whose spiritual daughter she was; on one
visit the elder took down the icon “Assuage my sorrows” and gave it to
her — here, my dears, is my blessing; soon your academies will begin —
which the young couple did not understand until his arrest, and which
became her stay in her endless fears and waitings; it hangs to this day in
the narthex of the church of Paraskeva at Velikodvorye. In 1923 Patriarch
Tikhon gave him the jewelled cross, and in 1927 he became a mitred
archpriest. In 1924 he was held ten days during the Renovationist
congress at Smolensk, as a zealous follower of Tikhon to be isolated; in
1927 he was arrested for group anti-Soviet activity and spreading
counter-revolutionary literature and sentenced to three years in a
concentration camp, which he served at Solovki, where, he recalled, they
even tried to drown him in the sea and the Lord kept him; from the camp
he sent his wife and sister-in-law his photograph inscribed with
Lermontov’s pine, alone on a bare northern height, dreaming of a palm in
the far-off desert of the sunrise — it best expresses my position and my
mood, Solovki, 2 July 1928. Maria sewed and embroidered and sold her work
for his parcels, and even went to Solovki, where at a meeting she set her
pies by a stump and someone came up behind and ate them while they
talked. Released early in 1929 he was exiled three years to Kadnikov in
the Vologda region, working at home as a cobbler, his wife with him,
visiting the Lazarus cemetery church at Vologda; on 7 March 1933 he was
arrested again as a member of an anti-Soviet group of exiles carrying on
counter-revolutionary agitation and given three more years, served in the
labour colony at Konosha. Released in April 1936 he was made priest of
the Kazan church of Narma in the Kurlovo, now Gus-Khrustalny, district of
the Vladimir region, closed in 1941; in April 1941 he was arrested for
unpaid taxes and given a year in a labour camp, and freed on 15 May 1942
went back to Narma. On 16 April 1943 Archbishop John (Sokolov) of
Yaroslavl, governing the Vladimir diocese, made him priest of the
Nativity church of Zakolpye, the first church reopened in the district;
its psalmist remembered so many people at Easter that ropes were tied
across the iron rails before the royal doors so that the crowd should not
crush him, each worshipper kissed three times at the cross with “Christ
is risen” after the Liturgy, his lapti on his feet as he walked the
villages for rites — “too little work, too little work”, he would say,
meaning too few rites — anointing, communicating and even daring to
exorcise the many nervous wrecks of the war years, and giving money to the
poor for building. On 18 June 1949, in his sixty-first year, he was
arrested for the sixth time, for anti-Soviet sermons, grouping hostile
church elements about him, agitating to wreck the Soviet measures,
calling the collective farmers to stay away from work and from the
elections, and keeping monarchist literature — forty-six books, two
portraits of Nicholas II and his family and his letters were seized — and
sentenced to ten years, sent under reinforced escort as an especially
dangerous criminal to the Mineral camp near Abez on the Pechora railway.
Released early on 28 November 1955 as an aged invalid of the second group,
into the care of Maria, who had to sign that she would keep her aged
invalid husband — she who had been turned out of the house and robbed of
her firewood after his arrest and lived on kind people’s help — he lived
a year at Zakolpye under police supervision as an exiled settler,
reporting monthly to the Kurlovo commandant’s office until released from
supervision on 28 March 1956. In December 1955, going to Bishop Onesimus
of Vladimir for an appointment, he met on the train the warden and
treasurer of the church of Pyatnitsa — Velikodvorye — also going to the
bishop to ask for a priest, who knew him from Zakolpye and asked him to
be theirs; on 13 December 1955 he petitioned, sincerely intending to serve
Christ’s Church to the end of his days, and was appointed the same day.
At Pyatnitsa he won respect and love from the first. The trains set his
day: most people came by night, were in church by four, and he rose at
three for his prayers, was in church by half past four confessing and
receiving, served the Liturgy from six with a blessing of water, an
akathist and a full panikhida, went home exhausted about noon, came back
an hour later for molebens and funerals, served the vigil from half past
four until nine on the eve of a feast, and in the evening talked with
people or answered his enormous post. Every day he blessed water for the
pilgrims, and what was left went into the well dug by his house, from
which people drew water to drink and to bless — drink the water, he said,
my blessing is in it; a blessed woman named Romanova said, springs will
flow to Pyatnitsa — and living springs did, from every corner of Russia,
simple people and high officials, bishops and clergy, for a blessing, for
comfort, for healing. Laying his stole or his hand on the sick place he
said, I am no doctor, I cannot help, I shall pray and the Lord will heal:
the schema-nun Charitina, who died in 2005, came for a blessing on an
operation for tumours spreading over her body, and he sat beside her,
asked what hurt, would not let her show him, led her to the altar, read a
prayer, served a moleben and blessed her — and the doctors found nothing,
and she came back to Pyatnitsa to live, to thank the Lord for her
salvation. His services were very solemn; he had the church repaired, its
fence restored, its walls and ceiling painted on canvas — there was never
electricity in it, nor is there now, coloured lamps burning in the
chandeliers, most touching on dark winter evenings; he served without
abridgement whatever the singers who pitied him said, clear reading,
harmonious singing on two choirs, singing himself when he could, musical,
with a pleasant soft voice, accompanying church hymns on the harmonium at
home; baptised, married and anointed without refusal, and in the fasts
often gave communion to five hundred, so that in 1969 Archbishop Onesimus
asked the Patriarchate for a chalice of a litre and a half, and he stood
with the chalice until his legs went numb and a church woman guided his
hand. Archbishop Melchizedek (Lebedev) remembered his general confessions,
not of the usual kind — he knew the human soul deeply, could feel for
human weakness and helped people fight sin; I never met a priest who
prayed so penetratingly, and he always served the Liturgy with tears, and
people felt the power of his prayer. He had foresight: a parishioner who
had meant to bring something for the church and then grudged it was asked,
and where is such-and-such? — forgive me, I forgot — one should not forget.
He loved a joke: father, how did you sleep? — oh, badly — what was wrong?
— the night was too short. Many priests of the Vladimir, Ryazan, Moscow
and other dioceses were his spiritual children; the archimandrite Abel of
the St John monastery honoured his fellow-countryman from boyhood as a man
of God, Archbishop Onesimus consulted him on theology, and Metropolitans
Nicodemus (Rotov) and Nicholas (Kutepov), Archbishop Melchizedek, the
archimandrite Cyril (Pavlov) and the archpriest Victor Kukin revered him.
He lived with God, in God and under God, in John of Kronstadt’s phrase,
the old Russian spirit alive in him, patristic Orthodoxy visibly embodied;
a Spirit-bearing, clairvoyant and wise peacemaker, attentive and
magnanimous, remembering everyone and seeing through everyone, his eyes
young and clear even in his last illness. Grateful people sent food, and
he and Maria fed the pilgrims and helped the needy with clothes and money;
the cabbage soup, kasha, potatoes and cranberry kissel cooked in the
Russian stove fed many and still went round to the houses where his
visitors lodged; of soured food he said only, it was worse in prison. They
sent him cloth for vestments, knowing his love of solemn worship — some
thirty embroidered linen vestments, given to priests after his death — and
he went about in light colours, white, pink and cream cassocks, especially
in summer. He was magnanimous, meek and without malice, a peacemaker who
gave presents to his ill-wishers; kindness joined in him with a just
severity, and he had the power not only to heal but to chastise for
correction. On 29 October 1961 Archbishop Onesimus congratulated him on
fifty years of pastoral service — a life full to the brim of every kind of
privation and trouble and very short of joys, upheld by a firm faith in
God’s providence and a burning love for Christ — and he answered with a
remarkable letter: the priest’s service is cross-bearing, every priest
suffers with Christ and Christ suffers in the priest; on Golgotha Satan
gave his flattering advice through the mouths of his people, come down
from the cross — and the enemy whispers it in my heart’s ear, you have
laboured more than enough; the powerful say it, the dark clouds gathering
on the church horizon say it. What then? Shall I leave Christ and lay
down the cross the Lord laid on my shoulders? I am a sinful man and my
strength is small, yet the apostle calls even me, unworthy, as a priest,
a fellow-worker with Christ; shall I go from him who has the words of
eternal life? God forbid! I shall go on working in Christ’s vineyard,
trusting that even my old man’s labour is not in vain before God. At
Easter 1963 he was given the right to serve the Liturgy with the royal
doors open to the Our Father, on 1 December 1967 Patriarch Alexis I gave
him the order of St Vladimir of the second class, and in 1968 a second
jewelled cross. Grown very old he still served, was led home by the arms
after the service and set in an old wicker chair, and went on talking with
people with his eyes closed; father, enough, rest, the warden Maria
Timofeevna would say, and he shook his head — I am doing my duty; soon I
shall rest. In the summer of 1972 he fell gravely ill and still served,
the last time on the Kazan icon, 21 July, tears running down his cheeks as
he closed the royal doors after the service. Before death he suffered
greatly, long without food or sleep, like the elder Leo of Optina, praying
in half-consciousness, serving molebens, panikhidas and funerals, praying
especially for the fatherland; Maria was paralysed and the schema-nun
Heliconis nursed him; to a woman who helped in the house he appeared in a
dream — father, what are you suffering so for? — for other people’s sins
— give them to us — it is not allowed. I stand on the edge, he said; he
was anointed and communicated daily, and died on 12 September 1972, the
feast of Alexander Nevsky — 30 August of the old style, the day the
Russian calendar keeps him — at a quarter to nine in the morning,
peacefully, with prayer on his lips. His body was brought to the church,
the Gospel read over it by priests until the burial, and on 14 September
Archbishop Nicholas (Kutepov) of Vladimir and Suzdal buried him with a
council of clergy from various dioceses, serving in his vestments and, by
the archbishop’s blessing, keeping them in his memory; the weather was
sunny and warm, the crowd greater than at Easter, grief at parting with the
grace-filled elder dissolved in the joy of hope that he would now
intercede for God’s suffering people in heaven, and under the funeral bell
and the irmoi of the Great Canon the clergy carried the coffin round the
church and buried him behind the altar. Maria, unable to walk, was carried
after the coffin on a stretcher, weeping inconsolably, and lived a little
longer than her beloved husband, dying on 4 December 1972, the Entry of
the Mother of God — take me home, she kept saying, meaning the church —
and asked not to be buried beside him: Fr Peter stands at the throne, and
I am unworthy; she lies north of the altar. The jubilee Council of Bishops
of 13–16 August 2000 glorified him in the synaxis of the new martyrs and
confessors of Russia, and on 22 October 2000 his relics were found and
placed in the church of the great martyr Paraskeva, at a service of
Archbishops Melchizedek of Bryansk and Eulogius of Vladimir with clergy of
the Vladimir, Moscow, Ryazan and Bryansk dioceses, the church overflowing
and most of the people communicating. Through five arrests and fifteen
years in prisons and camps he came out to the service of God, the Church
and people with a great gain: we know he had foresight and healed
sicknesses, but the chief thing was not those gifts — it was the true love
and compassion of Christ for a man that gave his prayer such power;
having passed through a real hell he came out a great soldier of Christ,
hallowing all who came with the great love of Christ. The archpriest
Vladimir Pravdolyubov remembered visiting him on the fiftieth anniversary
of the Soviet power, over tea — wine was never served at his table —
when he said, this is our festival, yours and mine: I was at the Council
of 1917–1918, when the palaces and rich men’s houses were confiscated and
their churches wrecked and the antimensia thrown into the street under
the wheels of cabs and the hooves of horses; the Council sent a delegation
to Lenin — two metropolitans, two archpriests and five laymen — with a
protest, and Bonch-Bruevich received them and said, Vladimir Ilyich is
busy with important affairs of state and cannot receive you; I shall pass
him your paper, of course, but you labour in vain — now that we have taken
power, in five years nothing will be left of you. Fifty years have passed,
and here we sit, two priests, old and young, drinking our tea. The Russian
calendar keeps him also in the synaxes of the saints of Vladimir and
Smolensk, of the new martyrs of Solovki, Vladimir and Smolensk, and among
the fathers of the Local Council of 1917–1918.
