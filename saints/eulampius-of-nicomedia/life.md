# Eulampius of Nicomedia

The Romanian life sets Eulampius in a reign it spells Maximilian, when the persecution was so heavy that many of the faithful left their houses and hid in the mountains and caves and waste places. Eulampius was a young man of noble family whose estate lay in the country of Nicomedia, a Christian in faith and in deed; hiding with others, he was sent into the city to buy bread and bring it out to them secretly. Over the city gates he saw the imperial decree for the killing of Christians posted up, read it, and laughed at the folly of an emperor who armed himself not against enemies but against the innocent and so laid his own empire waste.

The idolaters seized him at once, bound him and brought him to judgement. The judge, seeing him young and handsome, first tried to coax him, telling him not to waste so fair a face and dishonour his family; Eulampius answered that the judge's heart was full of deceit and his promises empty. He was then stripped, stretched on the ground and beaten with sinews, and afterwards laid on an iron bed heated red in the fire. When all were waiting for him to die on it he felt a strength come into him, rose and walked about whole, as though he had not been tortured at all.

His sister [Eulampia](/saints/eulampia-sister-of-eulampius), hearing that her brother was suffering for Christ, ran to the place, stood in the middle of the crowd and claimed her share in it. She was struck about the face until the beauty of it was changed and blood ran from her nose and mouth, and her brother strengthened her with words. When a cauldron was heated for them she went into it beside him, the force of the fire went out of it and the water cooled, and the two stood in it unhurt and sang; the life says two hundred of the people believed at that sight.

The judge, not knowing what else to do with them, condemned them to the sword. Their hands were bound behind them and the soldiers led them far off; at the place of execution Eulampius laid his head under the sword and it was struck off, while Eulampia gave up her soul into the hands of God before the blow came, so that the soldiers, seeing her dead, did not behead her.

The Greek calendar keeps the two on the same day, 10 October, and tells how they were taken. saint.gr sets them in the years of the emperor Maximian, and says the persecution was harsh and merciless, so that Eulampius and his sister Eulampia hid in the mountain with other Christians, living there in prayer and the study of the holy Scriptures. One day Eulampius went into Nicomedia to buy food; the idolaters recognised him and arrested him on the spot. Asked by the king whether he believed in Christ, he confessed openly that he was a Christian, and they put him into an idol temple to make him sacrifice by force — where he shattered the idol of the god Ares by his prayer.

As they began to scourge him in the most inhuman way his sister Eulampia rushed forward, embraced him, and begged God to let her suffer with her brother. They then put the two of them into a cauldron of boiling water, and by a miracle they were refreshed in it and came out whole and unharmed. That made two hundred pagans believe in Christ, and they were beheaded together with Eulampius and Eulampia for the truth of the Lord; the same calendar keeps those two hundred as [the Two Hundred Martyrs of Nicomedia](/saints/two-hundred-martyrs-of-nicomedia) in an entry of their own on the same day.

The Russian calendar keeps the two of them at the head of its own 10 October, «Мчч. Евлампия и Евлампии (303-311)», and the plain service icon stands immediately before that line, so the rank on this row is the pair's. The years are the page's own difficulty: the day line gives 303 to 311 and the life it reaches dates the emperor Maximian 284 to 305, and the two meet only at 305.

Its life makes the beginning his. Brother and sister of one family at Nicomedia at the start of the fourth century, they read the emperor's decree condemning every Christian to death, and he was indignant that the emperor should arm himself against his own subjects instead of against the enemies of his country. Taken before the court and required to renounce the faith, he was first torn with iron instruments and then laid on a red-hot iron bed. Then he suddenly asked to visit the pagan temple, and the judges were glad, supposing they had broken the young Christian's spirit.

In the shrine of Mars he cried out: in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ I command you, dumb and lifeless idol, fall to the ground and turn to dust — and the idol crashed down, and the people cried that the Christian God is one, great and strong. He was taken back to the tortures, and it was then that his sister [Eulampia](/saints/eulampia-sister-of-eulampius) came before the judges and declared that she too was a Christian. He gave her heart with the Lord's own words, that she should not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul. After the torments the two were thrown into a heated furnace and the Lord kept them from the fire as well; in the end he was beheaded, and she died of what had been done to her.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him and [Eulampia](/saints/eulampia-sister-of-eulampius) on the Julian 10 October under one heading, as brother and sister of Nicomedia, and makes the arrest an act rather than a recognition. During a terrible persecution some of the faithful had left the city and hidden, and the young Eulampius was sent in to fetch bread. Inside he saw the emperor’s order for the hunting down and killing of Christians pasted up on a wall — and he laughed at it, took it down and tore it up, and was brought before the judge at once.

The exchange that follows is the reverse of the one the other books give. When the judge began to counsel him to deny Christ, Eulampius began to counsel the judge to renounce the false idols and acknowledge Christ as the one living God. He was beaten a long while, until the blood covered him, and put to other bitter torments; his sister ran up to take them with him; and the two were thrown first into boiling pitch and then into a red-hot oven, and by the power of the sign of the cross and the name of Christ they made the fire harmless. At the last his head was struck off. The entry adds that two hundred Christians were put to death as well, having believed at the sight of the power and the wonders given to the two of them — [the Two Hundred Martyrs of Nicomedia](/saints/two-hundred-martyrs-of-nicomedia), whom the Greek calendar keeps as an entry of their own on this day.
