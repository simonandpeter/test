# Nicholas (Tsvetkov)

Nicholas was born on 8 March 1879 in the village of Nikolskoye in the
Zvenigorod district of Moscow province, the son of the deacon John Tsvetkov,
who had a house, a cow and fourteen desyatins of land, and a passion for
drink that kept a family of four children always in want — he could not and
would not work the land, let it to peasants and drank the rent. Nicholas
grew up devout, with the Church’s service for his dream from childhood, and
kept a diary from an early age to give himself an account of his own state,
writing down not only events but his thoughts, and notes on the services
whose meaning he tried to grasp exactly; the family’s poverty put into it a
drawing of his first boots, bought late, when it was time to send him to
school. He finished the church school and in 1900 the Bethany seminary; in
1901 the Volokolamsk cathedral needed a deacon with a good voice, the choice
fell on him, he was married to the orphan Anna, brought up by an uncle, and
ordained deacon for the cathedral. He took his old parents into his house,
where his father went on drinking and raging drunk, a heavy grief in a
deeply religious family that no effort of his could mend, and died drunk in
1908. The blow changed his whole life: no longer content with the prayers
the rule prescribed, he settled in a cell of his own in the bell tower and
led a life of fasting and prayer, sometimes the whole night through, the
little room filled with icons like a chapel, for nothing on earth could
change the lot of the dead but insistent prayer and works of piety; and he
set himself to help every needy and suffering person — there was not a
widow, a poor man or a man in trouble round about he did not help, a
cartload of wood to one, clothes to another, boots to a third; the whole
cathedral clergy owed their comfort to him, for when he saw a priest short
of food or clothes he supplied it, and his own share of the common income
never reached home, being given away on the road, until his wife, with two
small daughters, arranged to be paid it herself. Among people he began to
behave like a fool for Christ, answering questions with riddles and
proverbs, and holding a flower in his hand as he talked, as if smelling it,
real in summer and artificial in winter — even in prison he came to every
visit with a flower. Every summer the clergy went in procession through the
villages with the carved image of Nicholas the Wonderworker, now at Spirovo,
the priests in turn and of the deacons most often Nicholas, tens of
kilometres with several thousand people singing the akathist, stopping at
every village for a moleben and ending at the Joseph of Volokolamsk
monastery, so that the whole country knew the strange deacon who prayed and
fasted, and he came to know the people’s life, its joys and griefs and the
wonders worked at the prayers of the suffering. Made protodeacon for his
blameless service, he went from strength to strength, and the Lord gave him
foresight and healing, and more and more came to him. To the soldier’s widow
Pelagia, offered an old woman’s house in return for keeping her till death,
he said, Pelageyushka, it is good, very good, but hold off a week; wait; the
thing itself will show — a week, father, how long! — and within days the
house burned, and everything she had would have gone with it. To the devout
singer Elena, going home after the liturgy, he called from the tower, come
back, I forgot to give you a present for the road; do not open it now, only
at home — a bundle with everything needed for a burial, and candles; three
days later she died suddenly. He told a family not to go for grain to Nizhny
Novgorod; they went, could get no further than Nizhny, and came back with
nothing. To a woman whose husband had gone to Rzhev on business and vanished
he said, he will come on Friday, and on Friday she ran to say that he had. A
girl who asked his blessing to marry got instead a wrapper from a “crayfish
neck” sweet, was offended — am I a child, to be given a picture? — married,
and died of cancer two years later. Anna Surikova, who suffered from fits,
drank the holy water and herb he gave her and was healed for the rest of a
long life; a five-year-old girl with paralysed legs, whom the doctors had
given up, walked after the moleben before the icon of the Mother of God,
Seeker of the Lost, to which he sent her mother. The country round held him
a saint and a righteous man.

Then the godless came to power. In 1918 they arrested him and set him to
clean the town’s latrines, harnessing him in place of a horse to cart the
barrels of filth out of the town, meaning to shame the man the people called
a saint, and to him it was glory and honour to suffer for Christ; once, in
their hatred of the Church, they arrested the carved image of St Nicholas,
locked it in a shed, and shut the protodeacon Nicholas in with it. Near
Volokolamsk lived Sergei Semyonov, a peasant writer once praised by Tolstoy,
who had joined the Tolstoyans in England and whom the Bolsheviks called an
outstanding worker for enlightenment in the village; on 3 December 1922 he
was murdered near his village, and a well-off peasant Gregory, once chairman
of the local soviet, and his family confessed to it, the motive being
Semyonov’s years of pursuit of Gregory’s wife, ending in her adultery. The
militant godless turned a criminal case into an anti-religious one by
arresting the protodeacon with the murderers, knowing the people held him a
saint, on the ground that the wife had gone to him for advice about the
trouble in her home — and, said Pravda a month after the killing, the
Volokolamsk cathedral deacon told her once that the inner enemy must be done
away with. His diary of thoughts was seized. None of the accused, for all
the interrogators’ efforts, would admit any religious motive — do you
believe in God? the interrogator asked Gregory, who only swore at him. At
the end of April 1923 the visiting session of the Moscow provincial court
tried the case publicly for two days in the town theatre, reported in the
central papers, the protodeacon brought under heavy guard and seated apart:
Izvestia wrote that he was held almost a saint in the district, gave “lucky
roubles” to poor girls, was everyone’s man of prayer, and had called
Semyonov a black magician with the devil in him, and that though he denied
any incitement and claimed to have preached only forgiveness and love of
neighbour, he was made to admit in the end that he disliked Tolstoy’s
teaching and Semyonov who spread it; peasants called by the defence spoke of
his kindness to the poor and destitute; Gregory’s daughter testified that he
had said of Semyonov, since he does not baptise his children, does not go to
church and keeps no icons, the devil’s power is in him — I do not remember,
he answered; maybe I did say something about the devil; lucky roubles I gave
to young girls, and old maids, and mothers of many children. The judge read
out passages of the diary to mock him, and asked how he had sinned with a
maiden; he sighed and asked, and how is it written there — in thought or in
deed? He no longer tried to defend himself, seeing that they had decided to
condemn him, and took it as God’s will; after nine hours the court gave him
ten years’ prison and three of exile. In the Volokolamsk prison his elder
daughter died of typhus at twenty-one and he was not allowed to go to her;
he was moved to the transit prison in Moscow and then to Lefortovo for six
years, two of them in a solitary cell unheated in winter, its walls white
with frost, a brazier of coals for warmth that gave little heat and a
headache from the fumes; in time he was allowed small icons, wax candles and
a few books, prayed much, sang molebens under his breath and wrote letters —
to a devout girl who wrote that she wished to imitate the holy virgins he
answered, that is pride, pride; if only we could be a little like them.

Released under amnesty in 1929, his house taken and his wife and daughter
gone to Moscow, he lived with his brother the deacon Sergius at Yaropolets
and then with spiritual children in Moscow, Volokolamsk and Parfenkovo,
never long in one place lest he bring trouble on his hosts; the news of his
release ran round the country and the people came again for prayer and
counsel, and the authorities watched for an occasion. On 24 September 1931
the chairman of the Parfenkovo village soviet arrested him at the house of
the church warden Maria Shishayeva together with the novice Praskovia
Buleyeva of the closed Borodino convent, who had come to help lift the
potatoes; the believers gathered at the house — why are you taking our
father, he hinders no one — and the chairman sent him on to the OGPU with a
note: rid us of these guests, who hold all the unconscious poor in their
hands. Questioned on 29 September he said the country was dark and
distrustful of the collective farms, which should have been built more
slowly, one good one first; that two signs of antichrist could be seen in
the country, hatred — when a man got eight years people said, too little, it
should be ten — and the complete disappearance of love; that the churches
were empty because priests were forbidden to preach the word of God and were
silent for fear of arrest; and that the Volokolamsk cathedral had been
closed not by the believers, whom it did not hinder, but by the authorities.
The village chairmen testified that the “saint” back from hard captivity
told the women every evening that antichrist had loosed his servants on the
earth for men’s sins and that the collective farms were sent for our great
sins and led to the ruin of the faith, so that collectivisation stood still,
the grain, the insurance and the self-taxation came in slowly, and the poor
said, the kolkhoz is serfdom, there will be famine in it, we will not go. On
25 November 1931 the OGPU troika gave him and Praskovia three years in the
camps. The believers of Volokolamsk petitioned the Synod to intercede for
him, writing that in twenty years at the cathedral he had been their pattern
of sincere faith, meekness, temperance and reverent service, and in the
prisons of 1923 to 1929 of apostolic patience — what prison has not had you
for its prisoner — two years in a cold solitary cell that froze his legs and
left them with open sores, hauling wood and coal and potatoes and bricks as
punishment for the many letters of spiritual comfort he wrote. From the
Volokolamsk prison he sent Praskovia, who had knitted him stockings for his
sick legs, a note of thanks with the hymn of the Entry, a riddle about flax
and a folk song about it, and three new rouble notes for the new present. On
13 December he went to the Butyrki and then to the Mariinsk camps in
Siberia, where the prisoners lived in tents round an iron stove, the strong
and shameless nearest it; a prisoner who denounced him falsely for breaking
the rules got an easier lot, and he got the unheated punishment cell, an
icehouse by night, from which only prayer and the hardening of a man who had
bathed in the river in the bitterest frost saved him. When he fell behind at
a day’s task on the land a Tatar who had finished his own did the
protodeacon’s, which the prisoners counted a plain miracle, the norm being
barely within one man’s strength; nuns from Volokolamsk sent to the same
camp did not know him at first in his rags, one foot in a boot and the other
in a bast shoe because the wound on it had defied all treatment, washed and
bound the wound, sewed him something like a sheet and blanket and bought him
food from the camp stall — and in the morning he told them, a polecat came
and carried it all off. Almost the whole three years he was without his
ration, his meagre dinner often taken from him, and he bore it and said, I
can endure it, and they cannot. Released on 18 June 1934, he came to
Volokolamsk on the 27th and lived with his spiritual children and his
brother. He was arrested on 27 August 1937 and questioned on 6 September: he
had no fixed place or occupation, served in no church, but had not renounced
his orders; when believers came to him with their complaints of a hard life
he agreed that life had become hard and told them to hope in God, to pray,
and to ask God to deliver them from further burdens. Moved to the Taganka
prison, asked on 22 September only whether he admitted anti-Soviet agitation
— no — and whether he had been deprived of civil rights — always, and all
his property confiscated by the court, he said, in 1922 — he was sentenced
to death by the troika on 26 September 1937, shot the next day, 27 September
1937, and buried in an unknown common grave at Butovo. The life is by the
igumen Damascene (Orlovsky); the Church keeps him on 15 September of the old
style, 28 September, and in the synaxes of the new martyrs of Butovo and of
Moscow.
