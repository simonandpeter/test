# Philaret of New York

Philaret, George Nicolaevich Voznesensky in the world, was born at Kursk on 22 March 1903 into a devout Orthodox family, saint.gr says. His father, the archpriest Nicolas Voznesensky, came of a family of priests and was an ardent pastor and a great man of prayer, who was later tonsured with the name Demetrius and became a bishop and afterwards archbishop of Hailar. There were five children, two sons and three daughters, and George grew up from the first in an atmosphere of Christian love and church life.

In 1909 the family moved to the Far East, where George finished his eight years of basic schooling; and when the atheists took power they moved again, to Harbin, a city of intense church life, where after some years he studied at the Polytechnic Institute. In that period he came to know the works of the holy hierarch Ignatius Brianchaninov, and his soul was deeply satisfied by his teaching on the Christian life and the constant remembrance of death; from then on life in the world ceased to interest him. He was ordained deacon in 1930 and presbyter in 1931, and in the same year took the monastic tonsure with the name Philaret, after Saint Philaret of Gouverneto.

A monastic community gathered round him, with a consistent spiritual life, the reading of the Fathers and work for Harbin's orphanage and schools. His own guide in those years was the blessed Metropolitan Anthony Khrapovitsky, who kept a particularly warm relation with him until his death in 1936. Father Philaret had a truly merciful heart: he gave away everything he had, sometimes even his clothes, and relieved the needy however he could. He loved the word of God greatly and knew the whole Gospel by heart, and his name was known far beyond the bounds of the Harbin province. He was made abbot in 1933 and archimandrite in 1937.

In 1945 the Soviet army defeated the Japanese and the communist regime was established in China, and for the Russians who could emigrate to the West or to Australia a time of pain and trial began. The Soviet government began requiring Russian emigrants to take Soviet passports, to show that there was no oppression of believers in the USSR, and the Journal of the Moscow Patriarchate declared that there was no persecution of the Church there and that the only conflict was with counter-revolutionaries. Archimandrite Philaret refused to commemorate the atheist regime and declared openly what the real state of the Russian land was. For that he was called in for interrogation many times and persecuted, and his house was set on fire with the windows and the door sealed first; the Lord saved the life of his shepherd, and he escaped by jumping from the first floor through the flames, but with serious burns to the lower part of his face and to the vertebrae of his neck. The good shepherd did not abandon his flock until everyone who had managed to get a visa was out of China, so that he left for Hong Kong only in 1962.

He settled soon after in Brisbane in Australia, and in 1963 became a bishop there. In 1964 he attended the Synod of the Russian Hierarchs of the Diaspora in New York. Metropolitan Anastasius was then very old and about to retire; the election of his successor was held, and the two candidates tied. To keep peace in the Church, [John Maximovitch](/saints/john-maximovitch), archbishop of Shanghai and San Francisco, whose memory the page puts on 2 July, proposed a third candidate, the relatively unknown Bishop Philaret, the most recently consecrated of them; and so in 1964 the Russian Church Abroad appointed Metropolitan Philaret its third primate.

As head of that Church he stood firm on the independence of Orthodoxy from the Moscow patriarchate, which had been subjected to the atheist regime, while labouring hard for the peace of the Orthodox and to tame the discord inside the Russian Church Abroad. He worked gently but zealously for the keeping of the patristic tradition and defended Orthodoxy from the danger of the heresy of ecumenism, refuting the branch theory. In his years as primate the sanctity of [John of Kronstadt](/saints/john-of-kronstadt) was proclaimed, in 1964, and of [Herman of Alaska](/saints/herman-of-alaska) in 1970, [Xenia of Petersburg](/saints/xenia-of-saint-petersburg) in 1978, the New Martyrs and Confessors of Russia in 1981, and the venerable [Paisius Velichkovsky](/saints/paisius-of-neamt) in 1982.

He loved monasticism, the page says, because it lets a man give himself entirely to God: according to the teaching of the holy Fathers, in his own words, the monastic way is in itself the straight road to the heavenly kingdom, when it is completed as it should be. His sermons were substantial, simple and clear, and many witnesses said his eloquence was the equal of John Chrysostom's. He approached the holy mysteries with particular awe, reminding his flock that holy communion is a fire, that nobody may be forbidden it where there is no serious canonical impediment, and that a Christian should receive as often as possible — which is why, he said, it is given before the Beautiful Gate, for that signifies our nearness to the kingdom of heaven and urges us upward.

A pastor full of care and love for others, he was extremely strict with himself: he slept two or three hours a day, ate very little, watched over the sorrows and joys of his flock with a father's interest and kept vigil praying for it. His spiritual children witnessed many of his wonders, during his life and after his repose, and their testimonies speak eloquently, the page says, of the great power of his prayers. His life, equal to the angels', was crowned by his blessed repose on 8 November 1985, the feast of the Archangel Michael and all the bodiless powers.

He was buried at the Holy Trinity monastery of Jordanville. Thirteen years after his death it was decided to move him to the crypt beneath the Holy Trinity sanctuary in Jordanville, and his grave was opened on 28 October 1998. Archbishop Laurus of Syracuse and Holy Trinity, Bishop Hilarion of Manhattan, the archimandrite Luke and the brethren were present, and witnessed the complete incorruption of his relic: the body was white and even soft, and the vestments, the cross and the icon of the Mother of God were still bright. The faithful children of the Russian Orthodox Church Abroad received that incorruption as a wonder granted by God for the strengthening of their faith.
