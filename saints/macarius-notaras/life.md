# Macarius Notaras

Macarius Notaras, Michael in the world, was born in 1731 at Trikala in Corinthia, of the great Notaras family; his father Georgantas was a notable of the province, and he was one of nine children born to him and his wife Anastasia. He learned his first letters from Eustathios, a teacher out of Kephallenia, at the monastery of the Mother of God. As steward of the family's properties he was not a good one: he could not bring himself to collect the dues from the villagers, and gave away to the poor what he had of his own.

His father would not let him become a monk at the monastery of the Great Cave, and he gave himself instead wholly to the Scriptures and the fathers. When his own teacher died he took on his work, teaching the pupils of Corinth without pay for six years; his fellow citizens wondered at it. In 1764, when the metropolitan of the place fell asleep, all the clergy and the people of those parts asked the Ecumenical Patriarchate to ordain their teacher bishop of Corinth. He took their one voice for the will of God, and at his consecration received the name Macarius.

As bishop he had a wide work of renewal. He cared for the education of the clergy, kept from serving those who could not read, those who were very old and those entangled in politics, was careful whom he ordained and attentive to the canons, built schools, and preached repentance to an untaught people. The Russo-Turkish war of 1768 to 1774 broke off that work: after the bloody Turkish reprisals he fled with his family first to Zakynthos, where he taught the people for three years, and then to Hydra, and was never again allowed to return to his diocese.

Without having resigned he was replaced by another bishop, whom the Patriarchate was pressed into appointing, and from then on he signed himself «Macarie din Corint», Macarius of Corinth. In Hydra he met the layman Nicholas, afterwards [Nicodemus the Hagiorite](/saints/nicodemus-the-hagiorite), and the hermit Silvester. From Hydra he went to Chios, and in 1777 to the Holy Mountain, to the Vatopedi cell of Saint Anthony; there he met Nicodemus again and gave him the manuscripts of the Philokalia to correct, to introduce, and to furnish with short lives of the fathers whose texts it carries.

He had copied the whole of it out of manuscripts in the Athonite libraries, above all Vatopedi's, as Paisius Velichkovsky writes in a letter the page quotes; its nucleus, the page says on the authority of Professor A. Tachiaos, was the Vatopedi Codex 605 of the thirteenth century. The Philokalia was printed in 1782, and he is reckoned the beginner of the movement that takes its name from it. He was the spiritual head of the Athonias school, whose director, Athanasius of Paros, was afterwards his companion in Chios and his biographer; he took the great schema at the cell of Saint George Skourtaion.

One of the chief men of the Kollyvades on Athos, he moved to Chios, then to Patmos, where he stayed ten years at the monastery of All Saints of Koumanas, and at last back to Chios for the twelve years that remained to him, in asceticism, study, writing and teaching. His poor cell beside the church of the Apostles Peter and Paul became a school, a hospital and a place of confession for the islanders. In the years of the nation's servitude he prepared Christians for martyrdom, and was the teacher and confessor of several new martyrs: [Polydorus](/saints/polydorus-the-neomartyr) the Cypriot, who died in 1794, Theodore of Byzantium in 1795, Mark the New in 1801, Demetrius of the Peloponnese in 1803 and Angelis of Argos in 1813.

While he was composing the New Limonarion he fell ill, and his right side was paralysed. For eight months he remained in unbroken prayer and tears, saying that he had not yet made a beginning of repentance, communicating daily and counselling all who came for his blessing. He fell asleep on 17 April 1805 and was buried in the courtyard of the church of the Apostles Peter and Paul in Chios, where his grave still is. His relics were translated in 1808, and many healings were reported by those who touched them; the people of Chios honoured him as a saint while he was still alive.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and fills in the wandering years. saint.gr says he was born at Trikala in Corinthia in 1731 of parents of the famous line of the Notaras, his father Georgios and his mother Anastasia, and that his baptismal name was Michael. He learnt his first letters at home, but had a great zeal for the monastic life and ran away secretly to the Mega Spilaio, until his father found him and brought him back. When Corinth was for a time without a teacher he took the teaching of the young on himself without pay, and so distinguished himself and was so loved for the modesty of his life that after the death of their bishop Parthenius in 1764 the Corinthians proposed him — then still a layman — to the patriarch Samuel as successor, and he was raised to the throne of Corinth.

When the Russo-Turkish war began in 1768 he had to take refuge with his family on Zakynthos and from there on Hydra, where he lived quietly in a monastery; and when things had settled the Holy Synod of Constantinople elected a new bishop of Corinth, perhaps, the page says, because Macarius had left his province. He visited Hydra and Chios, and from Chios went to the Holy Mountain in 1777, settling in the cell of Saint Anthony belonging to his countryman the elder David, where he met Nikodemos the Hagiorite again. The Athonite community was then torn by the quarrel over memorials and kollyva — whether they might be served on a Sunday — and the state of it disheartened him; fearing for his own life among the disorders he went back to Chios, and then to Patmos, where he founded the kathisma of All Saints.

After dividing his patrimony he returned to Chios, went to Smyrna to meet I. Mavrokordatos with a letter from the notables of Chios, and came back to Chios again. He chose for his dwelling the little church of the Apostles Peter and Paul on the north-western skirts of Vrontados, at the foot of Aipos, and lived there about twelve years, finding his peace of soul in the company of Athanasios Parios, who wrote his life, and the hieromonk Nikephoros of Chios. He died on 17 April 1805, and his body was buried on the south side of the precinct of that church; the translation of his relics followed in 1808.
