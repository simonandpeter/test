# Antony of Thessalonica

saint.gr's calendar for 2 Νοεμβρίου names «Άγιος Αντώνιος ο ομολογητής, επίσκοπος Θεσσαλονίκης», and dates him «(; - 844)»; and its page for him is an argument rather than a narrative. The most important source we have for the person of Antony, it says, is a long and self-contained passage of the Life of Theodora of Thessalonica, whose author Gregory, a cleric of that metropolis, turned deliberately aside from his subject in paragraphs 10 to 18 to give what he knew about an iconophile church leader otherwise little known.

The occasion for that digression was Antony's kinship with Theodora, which the Life attests twice: once indirectly, where Theodora's daughter Theopiste is given into the little monastery of the Evangelist Luke, whose abbess was Antony's sister Catherine and herself a kinswoman of the blessed one; and once directly, where Gregory says he wished to go through the lives of Theodora's near relations and of her high family. From that kinship, the page says, Aegina can be inferred as Antony's birthplace, since Theodora came from there; but when and why he and his sister moved to Thessalonica cannot be determined.

His learning and his love of study were not confined to Christian letters, the page goes on: he became master of the general education of his time as well, and embraced the monastic life young, and the breadth of that education armed him with a high dogmatic panoply. The result, according to his biographer, was his election to the archiepiscopal throne of the metropolis of Dyrrachium. Since nothing is known of his predecessors there, the page fixes the date of that election only by a terminus ante quem: the rekindling of iconoclasm by the emperor Leo V the Armenian, which the sources unanimously put two years after his accession, that is in 815.

For the Dyrrachium years the page has one other source: two letters of Theodore the Studite to him, numbers 462 and 542 in the recent critical edition, the first sent before 815 and the second datable between May and November 826, which is the month of Theodore's death. Both are warm and tend to praise of Antony's person, which lets the page suggest that the two men were joined by an old personal friendship.

For the end the page turns to archaeology. The burial of the archbishop Methodios in the same grave where Antony's body had been laid revealed to those present — Gregory among them, probably as a deacon — that the relic had been kept incorrupt for forty-six years: «εὕρομεν τὸ πανάγιον ᾿Αντωνίου σῶμα μεθ᾿ ὧν κεκόσμητο ἀρχιερατικῶν ἐπίπλων σχεδὸν ἅπαν ὁλόκληρον καὶ ἀμείωτον». The chapel of John the Baptist that held the grave lay on the left side of the church of Saint Demetrius, and G. and M. Sotiriou found three chapels on the north side of that basilica, the oldest of them a funerary annexe with two brick-built graves in its floor; a later excavation by S. Pelekanidis brought another funerary annexe to light with eight box graves beneath it, two by two.

The two things by which Gregory certified his sanctity, the page says, were his confession of the Orthodox faith and teaching on the honour of the holy icons — which Gregory documents by quoting Antony's refutation addressed to the emperor Leo V — and the incorruption of his relic, as it had been plainly revealed to Gregory himself. Both were criteria of sanctity in wide use during and after the iconoclast period. His name was never written into the Synaxarion of the Church of Constantinople nor into the Typikon of the Great Church, the page admits, but it has been taken into later lists of the saints of Thessalonica and into the second office written by the hymnographer Gerasimos Mikragiannanitis in honour of all the saints of that city. The one known image of him is a modern wall painting in the metropolitan church of Saint Gregory Palamas at Thessalonica.
