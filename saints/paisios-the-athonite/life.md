# Paisios the Athonite

Paisios was born to devout parents, Prodromos and Eulampia Eznepidis, at
Pharasa in Cappadocia on 25 July 1924, a few days before the people of
Pharasa left their ancestral ground for Greece; at his baptism Arsenius of
Cappadocia, the priest of Pharasa, full of years and of holiness, named him
Arsenius — to leave a monk behind in his own place, as he put it. In Greece
the family settled at Konitsa in Epirus, where he passed his childhood and
youth, nursed on the stories of Arsenius’s wonderful life; from the age of
five he said he would be a monk, and once he could read his favourite
occupation was the lives of the saints, whose ascetic struggles he imitated
with warm zeal. After the ordinary schooling he would not go on with letters
but chose to imitate Christ and learned the carpenter’s trade, which he
practised with care and skill. At fifteen he was granted a sight of the Lord
for a single generous thought, by which he had repelled a demonic assault of
the temptation to unbelief, and from then the flame of the love of God and
the longing for the monastic life burned in him more than ever. Then came
the years of the occupation and the civil war; as citizen and as soldier —
he served from 1945 to 1949 — he showed matchless courage and
self-sacrifice, ready at any moment to give his life for the safety of
others, and often, under the storm of deadly fire, saved many soldiers by
his fervent prayers and was himself saved in a wonderful way. Most of his
service was as a wireless operator, and many books about him call him God’s
wireless operator; he himself, to someone who doubted the use of the
monastic life, answered from his own speciality that monks are God’s
wireless operators, meaning their fervent prayer and their care for the rest
of mankind. After these adventures he wished to join the angelic order of
monks on the wings that divine love gives, and went to the Holy Mountain
looking for a guide to the life of stillness in God; he could not fulfil his
longing at once, for his family fell into great want and called him to help,
so he went back to Konitsa and worked as a carpenter, and after three years,
in 1953, at twenty-nine, he left the things of the world and returned to the
Athonite state.

Having gone round sketes and huts, he followed a venerable elder’s advice
and joined the brotherhood of Esphigmenou, known then for its strict order,
where he lived in complete obedience and gave himself to immoderate
asceticism, exceeding in labours for the sake of Christ and his brethren,
and on 27 March 1954 was tonsured a rasophore with the name Abercius; with
the longing for the quiet and untroubled life unquenched in him, he took the
abbot’s blessing and went to Philotheou, then idiorrhythmic, where he
prepared for the hermit’s life under a discerning and wise elder, Symeon,
and on 12 March 1956 was tonsured to the small schema with the name Paisius,
after Metropolitan Paisius II of Caesarea, a fellow-countryman. In August
1958, obeying a divine will, he settled not in the desert he had prepared
for but in the ruined monastery of the Panagia of Stomio near Konitsa, where
he lived four years the life of an angel, wrestling with temptations, doing
good to the people of the district, saving many from the teaching of the
Protestant groups active there, and restoring the monastery with great toil.
In 1962, the restoration finished and the danger from the heterodox groups
gone, he begged God amid the temptations that besieged him daily to show him
the road to take, and received as sent from God a hierodeacon’s invitation
to accompany him to Sinai, the mountain God trod; there, on that waterless
and dry ground, in the cell of Galaction and Episteme, he lived at last what
he had longed for for years, solitude with God, and struggling with much
humility, continual fasting, unceasing vigil and constant prayer, overcame
the snares of the enemy of good and enjoyed union with God, full of the
grace of divine consolation in the furnace of the comfortless desert; he
became especially dear to the Bedouin, giving them food bought with the
money from the wooden crosses he carved and sold to pilgrims. He would have
had no reason to leave that arena of virtue had not bodily illness, from the
harsh climate, forced him back to his homeland. Returning to the Holy
Mountain in 1964 he did not lessen his ascetic labours for all the weakness
of his body, keeping the old fervour in his spirit, and living as a stranger
and sojourner on earth became a citizen of heaven: with practice as the
ascent to contemplation he reached high measures and became a sharer of
divine mysteries, delighted in the beauty of the Lord, received the blessing
of the Mother of God, conversed with saints who appeared before him, saw his
guardian angel, heard angelic hymns and was lit by the heavenly light.

In 1966 he fell gravely ill and was taken into the Papanikolaou hospital for
chest diseases, where an operation removed part of his lungs; until he
recovered he was cared for at the hesychasterion of St John the Theologian
at Souroti, and back on the Mountain he moved in 1967 to Katounakia, to the
Lavriote cell of Hypatius at Vlachika, on 12 August 1968 entered
Stavronikita and lived at its cell of the Holy Cross, and in 1979 left it
for the abandoned Panagouda, where he worked hard to make a cell for himself
and stayed to the end of his life. From then crowds of people visited him,
so many that signs were put up marking the road to his cell, so that
visitors should not trouble the other monks, and he received a great many
letters — from which, he said with sorrow, he learned only of divorces and
of sicknesses of soul and body. For all the burden of his day he kept up the
intense ascetic life, resting two or three hours a day, and went on
receiving and trying to help his visitors, and used to make little stamped
icons to give them as a blessing. To the daily weariness came illness: in
his last years the pains of colitis, which left him permanent dyspepsia, of
a hernia and above all of the cancer diagnosed in him grew and grew, and he
stayed calm, bore it without complaint, and went on praying for everyone.
After 1993 he had haemorrhages and refused to be treated — everything will
be settled with the soil, he said; in November of that year he left the Holy
Mountain for the last time for Souroti, for the feast of St Arsenius on 10
November, stayed a few days, fell ill as he was preparing to leave, and was
taken to the Theagenio hospital, where a tumour of the colon was found,
which he took as the granting of a request he had made to God and as good
for his soul’s health. He was operated on on 4 February 1994; the disease
spread to his lungs and liver, and though he announced his wish to return to
the Mountain on 13 June, high fever and breathlessness kept him. At the end
of June the doctors told him he had two or three weeks at most; on Monday 11
July, the feast of St Euphemia, he communicated for the last time, kneeling
before his bed, and in his last days chose to take no medicines or
painkillers despite the terrible pains of his illness. He died on Tuesday 12
July 1994 at eleven in the morning and was buried at the hesychasterion of
St John the Theologian at Souroti near Thessalonica. On 13 January 2015 the
Holy and Sacred Synod of the Ecumenical Patriarchate, unanimously accepting
the report of its canonical committee, entered the monk Paisius the Athonite
in the calendar of the Orthodox Church. He is kept on 12 July; this entry
stands under 20 September, where the Greek synaxarion’s day list carried him
when the corpus was first read.
