# Mark of Ephesus

Mark Eugenikos was born at Constantinople in 1392 and named Manuel, the son of George and Maria, both of believing family and honoured stock. The eastern part of the empire had been taken by the Turks, and the emperors were seeking an understanding with the popes — a council that would prepare the union of the Catholics with the Orthodox Church, and with it armies from the monarchies of the West. After Murad II failed to take Constantinople in 1422, John VIII Palaeologus resumed the negotiations with Pope Eugene IV; the patriarchs of Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem would not attend and sent representatives unwillingly.

The patriarch of Alexandria chose as one of his envoys the hieromonk Mark, whose theological writings were known through the whole empire. Both the emperor and Patriarch Joseph II wished him consecrated bishop so that he might stand first among the Orthodox delegates, and at forty-six he was raised to the metropolitan see of Ephesus, left vacant by the death of Metropolitan Ioasaph. On 27 November 1437 seven hundred bishops, archimandrites, monks, priests and laymen set sail for Italy, the emperor, the patriarch and twenty-two bishops among them. The council opened on Great Wednesday, 9 April 1438, in the cathedral of Saint George at Ferrara, and after fourteen sessions Pope Eugene moved it to Florence on 12 January 1439.

The chief matters brought for examination were the procession of the Holy Spirit — that is, the Latin addition of the *filioque* to the Nicene creed — the papal primacy, purgatory, and the use of unleavened bread in the Eucharist; the emperor would not let the Greek theologians raise the distinction between the divine nature and the sanctifying energies, for fear of obstacles to the union. Mark insisted that the canons of the Church and the decisions of the last five Ecumenical Councils be read before the assembly, and many of the Catholic monks present said they had never heard such things, and marvelled at him. «Our faith», he said, «is the right confession of our Fathers. With it we hope to stand before the Lord and receive forgiveness of sins; without it, I do not know what piety could deliver us from eternal torment.» Of the papacy he wrote that for the Orthodox the Pope is as one of the patriarchs — and only if he is Orthodox.

The pope and the emperor frightened and bought the Orthodox delegates into signing, and on 5 July 1439 the Union of Florence was sealed. When the pope asked whether Mark of Ephesus had signed and was told that he had not, he cried out that nothing at all had been done. The faithful then kept away from the bishops who had signed; the priests and hierarchs left in Constantinople refused to serve with the unionists; and in April 1443 the three patriarchs of Jerusalem, Alexandria and Antioch met at Jerusalem and anathematised the council. Worn out by so many hard struggles, Mark fell gravely ill, and after fourteen days gave up his soul to Christ on 23 June 1444, in his family house at Galata in Constantinople, at fifty-two years old.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, under the name by which Greek remembers him, Mark Eugenikos. saint.gr agrees with the Romanian page on the year of his birth, 1392, and names his parents as the Romanian life does: George, chief judge, sakellion and deacon of the Great Church, and Maria, daughter of a physician. Over his memory the page sets a distich that puts his labour beside the labour of Atlas: «Κρατεί μεν Άτλας μυθικώς ώμοις πόλον, Κρατεί δ’ αληθώς Μάρκος Oρθοδοξίαν» — Atlas holds up the vault of heaven in the myth, but Mark holds up Orthodoxy in truth.
