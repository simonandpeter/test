# Timothy of Pskov

Timothy was at first a pagan and bore the name Dovmont. He came from Lithuania and was prince of Nalshensk there; and in 1265, because of the fighting among the rulers of Lithuania, he was forced to abandon his province and to settle at Pskov with three hundred families. There, saint.gr says, the grace of God overshadowed him, and he was baptised a Christian, taking the name Timothy. Very soon the people of Pskov chose him for their own prince, for his bravery and for his true Christian virtues.

He governed thirty-three years in peace and good sense. He was just, merciful and kind, open-handed, and a protector of the monasteries and the churches, and he founded a monastery himself in honour of the Nativity of the Mother of God. In 1268 he was one of the heroes of the battle against the Danish and German armies that defended the Russian land, and before every battle he would go to the church to pray and leave his sword at the step of the holy doors, taking the priest's blessing. He won his last battle against the Germans on 5 March 1299, and fell asleep in peace that same year.

saint.gr adds that he went on guarding his city after his death. When in 1480 more than a hundred thousand Germans laid siege to Pskov, he appeared to a Christian and urged them to take his icon with a cross and carry them in procession through the city; the Christians of Pskov did as their saint had told them, and the Germans withdrew.

The Russian church calendar keeps him on 20 мая ст. ст. and gives him a long life. He was a Lithuanian, prince of Nalshany, and at first a zealous pagan; in 1265, escaping the feuds of the Lithuanian princes, he had to flee Lithuania and came to Pskov with three hundred Lithuanian households, and the land of Pskov became his second country. There, in the chronicler’s phrase, «дохнула на него благодать Божия» — the grace of God breathed upon him — when he received holy baptism with his whole retinue and was given the name Тимофей. Within a year the men of Pskov chose him their prince for his valour and his Christian virtues, and he governed the city thirty-three years: the only prince in the whole history of Pskov who managed to live so long in peace and agreement with the Pskov veche.

He was just, and strict in watching over the justice of others; he gave alms generously and took in the poor and the wayfarer; he kept the feasts of the Church reverently, was a patron of churches and monasteries and himself founded a house in honour of the Nativity of the Most Holy Mother of God. By his marriage to the daughter of the grand prince Димитрий, granddaughter of the prince Александр Невский, he came into the Russian grand-princely family. His chief service, the calendar says, was that for many years he reliably defended the north-western frontier of the Russian state. In 1268 he was one of the heroes of the battle of Rakovor, where the Russian host beat the Danish and German armies; before every battle he came to the church, laid his sword at the foot of the holy altar and took the blessing of his confessor, who girded the sword on him.

He made the fortress of Pskov unassailable, and the stone wall he raised beside the Krom at the end of the thirteenth century was named the Dovmont wall, and the ground it enclosed is called the Dovmont town to this day. In thanksgiving he had the habit of building, beside the Kremlin, a church in honour of whatever saint’s day he had won a victory on, so that the small area of the Dovmont town was covered over with churches. His last victory he won on 5 March 1299 on the bank of the river Velikaya, where with a small company he broke a large German force: the Livonian knights had fallen suddenly on the settlement outside Pskov, seized and burned the Snetogorsky and Mirozhsky monasteries and dealt cruelly with the monks, and he went out to meet them without waiting for the Pskov army to gather and drove them beyond the borders of the Russian land.

A few months later he died and was buried in the Trinity cathedral of Pskov, and the chronicle says that there was then great grief in Pskov among men and women and little children for their good lord. They remembered how he had led them into battle: «Добрые мужи псковичи! Кто из вас стар, тот мне отец, кто молод, тот брат. Постоим за Святую Троицу!» — good men of Pskov, whoever of you is old is my father and whoever is young my brother; let us stand for the Holy Trinity. Soon after his death he began to be honoured as a saint, and in 1480, when more than a hundred thousand Germans were besieging the city, he appeared in a dream to a townsman and told them to take the pall from his tomb and carry it three times round the city with the crosses and not be afraid; they did it, and the Germans withdrew. A service was composed for him after that deliverance, and his battle sword hung beside his relics in the Trinity cathedral, where it was given to the princes of Pskov at their enthronement.
