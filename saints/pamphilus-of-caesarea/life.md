# Pamphilus of Caesarea

Twelve martyrs suffered for Christ at Caesarea in Palestine, and the life counts them as answering to the twelve prophets and the twelve apostles. First of them, in number and in order, was Pamphilus, presbyter of that city: a man of Beirut by birth, trained from his youth in the learning of the world, full of spiritual philosophy, marked out by a good life and glorious for his confession of Christ the Lord. They lived, the life says, in the reign of Diocletian.

With [Valens](/saints/valens-the-deacon), deacon of the church of Aelia, and [Paul](/saints/paul-of-jamnia) of Jamnia, Pamphilus was put to various tortures by the governor Urban and then thrown into prison, where the three stayed two years, until Firmilian succeeded Urban in the governorship. In that time five young men, brothers in body and in spirit, who had escorted a hundred and thirty confessors banished from Egypt to the gold mines of Cilicia, came through Caesarea on the way home, confessed themselves Christians at the gate, and were shut up as robbers.

The next day, which was 16 February, the five Egyptians were brought out of prison together with Pamphilus, Valens and Paul, and set before Firmilian. The young men gave their names as Elias, Jeremiah, Isaiah, Samuel and Daniel — prophets' names they had taken in place of the idolaters' names their parents had given them — and named the Jerusalem above as their homeland. The governor, who knew the city only as Aelia, took this for a Christian fortress raised against Rome, and when he could get nothing more from them had all five beheaded.

Then he asked after Pamphilus the presbyter, Valens the deacon and Paul, and finding that they had already been tortured enough two years before under Urban and held in prison ever since, he did not torture them again, but only asked whether they would obey the emperor's command. They would not, and he sentenced them to the sword as well. Porphyrius, a servant of Pamphilus's household, eighteen years old and much loved by his master for his purity, cried out from the crowd that the martyrs' bodies should be buried, and was flogged until his bones showed and then burned.

[Seleucus](/saints/seleucus-of-cappadocia) brought Pamphilus word of that end while the condemned were still asking their executioners for time to pray, and Pamphilus gave thanks to God for it. The bodies, thrown outside the city, lay four days; when the pagans saw that neither dogs nor birds nor beasts would touch them, they ordered them taken up, and the faithful buried them with honour. Doxologia names Diocletian's reign but prints no year, and the page it offers as his troparion is a recording in Greek with no text.

The Greek calendar keeps the company on the same day and names twelve where the Romanian named four. Its line is «Άγιος Πάμφιλος και οι συν αυτώ Μάρτυρες», Pamphilus and the martyrs with him, and the page behind it lists them: Pamphilus, [Daniel](/saints/daniel-the-egyptian), [Elias](/saints/elias-the-egyptian), [Isaiah](/saints/isaiah-the-egyptian), [Theodulus](/saints/theodulus-companion-of-pamphilus), [Jeremiah](/saints/jeremiah-the-egyptian), [Julian](/saints/julian-companion-of-pamphilus), Valens, Paul, [Porphyrius](/saints/porphyrius-servant-of-pamphilus), [Samuel](/saints/samuel-the-egyptian) and Seleucus. It puts the martyrdom under the emperor Diocletian, whose reign it gives as 284 to 305, says that they came from various places but that the love and faith of Christ joined them, and that working at Caesarea in Palestine they confessed Christ before the eparch Firmilianus, who spent every effort on persuading them to deny their faith and could not move them from the piety of their fathers; so he ordered them put to death after being tortured first.

The page then divides the deaths. Elias, Pamphilus, Valens, Paul, Seleucus, Jeremiah, Isaiah, Samuel and Daniel were beheaded with the sword; Porphyrius, Pamphilus's servant, was seized while he was searching for his master's body and was burned alive together with the martyr Julian; and Theodulus they crucified upon a tree. So, it says, the saints were martyred and were added to the choir of the contenders of Christ. Of its five couplets, one is for Pamphilus alone — loving you, Word, above all, Pamphilus counted even the cutting of his head a thing to love — one for Valens, Paul and Seleucus, one for Porphyrius and Julian, one for Theodulus, and one for the five it marks as Egyptians, Elias, Jeremiah, Isaiah, Samuel and Daniel: the callings of prophets and the deaths of martyrs are the boast of five beheaded martyrs. A last line counts the day: on the sixteenth the sword's edge cut Pamphilus.

Of the eight the Romanian calendar did not name, this corpus now keeps each as his own folder, and the servant the page gives him — Porphyrius, who died searching for his master's body — is among them.

The Serbian church keeps him on the same day, in one entry for the whole company: the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović heads it „Светих дванаест мученика.“ — the holy twelve martyrs who suffered in the time of the emperor Diocletian — and then names all twelve in its body. He is the first of them: a presbyter of the church at Caesarea in Palestine, a learned and devout man, who corrected the text of the New Testament of the errors of its various copyists, copied that saving book himself and gave it to those who wanted it.
