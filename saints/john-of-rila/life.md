# John of Rila

John of Rila, the great ascetic and lamp of the Orthodox Church, was born at Skrino near Sofia, in the days of Tsar Boris, of poor but honest parents. After their death he became a monk and withdrew to a wild mountain, where he began his struggle in a cave, suffering many assaults from demons and from men, from robbers and from his own kin. Then he moved to the Rila mountain and settled in a hollow tree, living on grass and on the beans that by God’s providence began to grow nearby; for many years he saw no human face, until — again by God’s providence — shepherds searching for lost sheep found him.

So the saint became known, and people began to come to him for help in sickness and trouble; the Bulgarian tsar Peter himself visited him and asked his counsel, many zealots of the spiritual life settled near him, and a church and monastery soon rose there. He reposed in peace on 18 August 946, in his seventieth year, and appeared to his disciples after his death. His relics were taken first to Sofia, then to Hungary, then to Tarnovo — the Sretensky calendar keeps the day for that translation, in 1238 — and at last to the monastery of Rila, where they lie today; through the centuries, the Prologue says, the monastery of Rila was a lighthouse, a place of wonderworking power and spiritual comfort for the Christian people of Bulgaria, above all in the hard years under the Turks.

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 18 August, the day of the translation of his relics. Doxologia.ro's life calls him great among fasters, of the borders of the city of Sredets, from the village called Skrino, in the reign of Peter and of the Greek emperor Constantine Diogenes; it says that after his parents died he gave away all they left to the poor, took the monastic habit and went out of his own country with nothing on him but a leather garment, and lived on a high desert mountain eating wild herbs until robbers drove him away by night. He then settled in the wilderness of Rila and lived in a hollow tree, in fasting, prayer and tears. The same life ends with the translation of his relics by the emperor John Asen, who, coming to Sredets and hearing what was told of him, venerated the reliquary and had the relics carried in glory by the patriarch Basil to his imperial city of Tarnovo, and laid in the church built for them.

doxologia.ro's Romanian life tells the Hungarian translation at more length. The king carried the reliquary off to Esztergom, whose archbishop would not go and venerate it, saying he knew of no such man in the old writings, and whose tongue was bound at once; he ran to the coffin, kissed it, asked pardon, and had his speech given back. The king then adorned the reliquary with silver and gold and sent the relics home to Sredets in 1090, whence the tsar Ioan Asan later brought them with honour to his capital at Tarnovo.
