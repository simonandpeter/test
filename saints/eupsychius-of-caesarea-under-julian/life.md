# Eupsychius of Caesarea (under Julian)

Eupsychius was born and brought up at Caesarea in Cappadocia, of well-born parents of patrician rank, and passed the years of his youth blamelessly, in what the life calls the whole Christian wisdom. He married according to the law of marriage in the reign of Julian the Apostate, whom the page names only as «potrivnicul lui Dumnezeu, Iulian Paravatul» — the adversary of God — and before the wedding feast was over he had shown his zeal for his God. There was in that city a famous temple of an idol called Tyche, that is Fortune, where Julian himself sacrificed whenever he came to Caesarea.

The feast of that idol fell on the day of his own wedding. Seeing the pagans going up to sacrifice, Eupsychius took a crowd of Christians with him, broke the images and pulled the temple down to its foundations. Knowing what would come of it, he gave away his property to the poor and prepared himself in fasting and prayer. Julian, hearing of it, turned his anger on the whole city: leading citizens were killed or exiled and their goods plundered, the churches of Caesarea were stripped, the clergy were forced into the army, the city lost the honoured name it had held since the reign of Claudius and was demoted to a village under its old name of Maza, and a heavy tax was laid on the Christians.

Eupsychius, as the first cause of the demolition, was held in chains and brought out again and again to be questioned and tortured; he would have been forgiven, the life says, had he only worshipped the idols. He was hung on the rack and torn with iron combs to the inward parts, and was strengthened by an angel that appeared to him in his torments; at the last he was beheaded, and instead of blood milk and water ran from the wounds. The faithful took his body and buried it with honour, and after Julian's death the people of Caesarea built a church over his grave, from which the page says healings flowed.

The same life goes on to tell how [Basil the Great](/saints/basil-the-great) met Julian on his way to Persia with three barley loaves and was mocked with a bundle of hay, and how the emperor swore he would plough Caesarea under on his way home and instead perished there. A second Eupsychius of Caesarea, put to death under Hadrian, is [a different man](/saints/eupsychius-of-caesarea-under-hadrian), kept on 7 September; this one belongs to Julian's reign and to 9 April.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and tells the same deed. saint.gr says he lived in the years of Julian the Apostate, and that being newly married did not stop him from showing his great love for Christ in act: at Caesarea in Cappadocia Julian had built an idol of the goddess Fortune which the idolaters worshipped every day for all to see, and Eupsychius, taking some other bold young men with him, went and pulled it down. When he was arrested he told the official that Julian had no right to do the things of idolatry in Christian Caesarea.

He knew beforehand, the page says, that the sentence would be a condemning one, and so it was: those who had acted with him were condemned to torments and to exile, while he himself, as the leader of the deed, was condemned to death by beheading. So Eupsychius was numbered with that company of men of whom the Lord said, «Ό άπολέσας την ψυχήν αυτού ένεκεν εμού εϋρήσει αυτήν» (Matthew 10.39) — he who loses his life for my sake will find it. The page's couplets play on his name, and one of them says the sword cut his neck about the ninth of the month, which is the day the calendar keeps him.
