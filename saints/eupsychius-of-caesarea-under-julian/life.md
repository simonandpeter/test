# Eupsychius of Caesarea (under Julian)

Eupsychius was born and brought up at Caesarea in Cappadocia, of well-born parents of patrician rank, and passed the years of his youth blamelessly, in what the life calls the whole Christian wisdom. He married according to the law of marriage in the reign of Julian the Apostate, whom the page names only as «potrivnicul lui Dumnezeu, Iulian Paravatul» — the adversary of God — and before the wedding feast was over he had shown his zeal for his God. There was in that city a famous temple of an idol called Tyche, that is Fortune, where Julian himself sacrificed whenever he came to Caesarea.

The feast of that idol fell on the day of his own wedding. Seeing the pagans going up to sacrifice, Eupsychius took a crowd of Christians with him, broke the images and pulled the temple down to its foundations. Knowing what would come of it, he gave away his property to the poor and prepared himself in fasting and prayer. Julian, hearing of it, turned his anger on the whole city: leading citizens were killed or exiled and their goods plundered, the churches of Caesarea were stripped, the clergy were forced into the army, the city lost the honoured name it had held since the reign of Claudius and was demoted to a village under its old name of Maza, and a heavy tax was laid on the Christians.

Eupsychius, as the first cause of the demolition, was held in chains and brought out again and again to be questioned and tortured; he would have been forgiven, the life says, had he only worshipped the idols. He was hung on the rack and torn with iron combs to the inward parts, and was strengthened by an angel that appeared to him in his torments; at the last he was beheaded, and instead of blood milk and water ran from the wounds. The faithful took his body and buried it with honour, and after Julian's death the people of Caesarea built a church over his grave, from which the page says healings flowed.

The same life goes on to tell how [Basil the Great](/saints/basil-the-great) met Julian on his way to Persia with three barley loaves and was mocked with a bundle of hay, and how the emperor swore he would plough Caesarea under on his way home and instead perished there. A second Eupsychius of Caesarea, put to death under Hadrian, is [a different man](/saints/eupsychius-of-caesarea-under-hadrian), kept on 7 September; this one belongs to Julian's reign and to 9 April.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and tells the same deed. saint.gr says he lived in the years of Julian the Apostate, and that being newly married did not stop him from showing his great love for Christ in act: at Caesarea in Cappadocia Julian had built an idol of the goddess Fortune which the idolaters worshipped every day for all to see, and Eupsychius, taking some other bold young men with him, went and pulled it down. When he was arrested he told the official that Julian had no right to do the things of idolatry in Christian Caesarea.

He knew beforehand, the page says, that the sentence would be a condemning one, and so it was: those who had acted with him were condemned to torments and to exile, while he himself, as the leader of the deed, was condemned to death by beheading. So Eupsychius was numbered with that company of men of whom the Lord said, «Ό άπολέσας την ψυχήν αυτού ένεκεν εμού εϋρήσει αυτήν» (Matthew 10.39) — he who loses his life for my sake will find it. The page's couplets play on his name, and one of them says the sword cut his neck about the ninth of the month, which is the day the calendar keeps him.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same day and tells the same deed, with some things of its own. He was of noble birth and well brought up in the devout faith, it says; in the time of the emperor Julian the Apostate, when [Basil the Great](/saints/basil-the-great) was governing the Church of God at Caesarea, this Eupsychius entered into marriage with a maiden of good family — and not a single day of married life was given him, for at the time of his wedding there fell a pagan feast with sacrifice to the idol Fortune. Eupsychius went with his companions and broke all the idols in the temple, and pulled the temple itself down. Hearing of it, Julian was greatly angered and ordered that the authors of the deed be beheaded, that many Christians be taken into the army, that an enormous tax be laid on the Christians, that the temple of Fortune be built again at the Christians' own expense, and that the city be stripped of the honourable name Caesarea, which Caesar Claudius had given it, and be called by its former name of Maza. Eupsychius was first bound to a tree and cruelly tortured, and then beheaded, in the year 362 — the only year any source read for this folder gives him, and the tree is this entry's own where the Romanian life has the rack and the iron combs.

The entry closes with the exchange this life has above in summary, and gives the words. A little after the martyrdom the wicked emperor passed through that city on his road to Persia, against which he was making war; Basil came out to meet him and brought out three barley loaves in token of honour and hospitality, and the emperor ordered that a handful of hay be given him in return. You mock us, O emperor, Basil said: we brought you bread, which we eat, and you give us cattle fodder, which you cannot by your own authority turn into food for men. Know, the emperor answered, that I will feed you with that hay when I come back from Persia. But the malicious apostate never came back alive out of Persia, the entry ends, for he perished there by a deserved and unnatural death.

**The Russian calendar keeps him on the same menologion day**, and prints him first of the three lines it gives that day: «Мч. Евпсихия (362).» — the year this folder already carries, and the first source read for it that prints that year in the same place as the commemoration rather than inside a life. The name link behind the line, name page 696, gives the calendar’s own nominative with both epithets, «Евпсихий Кесарийский (Каппадокийский), мч.», which is the form stored here; the bare «Евпсихий» was not stored, because it is the `ru` form the other Eupsychius of Caesarea, kept on 7 September, already holds.

Its life page tells the deed this folder is built on and adds a reign and a threat. Julian is given dates here, «(361 - 363)», and Eupsychius entered into Christian marriage within them; there was then at Caesarea a pagan temple of the goddess Fortuna, much honoured by Julian the Apostate, and while his wedding celebration was going on the pagans arranged a sacrifice to her. He took fire for the Lord, gathered the people and destroyed the temple, knowing that punishment must inevitably follow; he distributed the whole of his estate to the poor and prepared himself for the martyr’s contest.

The emperor’s anger then fell on the whole population of the city, and this page’s list of what that meant is the fullest read for this folder: some citizens executed, the more honoured sent into exile, the Christian clergy taken into military service and the churches’ property taken away, the city stripped of the name Caesarea — that is, Imperial — and turned into a plain settlement under its former name of Maza, and the inhabitants laid under a heavy tribute. **And a threat none of the other three sources carries**: that the emperor would destroy the city altogether if its people did not build a new pagan temple in place of the one destroyed.

Julian ordered that Eupsychius be forced by any torments to sacrifice to the idols. For many days he was racked «на виселице», on a gibbet, and torn with iron claws; his faith held, and the judge gave the order to cut the martyr down with the sword. The page then tells the meeting with [Basil the Great](/saints/basil-the-great) as this folder already has it, and gives the date of Basil’s own death and day beside his name, «(+ 379; память 1 января)», which is the day this corpus keeps him on.

**The one thing this calendar brings that no other source read for this folder has is the name of Julian’s killer.** The page closes the threat against Caesarea by saying that the emperor continued on his way and soon perished on the Persian campaign, and then names how: «В 363 году он был убит святым великомучеником Меркурием (память 24 ноября)» — in 363 he was killed by the holy great martyr [Mercurius](/saints/mercurius), whose memory this calendar gives as 24 ноября. The Romanian life has the emperor simply perish there and the Ohrid Prologue has him die a deserved and unnatural death; this one has a hand in it, and this folder gains a `related` row from it.

**That day is not the day the other folder is kept on here.** Its Greek and Romanian rows stand on 25 November, where this page gives the Julian 24 ноября — a different menologion number and not a style conversion — and its Russian row stands undocumented still, the Russian 24 ноября being no part of this day’s reading. So the day is recorded as what this page says and nothing is written into a feast from it.

A last line of this day’s page is a service and not a life, and it is not taken here. Under «Службы» the calendar links a PDF of the whole service for 9 апреля, «Мученика Евпсихия», in Church Slavonic; the day page itself prints nothing under «Молитвы», so this calendar contributes no hymn to this folder, and choosing one out of a service book is a different act from copying the troparion a calendar prints. The question is put to the author in the batch that wrote this row.
