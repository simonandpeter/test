# Lazarus of Mount Galesion

Lazarus came from a village of Asia near Magnesia; his parents, Nicetas and Irene, were of good family and God-fearing. At his birth a very bright cloud was seen in the house, and the women who had come to the mother were so frightened at the sight that they ran out; and when the cloud had lifted and they came in again they found the newborn child standing upright on his feet, his face to the east and his hands folded on his breast in the form of a cross. From then it was said by everyone that the child's life would be a famous one.

He was given to a schoolmaster to learn the holy books, and learned easily, but hated his master's grasping for money, so that whatever came into his hands he gave at once to the poor. His master reproached and beat him for it and he did not stop; and in the end the man marvelled at the deed and praised him to everyone. Wanting to go to Jerusalem and be prevented by his kin, he was put into a nearby monastery called Orovi so that the many monks there should keep him from running away; but he slipped away from parents, kin and monks alike and made for Jerusalem.

He passed by Chonae, where the Archangel Michael worked his wonder, and prayed in the Archangel's church for help on the road; and at Attalia a lawless monk travelling with him bargained in Armenian to sell the boy to the Saracens there. A Christian who knew the language understood it and warned him, and Lazarus fled into the mountain nearby, where an old hieromonk, hearing his story, told him it was not fitting to go on where he wished. He stayed with him, serving the abbot who was his spiritual father and taking instruction in the ascetic life as his wage; and when the old man died he wept three days, then went out to a cave in a place hard to walk to, to be alone with God.

He hid his works and became the more famous for it, and every day crowds climbed to him for his blessing; to make the stony path easier they piled brushwood on it and burned it, poured vinegar over the rock to soften it, and broke it up with iron tools. So that the brethren who came and wished to be monks might have somewhere to rest, he built there a church of the Mother of God and cells, and then a great and beautiful monastery, the people of the country round helping him with all their strength.

One man told him he meant to go down a precipice for wild bees, and Lazarus warned him that a great danger hung over his head if he went near it. The man went, was let down on a rope, and the rope broke before he was halfway; and after that his words were taken for a prophet's. Another confessed that he lived by stealing from houses at night, hiding what he took, and then telling the owners that the saints revealed hidden things to him — taking up a cross or an icon, leading them to the place, bidding them dig, and receiving a share of the goods out of gratitude. Lazarus told him he was mocked by the devil and took darkness for light, and warned him of Isaiah's word against those who call the bitter sweet; and the man fell at his feet, wept, and kept his promise to leave it.

To escape the glory of men, and to fulfil the longing of his youth, he left the monastery he had built and went to Jerusalem; and after worshipping at the Lord's Sepulchre he went to the lavra of Saint Sabbas, where he was received with joy and served as sacristan six whole years. Judged worthy of the priesthood and pressed to it by the abbot, he was unwilling, and was ordained at last by the Patriarch of Jerusalem, and stayed six years more. Then, seeing the monks go out into the desert for the Great Fast after the old custom and come back in the week of the Palms, he went with them without telling the abbot, and on his return was shut out of the gate and only forgiven when the steward of the great church at Jerusalem, an old friend, came and begged for him. A year later he went out again, and this time did not come back: he found a high place, built a pillar on it, and went up as a soldier to fight not flesh and blood but the spirits of wickedness, and spent many years there.

Coming down one day onto level ground he heard a voice: Lazarus, you must go to your own country. He took it for the devil's until he had heard it a second and a third time, and until the fathers living nearby told him it was of God's providence. He went to Jerusalem, took a good monk named Paul for company as far as Sebastia, and, parting from him, turned towards Rome, where he had long wished to venerate the two great apostles; on the way two great bears met him in a wood and he prayed the words of David, and was delivered.

At last he came to an elder living on Mount Galesion, who told him of a cave at the summit — in deep wilderness, the mountain hard to climb, food scarce there, those who settle in it troubled by demons, and the great Paphnutius had finished his ascetic labours in it. Lazarus chose it. The demons filled the place with strange cries as he came, and he walked about singing, unafraid, knowing their works were only apparitions. At the cave's mouth he sang his usual hymn and signed the rock with the cross, and the cross was pressed into the stone so deep and so fair that anyone would have thought a skilled mason had cut it; and seeing a little water dripping from the rock above, he settled there.

Six monks found him out — none but the elder knew where he was, and the elder had told no one, as Lazarus had charged him — and begged with tears to live with him. He received them and gave them a rule, and with their help, and money from a rich and devout woman of Ephesus, built a small church of the Saviour by the cave door. Six more came, and when the twelve had no room for cells and were living under the open sky, he went up with them to the summit and built a church of the Mother of God with cells enough around it, and other hermit cells outside the monastery, far from one another, so that the brethren could have full silence.

One summer, reading the office of the sixth hour in one of those cells in great heat, he thought he would die of thirst and lay stretched on the ground with his hands raised; and God sent an angel to a disciple living there with a vessel full of water, bidding him run to the hermit, and so he was brought back from death. The devil came into his cell once in the shape of a great black serpent to frighten him, and he signed it with the cross and it vanished. When the brethren grew too many for the house, he prayed many days with tears to know God's will and where to lay the foundations; and one night a voice called him out of his cell, and he saw a pillar of fire reaching to heaven and the angels of God rising from the earth and singing, *Let God arise and let His enemies be scattered.* On that spot he built a great and beautiful church, and a monastery round it as large and as well adorned as any.

Where the silver for it came from, the life explains. Romanos, then emperor of the Greeks, in his anger against Constantine called Monomachos, one of the first nobles of Constantinople, whom he suspected of wanting the throne, sent him into exile on the island of Mitylene. Lazarus, grieving for a man who had done the emperor no wrong, sent people to comfort him and to give him good hope that he would be the emperor's successor, which he had foreseen by the gift of God in him. In a little while Romanos died and Constantine became emperor, as the saint had prophesied, and remembered the prophecy.

At the end, his disciples came to the pillar and found his relics, and wept inconsolably, calling on their father as orphans, grieved also that he had died leaving no testament. Gregory, who loved him most, spoke to him as though he were alive and reproached him for the two sorrows. Then, a wonder: the dead and breathless man raised his right hand like a living one, put it into his bosom, drew out the testament, gave it into Gregory's hands and lay back dead again. Gregory read it and saw that the saint's name was not signed on it as the custom is, and said that unless the signature confirmed what was written the sons would not bury their father; and at that the saint rose and sat up before them all and signed his name. Then he slept again the sweet sleep, and his relics were buried beside the pillar where he had laboured, and were an unfailing spring of healings.

The Romanian life gives no year anywhere; it places him by the reigns it names, Romanos and Constantine Monomachos, and by nothing else.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 7 November, and gives the course of his life in places and dates. saint.gr has him born in the eleventh century in a village of Asia near Magnesia, towards the river Neandros, of the devout Nicetas and Irene; at six years old he was given to the monastery of the Oroboi, and stayed there five years learning. Moved by divine zeal he left the monastery secretly for Jerusalem, and after venerating the holy places there he went to the monastery of Saint Sabbas, where he became a monk in community and then a priest. When the Arabs rose and profaned the holy places he was forced away to Ephesus, to a deserted mountain opposite the city called Galesion. There he lived at first alone in a cell, and later other monks gathered round him. Constantine Monomachos, then in exile on Mytilene, heard of his holiness and built a most beautiful church of the Resurrection on Mount Galesion and endowed it with many sacred treasures; and Lazarus raised a pillar beside the church and settled on its top, keeping his ascesis winter and summer, exposed to the heat and the frost. God gave him the gift of working wonders as well. So, having lived his life in that strict ascesis, he died in holiness at a great age in 1054.

The Russian life begins where the Greek one did not. He was born in Lydia, in the city of Magnesia, an educated and God-loving youth, and became a monk in «обители святого Саввы», the house founded in Palestine by that great ascetic. Ten years he spent inside its walls, and the brotherhood came to love him and to stand in awe of the height of his monastic labour.

Ordained presbyter by the patriarch of Jerusalem, he went back to his own country and settled near Ephesus on the desert mountain of Galesion. There he was granted a wonderful vision: a pillar of fire going up to heaven, with angels around it singing «Да воскреснет Бог и расточатся врази Его». On the place where the wonder was shown him he built a church in the name of the Resurrection of Christ and took upon himself the labour of the pillar; monks began to gather to him for the sake of his word and his example, and so a monastery arose.

He was given to know that his end was near and told the brotherhood of it, and at the tearful prayer of them all the Lord lengthened his earthly life by fifteen years. He died at seventy-two. The brotherhood buried his body in the pillar he had struggled on, and he was glorified by many wonders after his death.
