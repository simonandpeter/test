# Ecdicius, one of the Forty of Sebaste

In the reign of the unbelieving emperor Licinius (308–324), when there was a great persecution of Christians and all the faithful were being forced to sacrifice to the idols, there was at Sebaste, a city of Armenia, a governor with an army named Agricola, a destroyer, savage and clever in the service of the idols. In his company, out of the parts of Cappadocia, were forty soldiers who held the right faith in Christ, men strong and unbeaten in war and very well versed in the divine Scriptures.

Told of three of them — Cyrion, Candidus and Domnus — and of their companions, the governor had them seized and pressed to worship the idols, offering them greater gifts if they sacrificed, and dishonour and expulsion from the army if they would not. They answered that if they had fought and beaten his enemies for an earthly emperor, as he himself admitted, much more would they contend for the deathless King and beat his malice and his cunning. He sent them to prison, where they prayed and sang through the night, Cyrion beginning the verses and the rest taking them up after him.

At midnight the Lord appeared to them and said that the beginning of their struggle was good, but that he who endures to the end shall be saved. Brought out again, they would not be won by the governor's flattery; and when Lysias came to Sebaste after seven days and the two sat in judgement together, the saints answered them without fear, so that Agricola swore the men must be under some sorcery.

Then it was ordered that all forty be taken bound to the lake beside the city. It was winter, with a bitter frost and a great wind, and they were put naked into the middle of the water as the day drew towards evening, with soldiers and the prison guard set round to watch them. At the lake's edge a bath-house had been heated on purpose, so that any of them who weakened with the cold might come out and warm himself there if he would worship the idols. As they went they said to one another that they were not stripping off clothes but putting off the old man, and that the winter was hard but paradise was sweet.

At the first hour of the night one of them could not bear it, parted from that holy company and ran to the bath; but as his foot touched the threshold, scarcely feeling the warmth, he fell down dead. At the third hour a light like the sun shone upon them, warm as at harvest time, which drove off the frost, melted the ice and warmed the water. The guards were overcome with sleep, but the gaoler was awake; and looking up he saw coming down from heaven upon the martyrs' heads shining crowns, thirty-nine of them.

Understanding that the one who had run to the bath was cast out of their company, and that for this the crowns fell short of forty, he woke the other guards, threw off his clothes and leapt into the lake before their eyes, crying with a great voice, “I too am a Christian!” He asked God to number him with them, and so the forty were made up again; his name was Aglaius. In the morning the tyrants found them alive in the warm water, and had them dragged to the shore and their ankles broken with hammers.

The mother of Meliton, a young man of thirty among them, came to that place and strengthened the saints, stretching out her hands to her son and telling him to endure a little longer and not to be afraid, for Christ stood by to help him. The martyrs gave up their souls together, saying that their soul had escaped like a bird from the snare of the hunters; only Meliton still breathed, and was left behind when the bodies were carted off to be burned. His mother took him on her shoulders and followed the cart, and he died as she carried him, and she laid his body upon the others.

The bodies were burned on a great pile of wood near the river, and the tyrants threw what was left of the bones into the water, so that nothing of the martyrs' memory should remain. But after three days the saints appeared to Peter, the bishop of that city, and told him to come by night and bring them out of the river; and the bones shone in the water like stars, so that he gathered every one of them and laid them in an honoured place. The life says they were seized on 26 February and gave up their souls on 9 March, while Licinius ruled in the East, and it gives no year.

The names of the forty, as doxologia prints them, are Chirion, Candid, Domnos, Isihie, Ieraclie, Smaragd, Valent, Vivian, Evnichie, Claudie, Prisc, Teodul, Eutihie, Ioan, Xantie, Ilian, Sisinie, Aghie, Aetie, Flavie, Acachie, Ecdit, Lisimah, Alexandru, Ilie, Leontie, Gorgonie, Teofil, Dometian, Gaie, Atanasie, Chiril, Sacherdon, Nicolae, Valerie, Filoctimon, Severian, Hudion, Meliton and Aglaie. Ecdicius is named in that list, and the life tells nothing further of him by himself.

The Greek calendar keeps the forty on this same day, and saint.gr tells the trial in the same order. They were soldiers of the most select regiment of Licinius's army, and when he loosed the persecution they were arrested at Sebaste by the eparch Agricolas, who began with praise and with promises of pay and rank if they would deny the faith. The page gives the answer of [Candidus](/saints/candidus-of-the-forty-martyrs): they thank him for the praise of their courage, but Christ teaches them to render to every ruler what is his, and so to the emperor they render their soldier's obedience; and if, while they follow the Gospel, they do the state no harm but rather good by their service, why is he examining them about the faith that forms such characters and leads to such deeds?

Thrown into the cold water of a lake on a frozen winter night, they encouraged one another with the words the page prints: «Δριμύς ο χειμών, αλλά γλυκύς ο παράδεισος» — sharp is the winter, but sweet is paradise; let us endure a little, and in one night we shall gain the whole of eternity. One alone lost heart and came out, and the guard [Aglaius](/saints/aglaius-of-the-forty-martyrs), who had seen the crowns above their heads, confessed Christ and went into the lake in his place. In the morning they were drawn out half dead and their legs were broken, and Christians found the relics on a cliff and buried them.

saint.gr adds out of the Euergetinos the mother who stood on the shore over the youngest of them, holding out her hands and telling him to endure a little and he would be a child of the heavenly Father; and it records that the empress [Pulcheria](/saints/pulcheria-the-empress) found the relics in the year 438 behind the ambo of the church of St Thyrsus, in the tomb of the deaconess Eusebia, in two silver cases. The names it prints are those of the Paris codices 1575 and 1476.

The Serbian church keeps the Forty on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović names only one of them and tells the night of the lake in its own way. They were all soldiers in the Roman army, it says, and believed firmly in the Lord Jesus. When the persecution came under Licinius they were brought to trial before the commander, who threatened to take their soldier's honour from them; and Candidus answered for them that he should take not their honour only but their bodies as well, since nothing was dearer or more honourable to them than Christ their God. The commander ordered his servants to stone them, and the stones came back upon the throwers and beat them sorely, and one stone struck the commander in the face and broke his teeth.

They were bound and thrown into the lake, with a guard set round it so that not one of them should get out. The frost was terrible and the lake froze about their bodies; and to make the torment heavier the torturers heated and lit a bath at the lake's edge, in sight of the freezing men, to see whether any of them might be enticed into denying Christ and owning the Roman idols. One was enticed, and came out of the water and went into the bath. But in the night an uncommon light fell from heaven, which warmed the water of the lake and the bodies of the martyrs; and with that light thirty-nine crowns came down from heaven upon their heads. One of the guards on the bank saw it, undressed, confessed the name of the Lord Jesus and went into the lake, that he might be counted worthy of that fortieth crown in the traitor's place — and the last crown came down upon him.

The whole city was astonished the next day to find the martyrs alive. Then the wicked judges ordered their shins broken and their bodies thrown into the water, so that the Christians should not take them. On the third day the martyrs appeared to Peter, the bishop of the place, and called him to gather their relics out of the water; the bishop went out in the dark of night with his clergy, and they saw the relics shining upon the water, and every bone that had been parted from their bodies floated up to the surface and shone like a candle. They gathered them and buried them honourably. The Prologue puts the suffering in the year 320, which falls inside the reign this folder's interval already holds and is narrower than it, so nothing here was changed.
