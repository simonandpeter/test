# George of Megara

The time and the manner of the martyrdom of George and of his three companions, [Adrian](/saints/adrian-of-megara), [Polyeuctus](/saints/polyeuctus-of-megara) and [Plato](/saints/plato-of-megara), are unknown, saint.gr says. What is known is how they came to light: their graves and their holy relics were discovered, by the grace of God, at Megara.

In the years of the Turkish rule, between 1600 and 1670, a man of Megara called Oikonomou wished to raise a building on the spot where the church of the holy martyrs now stands. A workman digging there felt such heat under his feet that he could not work in that place; Oikonomou dug deeper with his own hands and found a marble slab with the saints' names cut into it, and beneath it their relics. Sacrilegious men stole them at once and went off secretly to the Peloponnese, and they were never recovered; Oikonomou took the slab to Constantinople and reported what had happened to the patriarch of the time.

So what is recorded of George is his name, fourth of the four on that slab, and the day the Greek calendar keeps him on. The page offers two guesses about how they came to be at Megara — that they were driven out of Serres and made perfect at Megara, or that they bore witness there as soldiers — and neither is a fact. It gives no year, no reign, no apolytikion and no kontakion. He is not the great-martyr George, nor any of the other men of that name the corpus already keeps.
