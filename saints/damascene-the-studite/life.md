# Damascene the Studite

saint.gr calls the bishop of Lete and Rentina an outstanding figure not only of the sixteenth century but of all the years of the Turkish servitude, a spiritual lighthouse that lit up the darkness of the nation and made his own small bishopric known everywhere. He was born about 1520 at Thessalonica, where he got an excellent education — in the world, the page says, he was perhaps called Demetrius — and went while young to Constantinople, where before 1546 he became a monk of the brotherhood «τῶν Στουδιτῶν», taking the name Damascene and the surname Studite.

Already as a subdeacon, studying in the patriarchal academy, he was a famous preacher of the City, and the sermons he sowed there became the material of his book the «Θησαυρός», the Treasury. Between 1550 and 1558 he was active about Trikala, most likely as teacher of the school there, received the priesthood before 1558, and in those same years went to Venice to have the Treasury printed. In 1560, in the church of the Archangels at Thessalonica, the Rotonda, the hieromonk Damascene was consecrated bishop of Lete and Rentina by Theonas, archbishop of Thessalonica.

Though he was only a bishop, saint.gr says, he did not cease to shine by the joining of a brilliant education to a boundless humility. The German theologian Stephan Gerlach, hostile to Orthodoxy as he was, confirms that this bishop of Lete and Rentina was one of the three best-educated Orthodox clergy of his time, and the most praiseworthy of them for his particular modesty, his frugality and his other virtues.

For those gifts the patriarchs trusted him with weighty missions as exarch: to the Holy Mountain in 1567, and to Little Russia, Ukraine, where between 1565 and 1572 he did much to defeat the Roman Catholic propaganda. Later, under his own disciple the patriarch Jeremias II, he had a part in drawing up the patriarchal dogmatic answer of 1572–73 to the Lutherans of Tübingen, and he kept the throne of Constantinople for several months while the patriarch was away. In 1574 he was advanced to be metropolitan of Naupaktos and Arta and exarch of all Aetolia, as Damascene III the Studite, a throne he served about two years.

In 1576 he was numbered among the learned men of the patriarchate of Constantinople, and in the saving year 1577 he fell asleep in the Lord and was buried in his own metropolitan district, at Naupaktos or at Arta. An epigram on him calls Damascene the wisdom of the Greeks and his death the bad hour that left all lovers of Greece orphaned. Besides the Treasury, which was printed some fifty-one times down to 1926, he left canons in honour of the neomartyr Nicholas, poems to the Mother of God in Homeric language, an exhortation to monks, and treatises of zoology and of meteorology that show how wide his learning was.

Learned enough in the Homeric and the Attic dialect, he nevertheless wrote in a plain and clean Greek for the common people of his day, who had much need of the solid food of God's word; and his Treasury was the most widely spread book of its kind, strengthening an enslaved nation in its griefs. It was turned into Turkish in 1731 for the Turkish-speaking Greeks, into Serbian in 1580 and into Russian in 1656 and 1715, and in Bulgaria his translation, the Damaskinaria, is held to have kept the Orthodox Bulgarians from being Turkified.
