# Titus

Titus was a Greek by birth and well educated. He was made a Christian by the apostle Paul, with whom he worked in the spreading of the Gospel: he followed him to Jerusalem, was sent to Corinth to see the state of the Church there, met Paul again in Macedonia on his way back, and went with him to Crete, where Paul made him bishop of the island and told him to appoint presbyters throughout it.

Paul’s letter to Titus calls him his true child, and from it we learn that he had distinguished fellow workers in Crete, Zenas the lawyer and the famous Apollos. He died, the synaxarion says, in the year 105.

The Russian and Serbian calendars keep him the same day, 25 August (Julian). The Sretensky calendar has him a native of Crete, the son of noble pagans, who in his youth studied Greek philosophy and the ancient poets carefully and lived virtuously, keeping his virginity, as Ignatius the God-bearer witnessed of him. At twenty he heard a voice in a dream bidding him leave the Greek wisdom that gives the soul no salvation and seek the wisdom that saves. He waited a year for a second command, and was told to read the prophets of God; the first book he opened was Isaiah, at the forty-seventh chapter, whose words struck him as spoken of his own state.

When the report of the great Prophet in Palestine and his wonders reached Crete, the island’s governor, his uncle, sent him there. He heard the Saviour’s words and saw his mighty works, his young heart cleaving wholly to Christ. Later he was baptised by Paul, whom he served as a son a father in the work of the Gospel — Paul calls him now son, now brother. He travelled much with him, was made bishop of Crete by him, was present at his martyrdom in Rome and buried his body honourably. Then he returned to Crete, where he baptised the pagans with great success and governed the church of God wisely into deep old age, reposing, the Prologue says, in his ninety-fourth year.
