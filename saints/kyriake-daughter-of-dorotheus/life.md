# Kyriake, daughter of Dorotheus and Eusebia

In the days of the emperor Diocletian, in the year 282, there lived a Christian named Dorotheus and his wife Eusebia. Having no children, they prayed God for fruit and promised to give back to Him the child that should be born; and God heard them, and their daughter was born on a Sunday, for which they called her Chiriachi, which means Sunday. They baptised her and brought her up in the teaching and word of the Lord, keeping the girl a virgin, since they had promised to consecrate her to God.

A rich pagan of the city, hearing her beauty and the goodness of her ways praised, decided to marry her to his son; and when he told her so, she answered that she was the bride of Christ and wished to die a virgin. Diocletian's persecution was then raised against the Christians, and the angry pagan went and denounced Kyriake and her parents to the emperor as Christians. Dorotheus answered the tyrant boldly that he had learnt from his own parents to honour none but the one God who made heaven and earth and was made flesh for our salvation.

He was scourged, and because he would not stop mocking the idols even under the blows, he and Eusebia were sent to the governor Justus in the parts of Melitene, in Lesser Armenia, who tormented them and then had them beheaded. Kyriake herself Diocletian sent to his son-in-law, the caesar Maximian at Nicomedia, who marvelled at her beauty and promised her marriage to a kinsman of the emperor if she would worship the idols. She would not bend at all, saying that nothing could separate her from Christ.

Maximian had her stretched on the ground and beaten with ox sinews until she should die; the soldiers beat her until they tired and changed three times, and she stayed insensible to the blows and shone the more with grace, so that the tyrant grew angry with his own men, thinking they spared her out of pity. Do not deceive yourself, Maximian, the saint said to him; you will never overcome me, for the grace of God is my help.

He sent her on to Hilarion, ruler of Bithynia, notorious for his cruelty to Christians. When he threatened her with fearful torments she answered that he would sooner soften iron than subdue her, and he had her hung up by the hair and burnt with torches; she bore it as though she were without a body, and that night Christ appeared to her in the prison, healed her wounds and promised to bring her through every trial.

An earthquake shattered the idols to dust and a wind scattered them, and the pagans fled; Hilarion alone stayed in the temple cursing, and lightning from heaven burnt his face, so that he fell from his seat and died. A new ruler, Apollonius, had a great furnace heated and her thrown in, and she stood in it several hours at prayer with her hands stretched to heaven and was not burnt; though it was clear summer, a cloud came and a great rain put the fire out. Thrown to two wild lions, she was fawned on by them like lambs, and many pagans confessed Christ at the sight and were killed.

The next day the ruler, seeing that nothing would move her, sat in judgment and sentenced her to be beheaded outside the city. She asked time to pray, and prayed long, thanking Christ who had made her worthy to confess His name before emperors and governors and had kept her virginity unstained. Then shining angels appeared to receive her soul, and she lay down gently on the ground and gave herself into the hands of God, so that the soldiers who came to strike off her head found her already dead and were afraid. A voice came to them: Go, brothers, and preach to all the greatness of God. While they went back to tell the governor, the Christians who were in hiding took her body and buried it.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 7 July, and calls her great martyr and patroness of Servia in Kozani. saint.gr says her parents Dorotheus and Eusebia were childless and begged God for a child, and that God consented; she was born on a Sunday, and for that they gave her the name Kyriake. In the persecution of Diocletian, in the year 282, her parents were arrested, and after questioning were tortured and beheaded by the duke Justus; and Kyriake was referred to the Caesar Maximian, and from him to Hilarian the ruler of Bithynia, who reminded her that her beauty was for enjoyment and not for torture. To that, the page says, the virgin answered that she gave not the slightest attention to her youth or to her beauty, that the brightest earthly things are temporary as flowers and hollow as shadows, that she was beautiful today and tomorrow an ugly old woman, and that she had learned the worth of her beauty from the wrinkles that awaited it and from the grave that called it; so she would not do the monstrous folly of losing eternal brightness to stay a little longer on the earth, and in life and in death she was and would be a Christian. Hilarian, enraged, tortured her cruelly and ordered her beheaded, but before the sword fell she gave up her spirit to the Lord in prayer.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her on the Julian 7 July, the same menologion day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep her on, and it calls her by the Serbian word for Sunday. In the time of the Christ-fighting emperors Diocletian and his son-in-law Maximian, it begins, there lived in Anatolia two devout old souls, rich and childless, who begged this child of God by unceasing prayer; and from childhood she gave herself to God, keeping back from everything that undisciplined children do. When she was grown, beautiful in body and soul, many suitors pressed her, and she turned them all away, saying that she had betrothed herself to Christ the Lord and wished nothing but to die a maiden.

One of those rejected suitors denounced her and her parents to Diocletian as Christians. The emperor had the parents tortured and then banished to the city of Melitene, where they ended their lives in torments for Christ, and sent her to Maximian for judgement. When she confessed her faith before him he had her laid on the ground and scourged with ox-sinews, and then gave her over to his commanders, first to a Hilarion and, after his death, to an Apollonius, who tortured her savagely in every way they could, and all of it was in vain.

As she lay in prison all in wounds, Christ the Lord appeared to her, healed her, and said: fear not the torments, for my grace is with thee. And indeed the grace of Christ saved her from the fire and from the wild beasts, where the godless judges thought she would certainly meet her death; and seeing that wonderful deliverance many pagans believed in Christ, and all of them were beheaded. She told Apollonius that he could by no means turn her from her faith: for the fire she had the example of the Three Children, for the beasts the prophet in the lions’ den, for the sea the prophet the fish swallowed, and for the sword the Honourable Forerunner — their names are set down in this folder’s attestation and not here, since an example quoted in a martyr’s answer is not a kinship.

Then Apollonius ordered that she be beheaded. She knelt, raised her hands to heaven, and prayed God to have mercy on and save all who should keep her memory, and to give her soul rest together with the souls of her parents; and having finished the prayer she gave up her soul to God before the sword came down upon her head. She suffered honourably and passed into eternal joy, this book says, in the year 289, at Nicomedia — a year inside the persecution the Romanian page names without dating her death in it, and so the folder’s recorded interval is left standing rather than narrowed to it.
