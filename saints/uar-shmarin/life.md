# Uar (Shmarin)

Uar — Peter Alexeyevich Shmarin in the world — was born on 11 October 1880
in the village of Novo-Sitovka in Tambov province, one of the thirteen
children of the poor peasants Alexis and Martha Shmarin, of whom only he and
a sister reached grown years; his father died early, and from six he helped
his mother, first by minding the geese. The young priest of the village
noticed that the widow’s son was forward for his years and advised her to
send him to school; she said he had nothing to wear, the priest paid for
everything, and Peter finished the four-year village school with excellent
marks in every subject. Then the priest called him and said, Petrusha, if
you give me your word that you will be a priest I will see to your
education, and got him a place at public cost, first at the gymnasium in
Tambov and then at the church teachers’ seminary at Novo-Alexandrovka in
Kozlov district. He taught school after it, and to keep his word had to
marry first: he courted Claudia, just out of the gymnasium at sixteen, of an
old Don Cossack family whose mother Anna Strelnikova, with merchant brothers
at Lebedyan, would not at first hear of a poor peasant’s son; he persisted,
they were married, and on 21 March 1904 he was ordained deacon for the
Saratov diocese, where two daughters and a son were born. In February 1910
the authorities offered him a post in America or Finland; he chose America
and his wife’s family would not let her go so far, so he went to Finland,
and on 28 October 1910 was ordained priest for the church on the island of
Mantsinsaari in Lake Ladoga. He never thought his schooling enough and
educated himself all his life, gathering a large library of church and
secular books, following the religious and philosophical currents of the
day, and studying medicine so thoroughly that he treated his own children
and the peasants of every village round, and sent only for a surgeon; the
peasants loved him as a pastor of their own kind, and monks from Valaam
nearby often stayed in the roomy parish house, where the family never sat
down to table alone. Three more sons were born there; then he was moved to
Mustamäki near Vyborg, and his wife’s family followed him to Finland. After
the revolution Finland separated from Russia and the whole family went to
Petrograd; he took his wife and children to his mother at Novo-Sitovka and
stayed in Petrograd, and in 1918, when the children had had typhus and
recovered, Claudia fell ill so fast that he arrived after she had been
buried. Five of the six orphans went to their grandmother at Lebedyan, to
the house of her brother the former merchant, and he was given the parish of
Tyutchevo ten versts away, with Kulikovo and Novodvorskoye, and three rooms
in the priest’s widow’s house; the parish gave the family a cow, and he was
arrested there several times for a few days at a time, the talk always
turning to laying down his orders, which he flatly refused.

When the Renovationist schism broke out in 1922 and the parishes of Lebedyan
district lost touch with their bishops, a meeting of clergy and laity
charged him with petitioning Patriarch Tikhon, and on 2 August 1923 he wrote
that the clergy and people who had kept to the historic order of the Church
had no lawful bishop and felt like orphans, and asked that the Lebedyan
church be placed for the time under the bishop of Yelets; the Patriarch put
them under Demetrius (Dobroserdov), bishop of Kozlov. In 1924 the OGPU came
at night, looked over the five children lined up in the corridor, and took
him to the Lebedyan prison, where through long polite interrogations they
offered him, as an educated man the new state needed, a good post, a flat
and every provision if he would only lay down his orders; he answered every
time, what you want of me you will never get; I am the sort of man who never
betrays what he believes, so your efforts are wasted — and they let him go.
His three younger sons went to Petrograd to their uncle; the eldest,
Nicholas, when his father was asked to accept a bishopric, was ordained at
his father’s advice for Tyutchevo so that the church should not be left
empty, and soon had to leave it secretly for Kamenka. On 20 August 1926,
tonsured with the name Uar, he was consecrated bishop of Lipetsk, over the
parishes of the Lipetsk, Borino, Nizhny Studenets, Krasnoye, Lebedyan and
Trubetchino districts, serving at the Nativity cathedral of Lipetsk and
after its closure in 1931 at the Dormition church, and became the
unshakeable bulwark of Orthodoxy in the Lipetsk country and the
irreconcilable enemy of Renovationism. In 1932 his son Nicholas was
arrested, refused to lay down his orders, and served three years in Siberia;
on his return, warned that he would be arrested the moment he served, he
told his father that he would not renounce his orders but could not serve,
and became a watchmaker. The three sons in Petrograd all caught
tuberculosis; the bishop brought them to Lipetsk and, knowing the best
doctors of the day and no little medicine himself, fought for their lives,
and buried them one after another.

The authorities looked for a pretext. In April 1935 a brigade came to take
the bells from the Nativity church at Studenki; the warden Akulina Titova
warned the watchman and the women, took the keys, and went to the bishop
meaning to complain at Voronezh; he told her the journey would be useless —
if they had decided to take the bells they would take them, and the power at
Voronezh was the same as at Lipetsk — and did not bless it. The bells came
down all the same, over two days of the women’s shouting and the villagers’
hauling the big bell’s clapper back up the tower by night; the priest, the
deacon, the warden and the most active parishioners were arrested within the
month, the priest still at liberty agreed to testify against the bishop, and
the arrested one, confused by that testimony, did the same. On 8 June 1935
the bishop was arrested and put in the Lipetsk prison, and for three weeks
was questioned about the women of Studenki and about talk of
collectivisation with the priest Constantine Sofiysky; he said he had taken
no part in anything, had never agitated against the authorities, and had
told the warden not to go; confronted with Sofiysky, who swore the bishop
had called the collective farms forced and predicted famine, he answered
that the one talk about collective farms he remembered was of an old peasant
from Kuyman who asked whether he might have dealings with a son who had
joined a collective farm, whom he told not to break with his son, that no
one forbade collective farmers the church and that it was no sin for a
believer to work beside an unbeliever, and that where the work was organised
there was bread; the priest Cyril Surnin, who had glazed his windows,
testified that he had said the laity of Studenki did wrong to give their
church for grain storage, which the bishop allowed might have been said, and
that he had told of women in the Trubetchino district who stopped a
bell-removal and beat an OGPU agent, which he corrected — they pulled him
off his horse; they did not beat him. On 11 September 1935 the visiting
session of the Voronezh regional court at Lipetsk heard him deny it all and
gave him eight years; his children were allowed to see him, and found him
much aged and quite calm: do not weep and do not fret; live as you have
lived, live honestly, do not avenge me; the main thing is to live a life
worthily. He was taken next day to the Michurinsk prison, and in March 1936
towards the Karaganda camps, reaching them on 8 February 1937, and wrote
home that he lived, glory to God, with nothing to complain of, that physical
work was good for him — he was laying roads — and that the food was cabbage
soup and kasha, our own peasant food; the warden of the Lipetsk cathedral,
in the same camp, wrote that the bishop was ill and weak and made to work
hard, but never despondent, keeping himself up and all of them. The doctors
found myocarditis and St Vitus’s dance and moved him to book-keeping; he was
kept in a barrack of political prisoners until 1938, when he was moved to
the Merkele section and put in a barrack of common criminals, who killed him
on 23 September 1938. He was buried in the cemetery of the Samara section of
the Karaganda camp, now the village of Samarka in the Michurinsk district of
Karaganda region. The life is by the igumen Damascene (Orlovsky), from his
daughter Claudia’s memories and the Lipetsk FSB archive; the Church keeps
him on 10 September of the old style, 23 September, in the synaxes of the
Lipetsk saints and of the Voronezh metropolia.
