# Naucratius, brother of Basil the Great

Naucratius's family had a great influence in the history of Christianity. His father, Saint Basil the Elder, a Christian rhetor, was a temperate man with a considerable fortune; his mother, [Emilia](/saints/emilia-mother-of-basil-the-great), was an orphan whose own father had suffered torments and death for Christ. Ten children were born of that harmonious marriage, five boys and five girls. It seems one of the boys died in childhood, for at the old Basil's death four boys and five girls were called to divide the considerable property they had inherited.

Of the nine children the eldest was a sister, who bore the same name as her grandmother, Macrina. The eldest of the sons was [Basil](/saints/basil-the-great); the second was Naucratius; the third was [Gregory](/saints/gregory-of-nyssa), who became bishop of Nyssa; and [Peter](/saints/peter-of-sebaste), the youngest of the children, was born shortly after his father's death and later became bishop of Sebaste. The life of the elder sister was written by Gregory of Nyssa, and it is from that work that what follows is known.

Macrina, Gregory writes, breathed into Naucratius the love of God and the wish to renounce the world; and Naucratius surpassed his brothers in his gifts. At nearly twenty-two he impressed a whole audience at a public gathering by his talent as an orator and the learning he showed. But he despised the fame and the riches around him and gave up the career he might have had, longing rather for a poor life given to God. He took nothing with him when he went into the wilderness. One of the household, Chrysaphius, followed him, loving and honouring him and wishing the withdrawn life for himself as well.

Going to a desert place near the river Iris, Naucratius came upon a thick and untrodden forest, and withdrew there, far from the disturbance of the cities and from worldly business. With the work of his own hands he cared for some poor and sick old people living nearby, providing what they needed, for he was skilled at fishing and hunting. He lived in perfect obedience to his mother Emilia, who after her husband's death was likewise leading a life of ascetic labour away from the world together with Macrina. So he passed his youth and adorned himself with good works, going forward in obedience to the divine commandments.

After five years of ascetic labour a grave and unhappy accident happened. While he was fishing with Chrysaphius to provide food for those sick old people, Naucratius was suddenly torn out of this life, without having suffered any illness or injury beforehand; and both he and his friend Chrysaphius died in the flower of their age.

The trial grieved his mother greatly, but having Saint Macrina the Younger beside her she overcame the pain and the sorrow of losing her son out of this world, and was reconciled to the thought of his dwelling with the angels in heaven beside Christ. Gregory writes in the Life of Saint Macrina: "Our mother was three days' journey away. When she learned of his death, though she was perfect in the virtues she was overwhelmed with grief, left without breath and without speech. Then the courage and the virtue of the great Macrina appeared. She met the grief with right judgement; so, keeping herself unbent by sorrow, she was able to strengthen our mother in her weakness and to raise her out of the depths of mourning. By her firmness and her manliness Macrina was able to teach her mother to show strength of soul."

At Annesi, Emilia built a chapel in honour of the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste, whom the Church commemorates on 9 March. Naucratius was laid in that place, where the relics of Emilia and of her husband Basil were laid as well; and afterwards Gregory of Nyssa buried his sister, Macrina the Younger, in the same chapel.
