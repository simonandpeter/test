# Theodosius of the Kyiv Caves

The Russian calendar keeps 14 August (Julian) for the translation of his relics in 1091; his chief memory is 3 May.

Theodosius, founder of the cenobitic monastic rule and father of monasticism in the Russian land, was born at Vasilev near Kyiv. From his youth he was drawn irresistibly to the ascetic life, living it already in his parents’ house — no games, constant church, and at his own asking the reading of the sacred books, which with his gifts and rare zeal he quickly mastered. He lost his father at fourteen and was left to his mother, a strict and masterful woman who loved him dearly and punished him many times for his striving; at twenty-four he left home secretly and was tonsured by the blessing of Anthony at the Kyiv Caves. Four years later his mother found him and begged him with tears to come home, and he persuaded her instead to stay in Kyiv and become a nun at the monastery of St Nicholas on Askold’s grave.

He laboured in the monastery more than the others and often took the brethren’s tasks on himself, carrying water, chopping wood, grinding rye and carrying the flour to each monk; on hot nights he bared his body to the gnats and midges, the blood ran down him, and he went on with his handiwork and sang psalms; he came to church before everyone and did not move from his place till the end, listening to the reading with particular attention. Ordained hieromonk in 1054 and elected igumen in 1057, he drew a multitude of monks by the fame of his labours, built a new church and cells, and introduced the cenobitic rule of the Studion, copied in Constantinople at his bidding; as igumen he kept the hardest obediences, ate only dry bread and boiled greens without oil, passed his nights in prayer, was never seen sleeping lying down, and in Great Lent withdrew to a cave near the monastery; his clothing was a rough hair shirt worn next to the skin, so that no one would have known the famous igumen in the poor old man — as the driver did not, who, taking him home from the Grand Prince Izyaslav, grumbled that the monk was always idle while he laboured, and made him take the reins, and was terrified when the boyars they met dismounted and bowed to the saint, who calmed him and fed him at the monastery. He kept no great stores, trusting God, so that the brethren sometimes wanted for bread, and unknown benefactors appeared at his prayer; the princes, Izyaslav above all, loved his spiritual talk, and he was not afraid to rebuke the powerful — the unjustly condemned always found a defender in him, and judges reconsidered cases at the word of the igumen whom all revered. He built within the monastery a special court where any needy man could get food and shelter. Foreknowing his end, he died in peace in 1074 and was buried in the cave he had dug himself for his Lenten solitude; his relics were found incorrupt in 1091, and he was numbered among the saints in 1108. Six homilies, two letters to Izyaslav and a prayer for all Christians survive; his life, written by Nestor the Chronicler, his disciple, some thirty years after his death, was always among the Russian people’s best-loved reading.
