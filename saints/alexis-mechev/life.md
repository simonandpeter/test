# Alexis (Mechev)

The Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery keeps him on 9 июня and calls him «Прав. Алексия Московского», which is the grade of a righteous man and the city and no surname. He is the Moscow elder, in the world the archpriest Alexis Mechev, and he is not the fourteenth-century metropolitan «Алексий Московский» whom this corpus already keeps and who died in 1378. He was born on 17 March 1859 into the pious family of the regent of the Chudov cathedral choir, and his father had himself been saved as a child from freezing to death on a winter night by the metropolitan of Moscow, who found him asleep in a sledge nobody had thought to empty.

In 1884 Alexis married Anna Petrovna Molchanova, a psalm-reader's daughter of eighteen, and on 18 November of the same year he was ordained deacon. The marriage was happy; he saw in his wife a friend and his first helper on the way to Christ and listened to her friendly remarks as another man listens to his elder. On 19 March 1893 he was ordained priest to the church of Nicholas the Wonderworker in Klenniki on Maroseika, a small church with a very small parish, with large and well-attended churches standing close by.

As rector of that one-priest church he brought in a daily service where the small churches of Moscow commonly had one two or three times a week. He came in before five in the morning and unlocked the church himself; he venerated the wonderworking Theodore icon of the Mother of God, prepared what was needed for the Eucharist and served the proskomedia without waiting for any of the clergy, then matins, at which he often read and sang himself, and then the liturgy. "For eight years I served the liturgy every day in an empty church," he said afterwards. The little wooden house his family lived in was rotten and dark, and in wet weather the streams ran down into the churchyard and the cellar.

Anna Petrovna died on 29 August 1902, and a merchant family close to him invited [John of Kronstadt](/saints/john-of-kronstadt), who was then in Moscow, to their house for the sake of this meeting. "Have you come to share my grief?" Alexis asked as he came in. "It is not thy grief I have come to share but thy joy," the answer was. "The Lord is visiting thee. Leave thy cell and go out to the people; only from now wilt thou begin to live. Thou rejoicest in thy sorrows and thinkest there is no grief in the world greater than thine. But be with the people, enter into another's grief, take it on thyself, and then thou wilt see that thy misfortune is a small thing beside the common grief, and it will be easier for thee." He took what was said to him as an obedience laid upon him.

From then the Maroseika church filled. He met those who came to it broken by hard circumstances, by mutual dislike, sunk in sins or forgetful of God with a warmth and love and compassion that left each of them feeling he had been pitied and consoled above all others. He had the gift of foresight and hid the fulness of it out of humility: he would tell a man a detail of his own life as though he were recounting a similar case that had lately happened, and he would say once only what should be done. After 1917 the numbers grew again, with part of the parish and choir of the Chudov monastery coming over when the Kremlin was closed, and many students and young people who had seen that the revolution brought new disasters instead of the promised good.

His true spiritual friends, the reading says, were the Optina ascetics of his own time, the hieroschemamonk Anatoly and the abbot Theodosius who led the skete; Anatoly sent the Muscovites who came to him on to Alexis, and the elder Nektary said to somebody, "Why do you travel to us? You have father Alexis." Theodosius, visiting the Maroseika church and seeing the files of penitents, the long and exact service and the crowd waiting to be received, told him: "All this work which you do alone would need several men with us at Optina. For one man it is beyond strength. The Lord helps you."

At the end of May 1923 by the new style he went as in other years to rest at Vereya, a distant little town of the Moscow region where he had a small house, serving his last liturgy at Maroseika and taking leave of his spiritual children and of the church before he went. He died on Friday 9/22 June 1923. The last evening he was glad and gentle with everyone and spoke of those who were absent; death came the moment he lay down in bed, and it was instantaneous. The coffin was brought to the church of Nicholas in Klenniki on the morning of 14/27 June, and the communities of Moscow came one after another with their pastors to sing the requiems and take leave, which went on until the next morning so that everyone who came could pray.

His grave was afterwards the most visited in the cemetery, by the testimony of its own administration, and earth had regularly to be added to the mound because those who had his help carried it away. On the first anniversary the Maroseika community asked whoever wished to write down what had passed between them and him, and many did; some of those recollections witness to his foresight and to wonders and prayerful help. He was numbered among the saints of the Russian Church for general veneration at the Jubilee Council of Bishops in 2000, and his relics are now in Moscow in the church of the hierarch Nicholas in Klenniki.
