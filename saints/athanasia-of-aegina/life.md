# Athanasia of Aegina

Athanasia is remembered, doxologia's life says at the outset, as the abbess who corrected faults only with gentleness. She was a ninth-century nun of Aegina; her life is mentioned in Codex Vaticanus 1660 and was written about the year 916, and she died on 14 August 860. Her parents were Nicetas and Maria, and it was their teaching that set her reading the Psalms at seven years old. Later, while she was working at the loom, she saw a shining star come down and settle on her breast and fill her with light before it vanished, and from then on she wanted to become a nun.

The wish was granted only after trials of her obedience and humility. At sixteen her parents settled a marriage for her; she submitted, and within a few days her husband was called into the army and killed by the barbarians who had invaded Aegina. As a widow she prepared herself for the monastic life more than ever, and again it was not to be: the emperor Michael called the Stammerer (820–829) decreed that young widows must marry again, to young soldiers. A second time she submitted. Her new husband seems to have been a Muslim, the page says, but he converted to Orthodoxy with her, and the two lived simply, helping the sick and the war-wounded and reading the Scriptures with the households of their friends.

After a time the couple agreed to enter the monastic life. Athanasia withdrew with a few devout women and built a monastery, and after four years they begged her to be their abbess. She corrected the sisters always with gentleness and never with anger, holding to the Lord's word that whoever would be first must be the servant of all; she wore poor clothes, kept vigil every night, ate little and fasted hard.

On Aegina there lived an old monk named Matthew, who had become an abbot and read the Psalter through every night with other prayers, and wept whenever he served or read. Saint John the Theologian had appeared to him and he had become a healer of the paralysed and the possessed, and it was he who blessed Athanasia to raise a monastery on a deserted hill of the island, beside an ancient church of Stephen the Archdeacon and First Martyr. Athanasia too received the gift of healing; after she cured a man of a disease of the eyes many came to her, and out of the offerings that followed she built three churches.

She did not want the fame or the crowds, and fled with two sisters close to her, Maria and Eupraxia, to Constantinople, where she lived seven years as a simple nun with no charge over anyone. The nuns of Aegina searched for their abbess, found her and begged her to come back; seeing God's will in the asking, she returned. Afterwards she had a vision of two shining men who gave her a paper that read «Aici este eliberarea ta, ia-o și bucură-te» — here is your release, take it and rejoice — which she understood as the announcement of her death.

She prayed without ceasing, and on the eve of the Dormition, when the whole Psalter had been read, she took leave of the sisters: it was 14 August 860. Forty days later two nuns saw her standing before the Royal Doors while two shining men set a crown of many crosses on her head and a robe of precious stones upon her. She had left word that food be given to the poor for forty days in her memory, and when the sisters stopped after nine she appeared and rebuked them, teaching that alms given in the first forty days help sinful souls to God's mercy, and that if the souls are righteous the mercy returns upon those who gave.

A year after her death a possessed woman was brought to the grave; the scent of myrrh came from it, and at her touch she was healed at once. So the myrrh-streaming relics were found, the face calm and shining as though asleep, and the body was moved into the church. When the nuns wished to change her poor habit for silk she clenched her hands on it, and only let go when one of them begged her to accept the new robe as their humble gift. Healings, the page ends, do not cease at her relics.
