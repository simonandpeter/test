# Athanasia of Aegina

Athanasia is remembered, doxologia's life says at the outset, as the abbess who corrected faults only with gentleness. She was a ninth-century nun of Aegina; her life is mentioned in Codex Vaticanus 1660 and was written about the year 916, and she died on 14 August 860. Her parents were Nicetas and Maria, and it was their teaching that set her reading the Psalms at seven years old. Later, while she was working at the loom, she saw a shining star come down and settle on her breast and fill her with light before it vanished, and from then on she wanted to become a nun.

The wish was granted only after trials of her obedience and humility. At sixteen her parents settled a marriage for her; she submitted, and within a few days her husband was called into the army and killed by the barbarians who had invaded Aegina. As a widow she prepared herself for the monastic life more than ever, and again it was not to be: the emperor Michael called the Stammerer (820–829) decreed that young widows must marry again, to young soldiers. A second time she submitted. Her new husband seems to have been a Muslim, the page says, but he converted to Orthodoxy with her, and the two lived simply, helping the sick and the war-wounded and reading the Scriptures with the households of their friends.

After a time the couple agreed to enter the monastic life. Athanasia withdrew with a few devout women and built a monastery, and after four years they begged her to be their abbess. She corrected the sisters always with gentleness and never with anger, holding to the Lord's word that whoever would be first must be the servant of all; she wore poor clothes, kept vigil every night, ate little and fasted hard.

On Aegina there lived an old monk named Matthew, who had become an abbot and read the Psalter through every night with other prayers, and wept whenever he served or read. Saint John the Theologian had appeared to him and he had become a healer of the paralysed and the possessed, and it was he who blessed Athanasia to raise a monastery on a deserted hill of the island, beside an ancient church of Stephen the Archdeacon and First Martyr. Athanasia too received the gift of healing; after she cured a man of a disease of the eyes many came to her, and out of the offerings that followed she built three churches.

She did not want the fame or the crowds, and fled with two sisters close to her, Maria and Eupraxia, to Constantinople, where she lived seven years as a simple nun with no charge over anyone. The nuns of Aegina searched for their abbess, found her and begged her to come back; seeing God's will in the asking, she returned. Afterwards she had a vision of two shining men who gave her a paper that read «Aici este eliberarea ta, ia-o și bucură-te» — here is your release, take it and rejoice — which she understood as the announcement of her death.

She prayed without ceasing, and on the eve of the Dormition, when the whole Psalter had been read, she took leave of the sisters: it was 14 August 860. Forty days later two nuns saw her standing before the Royal Doors while two shining men set a crown of many crosses on her head and a robe of precious stones upon her. She had left word that food be given to the poor for forty days in her memory, and when the sisters stopped after nine she appeared and rebuked them, teaching that alms given in the first forty days help sinful souls to God's mercy, and that if the souls are righteous the mercy returns upon those who gave.

A year after her death a possessed woman was brought to the grave; the scent of myrrh came from it, and at her touch she was healed at once. So the myrrh-streaming relics were found, the face calm and shining as though asleep, and the body was moved into the church. When the nuns wished to change her poor habit for silk she clenched her hands on it, and only let go when one of them begged her to accept the new robe as their humble gift. Healings, the page ends, do not cease at her relics.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her on the same day and gives the ascetic detail the Romanian life has not. She was born on the island of Aegina, it says, of rich and well-born parents; she gave her property away to the destitute and withdrew into a monastery, where she laid on herself an ever heavier and heavier struggle. She took food only once a day, and that bread and water; during the Great Fast, once in two days; and only at the Nativity and at Pascha did she taste oil and fish. And though she was the abbess of the monastery, she was a servant to all the other sisters and was ashamed to have anyone wait on her. She was counted worthy of a great gift of wonderworking, both in her life and after her death, and fell asleep in the Lord in the year 860 — the year this folder already holds.

**The Russian calendar keeps her on the same menologion day and prints the longest life of her this corpus has read.** Its line is «Прп. Афанасии игумении (860).» and its life names her parents, Nicetas and Marina, and has her learn the Psalter by heart at seven and read it constantly with compunction; and at the loom one day she saw a shining star come down to her, which reached her breast, lit the whole of her, and vanished, and from then she was enlightened in soul and resolved to go to a monastery.

At sixteen her parents begged her to marry; she submitted, and was married sixteen days, her husband being taken to the war and killed there. Widowed, she meant to do what she had long wanted, but a decree of the emperor Michael the Stammerer, whose reign the page dates 820 to 829, required young widows to marry young soldiers, and she had to marry again. In that marriage she kept house, helped the sick and the needy, took in strangers, and on Sundays and feasts called her kindred and acquaintance to her and read them the Scriptures; under her influence her husband went into a monastery and gave her leave to be tonsured.

She gave away her property, took the habit, and withdrew with some devout women to a solitary place; after a while the sisters begged her to be abbess of their small community, and she looked on that office as a particular service to God and to them, correcting every fault of theirs with love and without anger. Though she bore the title she counted herself the last of the sisters, remembering the Saviour's word that whoever would be first among them must be their servant, and would never let them wait on her, not even to pour water over her hands. She wore a hair shirt with coarse sheep's wool over it, slept little, prayed most of the night, worked with the sisters by day, and ate once, in the evening, bread and water, allowing herself oil, cheese and fish only at the Nativity and at Pascha, and in the fasts eating raw greens every second day.

An elder monk of Aegina, Matthew, who had been an abbot before, read the whole Psalter every night with prayers besides, slept sitting and very little, and could not hold back his tears at the psalms and the prayers and the bloodless sacrifice; he wore one sharp hair shirt and had dried up the flesh altogether by abstinence. He had a particular love for [John the Theologian](/saints/john-the-theologian), and once at the Divine Liturgy was counted worthy to see the apostle standing at the altar; he healed a paralytic with his mantle, straightened with the sign of the cross a man's face that the devil had twisted, drove out demons and worked many other wonders. It was he who blessed Athanasia to go with the sisters to a still more solitary place, and she built a house on a desert mountain of the same island, near an ancient church of the first martyr.

She was granted the gift of healings, and after she had cured a man whose eyes were diseased a multitude began to come to her; out of the offerings they brought she built three churches in the house, of the Most Holy Theotokos, of the prophet [John the Forerunner](/saints/john-the-baptist) and of [Nicholas](/saints/nicholas-of-myra) the Wonderworker. The fame of it weighed on her, and she took two sisters close to her in spirit, Mary and Eupraxia, and went secretly to Constantinople, where she entered a monastery as a simple nun and lived seven years, until the sisters of Aegina found out where she was and went and begged her to come back, and she obeyed what she took to be God's providence and returned.

Twelve days before her end she was in unceasing prayer. On the eve of the Dormition she called the sisters and said she had only been able to read the Psalter as far as the nineteenth psalm, and asked them to finish it for her in the church; they did, and came back to take their leave, and she blessed them and asked them to keep the feast solemnly and gladly, to set out a meal for the poor, and to bury her body after the Liturgy — and with those words departed to the Lord, «+ 14 августа 860 года». **That date is the death and not the feast**: the day page that carries her keeps her on 12 апреля, and the 14 August the life gives is the day she died, which is also the day doxologia.ro gives and which this folder already records as her year.

On the fortieth day two devout sisters were counted worthy to see her appear before the royal doors, where two shining men crowned her head with a crown of crosses, put a shining staff in her hand and led her through the doors into the sanctuary. She had charged them to feed the poor in her memory until the fortieth day; they kept the memorial table only nine days, and she appeared to some of them and said it was for nothing that they had not kept her charge, since the forty-day commemoration of the departed in church and the feeding of the poor help sinful souls greatly, and from righteous souls a heavenly mercy is sent down on those who keep it. The one who appeared drove her staff into the ground and became invisible, and the next day the staff had taken root and become a living tree.

A year after her death a woman possessed was brought to her tomb; when the earth was opened there was a fragrance, and the coffin was taken out, and at the touch of it the woman was healed at once. They opened the lid and saw the body incorrupt and streaming myrrh, as though she were asleep, the face shining and the whole body soft, the hands even bending. The priests resolved to set the body in the church, and when it was moved to a new ark the nuns took the hair shirt off the relics and meant to clothe them in silk; but her hands pressed so close to her breast that they could not, and so she showed her love of poverty after death as well. Then one of the sisters knelt and prayed her — our lady, as you obeyed us without a word while you lived with us, so now consent to obey us and put on these clothes, our humble gift to you — and Athanasia raised herself as though alive and stretched out her hands to the garment.
