# Peter (Petrikov)

Peter was born on 19 January 1903 at Mozhaisk in Moscow province. His father
Sergius, born in 1873 at Yenkayevo in Tambov province, was orphaned at
eight, herded the village cattle as a hired boy, served as a soldier, stayed
in Moscow, got an education with good people’s help and went onto the
railway as watchman, clerk and at last assistant stationmaster at Tuchkovo,
when the family was given land and helped to build a house; his mother
Tatiana came of Samara merchants. At eighteen he wrote to the commissar of
health Semashko asking help to enter the medical faculty of Moscow
university — his first years had run by quickly, school at Batyushkovo,
Vyazma and Kubinka as his father was moved, seven years at the Mozhaisk
modern school away from the family, the plan of 1920 wrecked by his father’s
paralysis, a winter of trying to learn telegraphy, poetry and sketches,
painting he had no means to keep up, music he loved best and could not study
— creative, inspired, impetuous, he felt the wholeness of his soul forming
and, he wrote, the springs of eternal youth and joy opening in me. Semashko
supported him and he entered in 1921, overworked in his second year, fell
gravely ill and was treated for two years at the railway hospital’s
out-patients; and in those years his soul turned over, and the springs of
eternal youth and joy he had guessed at he found now in Christ. He became a
spiritual son of the elder Nectarius of Optina and one of a circle of young
people who, like him, sought the truth without compromise in the sea of lies
closing over the country, among them Andrew Elbson; in June 1925 Bishop
Boris (Rukin) of Mozhaisk ordained him deacon, and the same year the locum
tenens Metropolitan Peter (Polyansky) ordained him priest. He had no parish
at first and served where friends did, at the Alexandrian metochion in
Moscow where Elbson was priest; in March 1931 he was appointed to St
Nicholas in Klenniki, the church of the long labours of the archpriest
Alexis Mechev, and served there until 14 April, when he was arrested with
fifty priests, monks and laity and put in the Butyrki. Asked about his past
he told of his origin and studies and said he had taken orders from inner
conviction, which was enough for the interrogator, since it showed a man
alien to the godless order; on 5 June 1931 the Special Board gave him three
years in the camps and he was sent to the Mariinsk camps, where his mother
and his spiritual daughter Vera Mironovich, afterwards his cell-attendant
and helper, followed him, settled nearby and petitioned against a sentence
for a counter-revolution that had never even been put to him, and on 17
September 1931 the Board released him with a ban on twelve cities for three
years.

He settled at Murom and was arrested there in May 1932; on 3 June he wrote
his answers in his own hand — he knew no ciphers and had used none; no
services had been held in his flat; he recognised the authorities not for
fear but for conscience’ sake, had kept every law that touched him, used the
freedom to believe what he pleased that the constitution gave, and saw in
everything that had happened to the country the ways of God’s providence,
which always leads to good; the Church is not of this world, his interests
were purely spiritual, the receiving of grace and of the perfections that
God has, he understood nothing of politics, submitted to the power in
conscience and would sacrifice everything for it if need be, only not his
faith in God, if anyone should ever demand it; and he asked to be spared
political conversation, of which he understood nothing. On 17 August 1932
the case was dropped for want of material and he was freed. He lived at
Murom until the summer of 1936 and then went home to Mozhaisk, where his
spiritual daughter followed and priests he knew and his family visited him;
he rented a room, made a house church in it, and until his arrest served the
liturgy there after the pattern of the ancients, as it was served in
Christians’ houses in the pagan persecutions. Before the mass arrests of the
summer of 1937 the NKVD took first the priests, monks and laity they already
suspected of disloyalty — those who held the godlessness enthroned on
Russian soil for the greatest evil ruining the Russian people — fifteen of
them in Moscow between February and April, and him on 10 April 1937; the
charge was an illegal church-monarchist organisation which since the Stalin
constitution had held meetings in Moscow, resolved to plant cells of
returned exiles and to make secret prayer houses, and had bought or rented
houses at Mozhaisk, Maloyaroslavets and Kimry to settle returned monks and
clergy in. Questioned on 11 and 13 April and again on 10 May he answered
most questions with ignorance and a bad memory, and said flatly that he had
held no political conversations; a Mozhaisk acquaintance from the transports
testified that he served in his flat with his spiritual daughter as reader
and visiting priests present, and belonged to the “true Orthodox church”,
extremely reactionary and anti-Soviet; in July the decision was put off for
the coming mass arrests, and on 26 August a duty witness from among the
priests called him an anti-Soviet man who hated the Soviet power and the
party. On 21 September the investigation closed: he was a member of the
organisation, head of its Mozhaisk branch, and kept a secret house church
where his fellow-thinkers from Moscow and elsewhere gathered. On 26
September 1937 the troika sentenced him to death; he was shot the next day,
27 September 1937, and buried in an unknown common grave at Butovo. The life
is by the igumen Damascene (Orlovsky); the Church keeps him on 15 September
of the old style, 28 September, and in the synaxes of the new martyrs of
Butovo and of Moscow.
