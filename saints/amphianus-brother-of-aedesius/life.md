# Amphianus, brother of Aedesius

Amphianus and [Aedesius](/saints/aedesius-brother-of-amphianus) were brothers in the flesh, sons of one father, born in the heathenism of the Greeks; their home was the city of Patara in the country of Lycia. Their parents sent them from there to Beirut for the learning of the outward wisdom, and there they passed the days of their youth with such honour, gentleness and good sense that everyone wondered at their blameless life: they put off the ways young men usually have and were like honoured elders, having wisdom instead of grey hairs and a clean life instead of old age.

The light of the knowledge of the truth rose in their hearts, and they began to see the error of the idol-worshippers and to know the straight road of the Christian faith. Coming home from Beirut, they found their father in office at Patara and still unwilling to leave the Greek heathenism, and their kindred with him; and rather than share in it, they went away secretly, leaving parents, house, wealth and the ease of life for Christ. They came to Caesarea in Palestine and found there the Christian presbyter Pamphilus, who himself became a martyr afterwards, and gave themselves to him for teaching; they were enlightened with holy baptism and lived with their teacher, reading and learning in the law of the Lord day and night.

At that time Maximian, the second emperor of the name, took the East from Maximian Galerius his uncle, and persecuted the Church of Christ more bitterly than the emperors before him, so that there was great trouble everywhere in the East. Many left their houses and cities and hid themselves; many gave themselves into the hands of the heathen. Amphianus was one of these — barely twenty years old in body, the life says, and a hundred in understanding and greatness of soul.

When the criers were calling the citizens by name to the temples, he came out of the place where the Christians were hidden, told no one, and went to the idol temple where Urban the governor was at that hour sacrificing. He came up to him without fear, took hold of his right hand, the hand with the sacrifice in it, and with a great voice told him to leave off that error and not to hold devils and things made by men's hands for gods while he slandered the one true God.

That boldness strengthened many Christians in the faith and moved the governor to rage. The soldiers with him fell on him like wolves on sheep, struck him about the mouth and the face and over the whole body, rolled him on the ground and trod on him, and threw him into prison in bonds. The next day he was brought out to trial and pressed to sacrifice, and would not; hung up and torn to the bone with sharp irons, beaten with rods of lead across the face, the neck and the ribs until his face could not be told even by those who knew him and his ribs were broken, he did not stop confessing the name of Jesus Christ, and suffered as though in another man's body.

Then they soaked him in oil, wrapped him about, bound his feet and set him alight, and the sufferer melted like wax; and even that did not overcome him, but he took greater boldness from it and cried out with a loud voice, glorifying Christ and reproaching the Greek godlessness. They threw him into prison, and on the third day, when he was barely alive, they questioned him again under torture and found him unchanged in the same confession; and the governor ordered him thrown into the depth of the sea. They carried him out into the middle of it, tied a stone to him and cast him in; and in that hour the sea swelled with waves, the earth shook, the city rocked and great fear took hold of everyone, and the waves rose and put the martyr's body out on the land before the gates of the city. The life dates his end the second day of the month of April, on a Thursday.

The Greek calendar keeps the two brothers on the same day and names its source, Eusebius of Caesarea. saint.gr has them born of notable parents living at Gaza — which the page calls «σημαντική πόλη της Λυκίας», a significant city of Lycia — and sends Amphianus to study at the schools of Berytus, then famous above all for law, from which he came home untouched by the traps of the enemy that lie in wait in a great cosmopolitan city. Unable to bear living with a family whose spirit was not his, he took refuge at Caesarea in Palestine and shared a house there with [Pamphilus](/saints/pamphilus-of-caesarea), whose memory the page puts on 5 November, and with his disciples, and trained with them. The page names the governor he confronted, Urbanus of Caesarea in Palestine, and the emperor whose letters ordered every inhabitant without exception to sacrifice, Maximinus II, about the year 305.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two brothers under one heading on the same menologion day and tells the same story, with Patara for their city — agreeing with the Romanian life against saint.gr's Gaza — and parents of note who were pagans. They were studying the outward sciences in the city of Beirut, it says, when they were somehow enlightened by the Spirit of God, so that knowing the falsehood of heathenism they made out the truth of Christianity; and when they came home they could no longer live with pagan parents and kindred, but fled secretly to Caesarea in Palestine, to the presbyter [Pamphilus](/saints/pamphilus-of-caesarea), known for his holiness and his spiritual learning, and were taught the law of God with him day and night. Of Amphianus it says that in body he was twenty years old and in understanding and greatness of soul a hundred.

When the persecution came, in the time of Maximinus, many Christians fled the city and hid, while others gave themselves up willingly and gladly into the hands of the tormentors, to suffer for the name of him who had suffered first for them; and Amphianus was among these last. He came without fear into the pagan temple where the governor Urban was offering sacrifice to the idols, caught the governor by the hand that held the sacrifice, and cried out to him to leave off serving and sacrificing to dead idols and to know the true God. Some who heard those words and saw such courage repented and received the faith of Christ. The governor in his rage gave him over to torments; among the rest they wrapped his feet in cotton and set it alight, and when he was still alive after that they threw him into the sea with a stone about his neck — and the sea rose and put the martyr's body out in the city. The entry closes on both brothers together: like two innocent lambs they were offered for Christ about the year 306, which is the only year any source read for these folders gives them.

The Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery keeps the two brothers on the Julian 2 April under one line, «Мчч. Амфиана и Едесия (306).», and the life behind it is titled for them both. It agrees with the Romanian and Serbian readings on Patara in Lycia for their home and on a pagan father who held office in the city, and on Вирит — Berytus — as the place they were sent to for the heathen learning and where they came to believe in Christ. Where it differs is the city they fled to: it has them leave their pagan parents for «Кесария Александрийская», Caesarea of Alexandria, and not Caesarea in Palestine.

There they found a teacher, a presbyter named Pamphilus, and grew with him in prayer and the reading of the holy books. The page sets the persecution under the emperor Maximian, whose reign it gives as 305 to 313, and has many Christians hide themselves from the sacrifices the decree required, the brothers among them. When the governor of the city was to sacrifice to the idols, Amphianus walked into the temple without fear, took him by the hand as he stood at the altar and urged him to leave his error and believe in Christ. He was beaten and thrown into prison, brought out to trial on two days, beaten with iron rods and burnt with bundles of flax soaked in oil, and at the last thrown into the sea with a stone about his neck; a sudden storm put his body back on the shore, and the Christians buried him. The governor is left unnamed here, where the other pages call him Urban.
