# Barypsabas

Of Barypsabas it is said that he received from a hermit the precious Blood
that ran from the Lord’s side, and healed many sicknesses with it; for this
the unbelievers killed him with clubs in the night, and the precious treasure
of the Master’s Blood remained whole and entire, kept by the saint’s
disciple. The verse says that for the divine blood of the Master, Barypsabas,
crushed with clubs, pours out his own.

Nikodemos of the Holy Mountain adds a long note on what that blood was.
Some say it was not the blood shed from the Saviour’s side itself — which,
in some theologians’ opinion, rose again with the Lord’s risen body — but
blood that ran from the side of an icon of Christ which the Jews pierced,
the icon that was at Beirut, as the divine Athanasius tells the wonder.
Meletios writes of a most precious treasure of the Lord’s blood brought to
Augia in 923, which Marianus Scotus says ran from an icon of Christ pierced
by the Jews in the city of Bergos, suffering what the Lord had suffered
alive; Dositheos says it was from the icon wounded by a Jew at
Constantinople; and Euthymios Zigabenos, writing against the pagans in the
twenty-sixth title of his Panoply, says that it was Christ himself and not
his shadow that was crucified in the flesh, and that the blood which flowed
from his all-holy body is kept among Christians, pours out healings, and is
witnessed by many miracles to be Christ’s. Some say a small casket was found
in the treasury of St Mark at Venice, taken by the Latins from
Constantinople, with a verse on the outside — “You hold me, Christ, bearing
the blood of my flesh” — which works numberless wonders; and Joseph
Bryennios, in his study on the Cypriots, says that the robe and girdle of
the Lady of the angels, the Master’s seamless coat and his garments, the
blood that ran from his side, the lance, the nails, the sponge and the reed
were all at Constantinople. Perhaps, Nikodemos concludes, the Lord’s blood
above was that which ran from the said holy icons of the Saviour, and
especially the one at Beirut, which John Tzimiskes later carried to
Constantinople, and of which the synod at Jerusalem wrote to the iconoclast
emperor Theophilus. The Greek calendar keeps Barypsabas on 10 September.
