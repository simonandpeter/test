# David of Wales

David, whom the Romanian calendar names the protector of Wales, lived in the second half of the sixth century. Ordained a priest, he settled in the south-western corner of Wales, at Menevia, and there founded a monastery known for its extreme austerity, in imitation of the fathers of the desert: David and his monks kept a very strict rule, drinking only water and eating only bread and vegetables.

By tradition he was ordained bishop by the Patriarch of Jerusalem, when he went there to worship. He worked hard to spread Christianity in Wales, and above all in the south-west, in the part now called Pembroke; he founded twelve monasteries, and is reckoned one of the best-loved saints of the British Isles. doxologia says he did many miracles during his lifetime.

He fell asleep in the Lord around the year 601. Saint David's Day has been kept as a national feast in Wales on the first of March since the twelfth century. doxologia prints no hymn for him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and saint.gr, which writes his name in Latin letters and renders it «Δαβίδ» in brackets, fills in the Welsh geography that doxologia left out. It has him tonsured a monk young and studying many years as a priest, and says that by one tradition he was elected bishop by the Patriarch of Jerusalem when he went there to venerate. He worked hard to spread Christianity in Wales, and above all in the south-west of it, in what is now Pembrokeshire; there he founded the monastery at Mynyw — Menevia, today's St Davids — and is honoured as the first bishop of that place. He and his monks kept a very strict rule, drinking only water and eating only bread and vegetables, in imitation of the monks of the Egyptian desert who joined manual work to study, and his house became a nursery of saints. A favourite ascetic practice of his own was to say the Psalms by heart standing plunged in cold water. He took part in the synod of Brefi in 545, where he is said to have been chosen archbishop by common consent and his monastery declared the mother church of all Wales; twelve monasteries are credited to him, and after his repose he began to be honoured widely in south Wales and in Ireland, Cornwall and Brittany. His relics are kept to this day in his cathedral at St Davids, and the page adds that the daffodil and the leek, the two national emblems of Wales, are said to have grown about the place of his monastery and that the leek was the staple of his own diet and his monks'.
