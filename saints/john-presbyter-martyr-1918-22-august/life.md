# John (Boyarshinov)

John Boyarshinov was born in 1858 to a craftsman’s family. After the
Ochyor primary school he served as psalmist at the church of Peter and
Paul of the Pavlovsky works in the Okhansk district, and was a worker of
the missionary department of the town of Okhansk; on 27 October 1900
Peter (Losev), bishop of Perm and Solikamsk, ordained him deacon to the
Dormition church of the village of Putino, where he taught the Law of God
at the people’s school, and on 9 September 1902 he was ordained priest for
the co-religionist church of the Archangel Michael at Sitnikovo in the
Okhansk district, heading its parish school. In 1904 he was moved back to
Putino, in charge of the Kizel parish school and catechist of the Putino
zemstvo school; on 4 April 1912 he was sent to the St Nicholas church of
Kuprosskoye in the Solikamsk district, where for bringing schismatics
back to Orthodoxy he was given the nabedrennik, and on 3 May 1918 he was
moved to the church of the Meeting of the Lord at Sepych in the Okhansk
district. Sepych was one of the oldest Old Believer settlements of the
upper Kama, peopled from the end of the seventeenth century by exiled
streltsy who had built the first schismatic sketes on the Sepych, Sabantsa,
Lysva and Ochyor rivers, living “like populous monasteries” of a hundred,
tonsuring themselves, praying morning and evening and singing the psalter
by the monastic rule, and it had become the centre of Urals Old Belief;
but by the 1830s an Orthodox church of the Meeting stood there, by the
mid-nineteenth century more than half the village had returned to
Orthodoxy, and the fanatical Old Believers kept apart, handing down their
ancient ways. The village had large holdings, much cattle, thriving
farmsteads, merchants and leather-workers — an exception among the poorer
north-western districts — and its peasants, living by tradition, did not
accept the events of 1917, keeping an elected zemstvo until the summer of
1918; the outrages of the requisition squads and committees of the poor
could not fail to rouse them, as they roused most of the Okhansk
peasantry — at nearby Bub a volost meeting in June 1918 declared that they
must elect a soviet and let no communists in: we have no need of
antichrists, we need Orthodox people. In early June the Sepych volost
soviet clashed with the all-Bolshevik Verashchagino soviet and refused to
recognise it or to have its Red Guards on its territory, warning that the
people would stop at nothing to resist them. On 1 June a Red Guard
detachment came to impose communist order and terrorised the volost with
illegal requisitions, beatings and random shooting; by August the Whites
had reached the Kama, Kazan fell on 6 August to the Czechs and the
People’s Army, the Bolsheviks ordered a broad mobilisation, the Izhevsk
and Votkinsk workers rose against it on 7 August with the wide support of
the Kama peasantry driven to despair by the food policy, and the
mobilisation announced in Perm province on 15 August was the last drop.
Meetings and mutinies of the mobilised broke out across the Okhansk
district, and at Sepych too the call-up set off the rising: from the
beginning of the summer, the villagers wrote afterwards, hatred had
ripened against the local Red Army, formed of poor and unenlightened
peasants who had turned part of the volost into a commune, and people who
knew God and conscience, unwilling to give their sons to the Red Army,
formed a secret organisation to disarm it. On 17 August, when Tunev’s Red
Guard detachment left for Kuliga, the village met, Orthodox and Old
Believers forgetting their old feuds that had often come to blows, chose
leaders, proclaimed a regiment of the People’s Army and a mobilisation of
the men, posted guards, sent a party to head off Tunev, disarmed the Red
Guards left in the village and herded many of them into a barn; Tunev,
outnumbered, fell back after a fight, and by 19 August nine neighbouring
volosts had raised companies of about a hundred each and were converging
on Sepych. The rebels cancelled the mobilisation and the grain levy, gave
out goods from the co-operative shop and bread and money to the poorest
families; and with the Izhevsk-Votkinsk insurgents fifty versts from the
Glazov–Verashchagino railway where Sepych patrols were already riding, a
junction would have cut the whole Perm group of the Red Army off from the
centre. The Bolsheviks threw in their last reserves — workers’ detachments
from Ochyor, Pavlovsk and Motovilikha and an international company of
Finns from Perm — and half-encircled the village; at four in the morning
of 21 August about a hundred horsemen with some infantry tried to take it
by storm and were met with rifle fire from three sides and thrown back by
ten with heavy losses, and all day the Red detachments closed in under
positional fire. The staff and observation post of the Sepych regiment
were in the Orthodox church of the Meeting, from whose bell-tower signals
were given during the fighting by strokes of the bell — a tactic the
Bolsheviks did not miss: the priest of the Sepych church, wrote Vorobtsov
of the provincial Cheka, rang the bells during the battle, two strokes
meaning retreat and three attack, making the church in which he had
preached so much an observation post for signalling. Early on 22 August
Red detachments with artillery came up from Perm, Okhansk and
Verashchagino; wavering had begun among the rebels the evening before,
half of whom were armed only with pikes, crowbars and flails, and
agitators drafted a letter — we, citizens herded here by force, do not
want to fight the Soviet power; we will lay down our arms and go home —
won over a wavering company at Fedoseyevka, freed and armed the hundred
Red Guards in the barn, seized many of the leaders, and the rebels, at the
news of the Okhansk detachment’s guns and machine-guns, threw down their
arms and went home; at midday the Reds entered Sepych unopposed. On 25
August a Cheka detachment with an investigating commission arrived from
Perm, arrested the ringleaders and participants, and passed resolutions
to shoot eighty-three men found by the inquiry to be the chief culprits —
kulaks, priests and officers, Vorobtsov reported — carried out on the
spot. On 2 September 1918 the commission resolved that the local priest
Boyarshinov had taken part in the counter-revolutionary rising, since the
church bells had rung the tocsin throughout it and given the agreed signs
of retreat and advance, the bell-tower had been made an observation post,
the priest had opened the church and allowed it, and had himself been a
participant in the White Guard bands’ rising; and that since such persons,
going against the people, ought not to exist in the Soviet Federative
Republic, citizen Boyarshinov be declared outside the law and shot. On 4
September 1918 — 22 August of the old style, the day the Russian calendar
keeps him — he was shot. Some of the leaders escaped, the villagers wrote
in “Free Perm” the next March, some were shot by the hands of the traitor
communists, some fled through the Izhevsk front to the People’s Army, many
peasants paid contributions — and the priest of Sepych was savagely shot:
bright memory to the honest martyrs fallen by the traitors’ hand. The
jubilee Council of Bishops of 2000 glorified him among the new martyrs and
confessors of Russia; the Russian calendar keeps him also in the synaxis
of the saints of Perm.
