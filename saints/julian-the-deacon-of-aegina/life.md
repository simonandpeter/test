# Julian of Aegina

saint.gr says Julian was born in the year 319 on Aegina, of well-off and devout parents who brought him up in the learning and admonition of the Lord. He studied at Athens together with, in the page's words, the saints [Basil](/saints/basil-the-great) and Gregory; it does not say which Gregory, and nothing more is said of him here than the page says. When Julian came back to Aegina he resolved, together with the presbyter Julius, to imitate the Apostle of the Nations Paul and to preach Christ.

The bishop of Athens ordained Julian a deacon. Adorned with the grace of the priesthood, he went out with the presbyter Julius to preach the Gospel and to baptise many of the gentiles. At the end of his life he withdrew to Gozzano on Lake Maggiore, where, after ascetic labour and prayer, he fell asleep in peace in the year 391. The page prints no hymn for him and nothing else.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 21 June, together with his brother, and it is the first of the three calendars to keep the two of them on one day rather than on two. It calls them brothers by birth, which the Greek page does not: brought up in Christianity from childhood, with a vow to live always in virginity and to serve the church, [Julius](/saints/julius-of-aegina) a presbyter and Julian a deacon. It has them obtain from the emperor it names a charter allowing them to pull down the idols throughout the empire and to build Christian churches — which cannot be squared with the year of his repose above, and is written here as this book has it and reconciled with nothing. That emperor is named in the attestation note and not here, his name being a corpus folder’s own, and an emperor who grants a charter being no relation.

Like two apostles, it says, the two brothers turned unbelievers into Christians through the East and the West, and built churches; and they built a hundred churches in their lifetime. They fell asleep in peace in the Lord near Mediolanum, which is the city that Gozzano and Lake Maggiore stand near, so that the two accounts end in the same country if not in the same place.

The Russian calendar's own account of the two brothers has a stretch of narrative neither of the books already quoted here carries. They got leave to build churches, it says, and went out to preach in the remote places of the East and the West of the Roman empire, where the idol-shrines still stood; and at Constantinople they asked the emperor «Феодосий Младший» for leave to raise churches on the sites of those shrines. With the patriarch's blessing and the emperor's permission they built a great many, and the people held it their duty to help in the work.

Two things the page tells at length. Some men driving past a church that was being built, afraid of being asked to help, pretended that one of them was a corpse they were taking to burial; asked whether they were lying, they held to it, and the deacon said, then let it be to you according to your word — and a little way on they found that the feigned dead man was dead indeed. And the presbyter, foreseeing his own end, went to look for a place for the hundredth church, came to a lake, and saw in the middle of it an island nobody could live on for the snakes that bred there; he prayed, crossed to it on his mantle as on a boat, planted a cross, and in God's name called all the snakes together and bade them leave the island to the house of God.

He laid the foundation of a church of the Twelve Apostles on that island. His brother, meanwhile, finished a church near a town and set about building a tomb beside it for the presbyter; and when the presbyter came to visit him he told him mildly to hurry with the building, foretelling that it was he himself who would lie in it. So it was: the deacon died soon after and was buried in the tomb he had built, and the presbyter buried him with honour, returned to his island, died there, and was buried in his own church of the Twelve Apostles, where many of the sick were healed at his grave. The page sets the end of both in the first half of the fifth century.
