# Vladimir Moshchansky

Vladimir Moshchansky was born on 15 June 1866 in the village of Zaborovye
in the Yesenovichi district of Tver province, the son of the priest
Demetrius Moshchansky; his mother Anna, widowed early and very devout, fed
the poor and the beggars, and when she died they all came to bury her —
only then did many see how many she had helped. After the Tver seminary he
married Eudocia, daughter of the priest John Dimitrievsky, and was soon
ordained deacon to the church of Rogachevo in the Rzhev district; a
daughter and three sons were born to them, and at the birth of the fourth
child Eudocia died, leaving him with small children whom her unmarried
sister Alexandra helped him raise. After a time he was ordained priest of
the Dormition church at Spasskoye in the Kashin district, where he served
until the Bolsheviks closed the church in 1924. At the last service the
church was full, everyone on their knees, many weeping, and the whole
parish walked the thirty kilometres to Kashin to see him off. He settled
at Vyshny Volochyok in his late father’s house and served in the winter
cathedral; he did not stop serving as the persecution hardened, and went
about the town in his cassock, preaching the word of God by his dress. In
his free hours he taught his granddaughter Olga every subject from the old
pre-revolutionary textbooks, and before all the prayers, so that when the
school tested her they put her straight into the fifth class. Once the
school set her a rhyme to learn — I didn’t go to church today and didn’t
confess to the priest, I don’t even want to see such a crocodile — and she
recited it at home in all simplicity; Alexandra gasped, and Father Vladimir
was silent, looked at her carefully, and said: and did you think, Olya,
that your grandfather is a priest? — and she fell on her knees and asked
forgiveness. As his health failed he could not always serve the Liturgy,
but came to church to pray, baptised the children and served panikhidas at
the Pyatnitskoye cemetery, where his father had served at the Elijah church
and the family grave lay behind its altar; and before going to it he would
go to another part of the cemetery, where there were then no graves, and
serve a panikhida and pray there, as if it had been revealed to him that
he would lie not in the family plot but in a prisoner’s grave.

On 13 February 1938, in his seventy-second year, he was arrested and charged
with anti-Soviet agitation. He did not admit it, and of all the witnesses’
statements confirmed only one, a conversation with schoolchildren of the
eighth and ninth classes in November 1937 in which he had said: the
scientist Pavlov asked before he died that the church buildings not be
pulled down or turned into museums — you see, even the scientists follow
religion. At his trial on 27 June 1938 he answered that if he had ever said
anything it was not for agitation; he was sentenced to seven years and
three years’ loss of rights, appealed, and was held in the Vyshny Volochyok
prison while the appeal was considered, in cells so full that the prisoners
stood almost round the clock, with no room even to lie on the floor, and
fell out into the corridor when the door was opened. He kept a strict fast
in prison and gave away almost every parcel Alexandra brought; his health
went from bad to worse and the administration did nothing, until the men in
his cell rose and beat on the iron door shouting, why do you torment an old
priest, what has he done, we believe in God too — and nothing changed. He
died there on 25 August 1938, 7 September of the new style. The prisoners
made his coffin themselves and dressed him in the clean linen he had kept
against his death, and the cart carried him to the Pyatnitskoye cemetery
past his own house; Alexandra, not knowing, went on bringing parcels to the
prison for days. Then the old priest began to appear before the eyes of the
prison’s quartermaster, wherever he went, whatever he did, by night as well
as day, until at dawn on the ninth day the man walked to the cemetery
street, stopped without knowing why at the priest’s house and knocked at
the window: get dressed and come out, your grandfather has died, I will
show you the grave — and led her to it, on the spot where the priest had
served his panikhidas in life. A cross stands there now, and his son and
daughter, Vladimir and Olga, are buried beside him. He was canonised for
the Tver diocese on 19 September 1999 and for the whole church by the
Jubilee Council of Bishops of August 2000; the Russian calendar keeps him
on 25 August (Julian).
