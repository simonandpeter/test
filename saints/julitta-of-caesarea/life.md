# Julitta of Caesarea

Julitta came of a very rich family, and for that reason a man of Caesarea in Cappadocia coveted her property. She went to law against him for what was hers and called him before the judge. But the decrees of the emperor Diocletian held Christians to be outside the laws and outside civil rights; and the man, having no way of defending himself, accused Julitta of being a Christian.

The judge, wanting to satisfy himself that it was so, called for fire and incense so that she should sacrifice to the idols. Because she refused to do it, she was thrown into the fire. Her body was found almost whole, so that it is thought she may have died of the smoke. She was buried in the porch of the great church in Caesarea.

That is all doxologia.ro prints of her. The page gives no year and names no other person, and the day's service on that site carries no hymn of hers.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 30 July, and sets her in another reign and in another company. saint.gr says Julitta lived at Caesarea in Cappadocia in the days of [Basil the Great](/saints/basil-the-great), who had a great regard for her because of her piety and her many virtues. She had a large property, the page says, and was warred upon by a certain covetous and grasping man of power, who entangled her in lawsuits and set false witnesses against her. Julitta begged Basil to protect her; and he, knowing that she was in the right, agreed, and wrote to Palladius, a good and God-fearing man, to speak for her before the prefect.

To Julitta herself Basil wrote to strengthen her, that «δυνατὸς δὲ ὁ ἅγιος (θεός) διαγαγεὶν σὲ πάσης θλίψεως, μόνον ἐὰν ἀληθινὴ καὶ γνησία καρδία ἐλπίσωμεν ἐπ’ αὐτόν» — the Holy One is able to bring you through every affliction, if only we hope in him with a true and genuine heart. In the end her adversary denounced her for reviling the decrees of Julian in favour of idolatry. Asked about it, Julitta answered that she did condemn idolatry and that it was her duty to strengthen Christians in the true faith; and for that confession she was condemned and thrown into the fire. Her body remained whole, and the faithful found consolation and healing in it.

So the Greek page puts her under Julian and in Basil's lifetime where the Romanian page puts her under the decrees of Diocletian. Both accounts are kept here as they are printed; this life does not choose between them.

The Охридски пролог keeps her on the Julian 31 July, a day later than the other two calendars, and tells the Romanian account’s story in four sentences of its own. She was of Caesarea in Cappadocia, it says, and had a dispute with her neighbour over property. He went to the court and declared that Julitta was a Christian — which at that time, the book explains, meant the same as being outside the protection of the law. Saint Julitta gave up her property sooner than her faith. But the malicious heathen did not leave her in peace even after that; they tortured her and at last burned her in the fire, and so this follower of Christ gave up both her property and her body for the eternal salvation of her soul.

That book adds the one thing neither of the other two has, a year: 303. It does not settle anything. 303 stands with the Romanian account, which puts her under the decrees of Diocletian, and against the Greek one, which has her in the lifetime of [Basil the Great](/saints/basil-the-great) and under the decrees of Julian some sixty years later. Three books, two reigns and one figure between them: the year is recorded here as the Prologue prints it, and this folder claims none of the three datings as its own.
