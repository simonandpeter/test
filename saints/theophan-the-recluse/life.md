# Theophan the Recluse

Theophan was called George Vasilievich Govorov in the world, and was born on 10 January 1815 in a village near Orel where his father was a priest, so that he stood in the Church from his first steps. He studied at the preparatory theological school at Livny and then at the seminary of Orel, and from 1837 to 1841 at the Theological Academy of Kyiv. He went often to the caves of the Kyiv Caves Lavra, and it was in those times of recollection, the life says, that the resolve to leave the world took shape in him; he was tonsured just before he finished his courses.

After his tonsure he went with other newly tonsured monks to the well-known elder Parthenius at the Lavra, who told them that learned monks take on all sorts of rules, but that one thing is needful above all: to pray, to pray unceasingly in the mind and the heart to God. Ordained hieromonk, he was made acting director of the Sophia Theological School in Kyiv, then director of the Novgorod seminary, then a teacher and assistant inspector at the Academy in Petersburg.

Purely scholastic work did not satisfy him and he asked to be released from the academic service. He was appointed to the Russian Mission in Jerusalem; then, raised archimandrite, rector of the Olonets seminary; then transferred to Constantinople as first priest of the embassy church; then recalled to Petersburg as rector of the Theological Academy and inspector of religion for the capital's lay schools. On 9 May 1859 he was consecrated bishop of Tambov, where he founded a school for girls, and where he first came to love the secluded hermitage of Vysha. In the summer of 1863 he was moved to Vladimir, and served there three years, opening a diocesan school for girls, preaching constantly, travelling through the diocese and restoring churches.

In 1861 he was present at the uncovering of the relics of [Tikhon of Zadonsk](/saints/tikhon-of-zadonsk), whom he had loved since childhood and who had also left a diocese to become a spiritual guide to the whole of Russia; the life says the two had much in common, and that the joy of the glorification was beyond expressing. In 1866 Theophan asked to be released from the see of Vladimir and was appointed head of the Vysha hermitage, and soon, at his own further request, released even from that. He took leave of his flock on Sunday 2 July 1866, after a last sermon heard in a graveside silence broken by stifled weeping.

So began twenty-eight years of solitary life. For the first six he went to every service and early Liturgy, standing without moving, his eyes shut so that nothing would distract him. From 1872 he broke off contact with everyone but his confessor and the protopresbyter, no longer went to the monastery church, and built with his own hands a chapel in his cell dedicated to the Baptism of the Lord, where he served the Liturgy alone — for ten years on Sundays and feasts, and for the eleven years after that every day, sometimes in silence and sometimes singing.

He seemed no longer a mere man, the life says, but an angel, full of humility and a childlike gentleness: when anyone came to him with a trouble he said what was needed and sank back into prayer. He ate only enough not to ruin his health and sent what he received to the poor, keeping back what would buy the books he needed. He painted icons and was skilled at woodcarving and locksmithing. He received between twenty and forty letters a day and answered all of them, entering with extraordinary sensitivity into the state of whoever had written. His books — on the spiritual life, on the way of salvation, on prayer and sobriety, and commentaries on the Pauline epistles and on Psalms 33 and 118 — spoke out of what he had passed through himself.

His life went unseen by the world and death found him in the same solitude. In his last years his sight began to fail, but he did not give up his work. From 1 January 1891 there were irregularities in his order of the day. At half past four in the afternoon of 6 January his cell attendant, uneasy at his weakness over the last days, came into the room and found him dead on the bed, the left hand on his breast and the right laid as though ready to give the bishop's blessing. His body lay three days in the chapel of his cell and three more in the cathedral, and showed no sign of corruption.
