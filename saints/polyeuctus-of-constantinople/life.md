# Polyeuctus

Polyeuctus was born at Constantinople and became a monk while still young in a monastery on the island of Proti. He lived simply and with self-restraint, and many times fed himself on a piece of dry bread so as to feed the poor. He had a great theological education, and was marked out by his modesty, his character, his continence and the fairness of his judgement; many, the page says, likened him to St John Chrysostom.

When the patriarch Theophylact died in April 956, Polyeuctus was consecrated patriarch of Constantinople. In 957 he baptised at Constantinople the Russian ruler [Olga](/saints/olga-of-kyiv), who, as some say, was named Helen. He was a rule of virtue and godliness, and so did not fear to call the emperor Nikephoros Phokas to account when he decided to marry the empress Theophano, widow of the emperor Romanos II; and because the marriage had been performed without his knowledge, he refused to receive the emperor at the Liturgy in Hagia Sophia. Afterwards he gave his forgiveness and consent, to order matters and not bring the state into chaos.

Nikephoros Phokas fell to a savage murder, made the more loathsome because the empress Theophano took part in it and made it easy. Polyeuctus was shaken by the terrible and unholy crime; and so when John I Tzimiskes came to Hagia Sophia seven days after his accession, to be crowned by the patriarch, the saint would not let him enter the sanctuary, and demanded first that the emperor fulfil three conditions: that Theophano be driven from the palace; that he name and punish the man who killed Nikephoros Phokas; and that he revoke Phokas's decrees about church affairs.

So, the page says, Polyeuctus secured for the Church its true freedom, and gave it the right to act against the excesses of political rulers when these harm the Church and butcher the traditions of the people of God. His love for monasticism and the ascetic life showed itself too in the founding, in his days, of the monasteries of the Great Lavra and of Iviron on the Holy Mountain. He fell asleep in peace in the year 970. The page prints no hymn for him; its couplet says that he who bore the name of the much-prayed-for obtained the end he had prayed for.
