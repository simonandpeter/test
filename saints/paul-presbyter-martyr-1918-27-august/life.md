# Paul (Fokin)

Paul Fokin was born in 1883 to a cleric’s family; his mother baked the
prosphora. Besides Paul two more sons were raised in the family, who
later became priests, and two daughters, Eugenia and Vera; like his
brothers he chose the spiritual road and entered the Perm seminary, and
after two years married Taisia Vsevolodovna, the daughter of well-off
townsfolk, and five children were born to them — a son Nicholas and
daughters Lydia, Zinaida, Nina and Eugenia. Leaving the seminary he was
ordained deacon in 1900 and served twelve years in the Perm diocese; in
March 1912 he was moved to Ekaterinburg, to the cathedral of St
Catherine, where he served in a psalmist’s vacancy, and in December of
that year was ordained priest and sent to the church of the Meeting of
the Lord in the village of Murzinskoye in the Verkhoturye district. The
village, on the Neiva a hundred and ninety kilometres from Verkhoturye,
was founded in the first half of the seventeenth century, sacked by the
Bashkirs in 1662 — peasants killed, the first wooden church and houses
burned, cattle driven off — and given in the eighteenth century a new
stone church with altars of the Meeting, St Nicholas and the martyr
Paraskeva, whose icon, its chief treasure, drew pilgrims from far and wide
and was dressed by their gifts in a silver-gilt riza set with the stones
of the local mines, amethysts, aquamarines and golden topazes. By the
turn of the century the village had some five hundred people, a zemstvo
school, shops, six smithies and a mill, and beside farming the peasants
dug and cut gems — Italians brought to Russia in the eighteenth century
to find building stone for the new capital had found topaz, beryl and
amethyst there, and since then men, women and even children cut stones
that went through dealers to Petersburg, Moscow and Perm — which left its
mark on the villagers’ morals: the digging was done in spring, before or
during Great Lent, and a good find was drunk, to the clergy’s constant
trouble. Five years he served at Murzinskoye, enjoying, for his zealous
labours, the trust and love of the whole community, and raising five
small children with his wife. Then came 1917: after February the
outrages against the Church and its servants began — priests handled
roughly, men in hats in church, cigarettes lit from the candles — and
after October the mass terror against every real and imagined enemy of
Bolshevism, believers among them; the Cheka was founded in December 1917
and its local organs spread through the spring and summer of 1918, and
though terror was proclaimed officially only on 5 September 1918, in the
Urals it was loosed months earlier by scattered Red Guard detachments of
workers, revolutionary soldiers, former political exiles and released
criminals, marked by utter licence, senseless and often perverted
cruelty, mockery of everything holy and almost no control — churches
robbed with unbelievable blasphemies, sacred objects hung on pack animals,
icons defaced, their eyes put out and cigarettes stuck in their mouths,
shrouds and even antimensia cut up, the Holy Gifts scattered in the
sanctuary, every valuable carried off — and almost everywhere they came
the clergy were arrested, at home, in the street, on the road or at the
altar, on charges of counter-revolution, of Cadet or bourgeois sympathies
or of sermons against the Soviet power, which sufficed for death with
cruel torments. This touched the clergy of Murzinskoye directly. Of all
its clergy Fr Paul was evidently the most zealous and active, preaching
and talking with his parishioners in person, which was counted anti-Soviet
agitation; under threat of arrest and the revolutionary tribunal the
clergy of the church were officially forbidden “all agitation”, and on 8
June 1918 he signed an undertaking that he would not preach against the
Soviet power from the ambo, in meetings and common prayers or in private
talk, acknowledged the local and state power as lawful and intended no
opposition to its orders. About then a military collegium was set up in
the village at the regional soviet’s suggestion, of “persons of unenviable
reputation”, as the paper “Urals Life” wrote afterwards, with unlimited
powers — your whole life is in our hands, they told the villagers, and we
do as we please — which now and then called in punitive detachments to
restore “order”: the deacon Alexander Zemlyannikov, a member of the volost
board and book-keeper of the consumers’ society, was arrested first, held
seven days in prison at Alapaevsk and released on condition of leaving the
village at once. Soon the mobilisation into the Red Army began there as
everywhere in the Urals, and the whole male population refused to join;
the collegium reported it to Nizhny Tagil, whence a punitive detachment
came at once under the commissar Chugunov, who levied a contribution of
ten thousand roubles and ordered the arrest of the priest and of seventeen
men liable to mobilisation, all sent to Alapaevsk — and there, in the
night of 9 September 1918 — 27 August of the old style, the day the
Russian calendar keeps him — he was shot. The shootings at Alapaevsk were
done near the railway station, where in a few months of 1918 about three
hundred people met death: the railwaymen told how the Reds shot first
beside the station beyond the semaphore, then carried their victims out of
town in goods wagons — an old man who could not climb in was whipped
until, mad with pain, he climbed, or shot or flogged to death on the spot
— and how, in the sandy ground nearby where the townsfolk had long dug
sand, leaving pits with burrow-like approaches, they brought a prisoner or
hostage to a burrow, shot him, climbed on top and jumped until the upper
layer caved in, sparing themselves the digging of graves; in one of those
places, it seems, the priest Paul Fokin was killed. His widow was left
with five small children and no means; that autumn, as the Whites freed
the Verkhoturye district, the diocesan assembly at Ekaterinburg under
Bishop Gregory resolved on help for the families of clergy killed by the
Bolsheviks, hers among them — monthly collections from the clergy for
three years, church collections, readings, lectures and concerts in memory
of the murdered priests, so that, the diocesan gazette wrote, the poor
orphans should have help in time in their hopeless plight and know that
the whole diocese shared their grief and that the priests at the altar
would pray for the martyr priests fallen at their post — but the return of
Bolshevik rule in the summer of 1919 made such help impossible. He was
glorified in the synaxis of the new martyrs and confessors of Russia for
the Ekaterinburg diocese on 17 July 2002; the Russian calendar keeps him
also in the synaxis of the saints of Ekaterinburg.
