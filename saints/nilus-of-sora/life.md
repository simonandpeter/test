# Nilus of Sora

He was born in 1433 into a good family of Moscow that loved Christ, and wishing to take the good yoke of the ascetic struggle he became a monk in the monastery of Saint Cyril at the White Lake. Afterwards he withdrew into stillness and made himself a wooden cell, where he laboured as a great hermit. Hearing of the purity of his life, many young men came to him and became monks, and that hermitage grew famous in the north of Russia.

Twice a week, on Wednesday and on Saturday evening, all the brothers gathered for an all-night vigil; in the morning they heard the Liturgy and communicated of the most pure Mysteries, and then each returned to his cell, thinking on holy things and praying without ceasing. They fed themselves by the work of their hands, did not receive lay people and above all not women, and avoided heavy farm work, speaking unceasingly with Christ. So Nilus became known through all Russia as a great spiritual father.

In 1503 he argued at a council that the monasteries should be released from the administration of the villages and the great agricultural estates given to them, which draw monks away from prayer and from a pure life in Christ; on the contrary he urged all to the hesychast life and to the practice of unceasing prayer. This, the page says, troubled the Orthodox Church of Russia for a long while.

In old age he withdrew to the stillness he loved, in the hermitage he had founded, which was called Sorska and was famous in all northern Russia, and gave his last years to the study of Holy Scripture and of the writings of the Fathers. Seeing himself old and burdened with days he composed a testament for his burial, in which he wrote, among other things: after my death, throw my body into a waste place, for the animals and the birds of heaven to tear, for it has sinned so much against God that it is unworthy of burial. On 7 May 1508 he gave his soul into the hands of Christ, and his disciples wept much for him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on this day and names him Nilus of Sora at once. He came of the noble family of the Maikovs, saint.gr says, and was born at Moscow in 1433 of devout parents; as a young man he worked as a shorthand writer in the service of the great prince of Moscow. His love of the monastic life took him to the monastery of the White Lake, which the ascetic Cyril had founded, and a little later to the Holy Mountain with his fellow monk Innocent, where he lived ascetically in the Skete of the Carpenter; there is also a report, the page adds, that he stayed a long while at the monastery of Saint Dionysius on Athos, which was then at its height.

He and Innocent returned to Russia about 1480, and his search for stillness took him to a small deserted place a little beyond the White Lake monastery, on the river Sora, where he built a small church in honour of the Meeting of the Lord and gave himself to the contemplative life and unceasing prayer. Even there he counselled caution and discrimination: no one should enter the higher spiritual regions before his time, and every initiation into the spiritual life should be careful and by degrees. Measure in the ascetic life and discretion in the way one deals with other people are, the page says, the marks of his spirituality; humility is the mark of his life, and he is above all the saint of self-emptying. To be useful to his brethren he would accept to be taken for a fool, and so disclose to them the secrets of the spiritual life.

His holiness and his theological training drew the attention of the church authorities and of the state. In Novgorod the heresy of the Judaizers had broken out, who said among other things that in the year 7000 from the creation of the world, that is in 1492, the world would end; and he helped the Church and the state to form a right understanding of it and to condemn it. The other matter he faced was the property of the monasteries, for he knew well the great danger that threatened monks from the piling up of wealth in a house, even wealth that belonged to the house and not to them. It was discussed at a council called at Moscow in 1503, and it appears that for pastoral reasons the council did not adopt his mind; yet that mind of his influenced Russian monasticism for good.

His writings are many, and the chief of them are ascetic and concern the ascetic and the hermit life. He fell asleep in peace on the Sunday of the Myrrhbearers in 1508 — a day the Greek gives as moveable, though the calendar keeps his memory on 7 May.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him not on 7 May but on the Julian 7 April, and gives him four sentences. He was one of the great fathers of the Russian church, it says, and the founder of the skete life of monks in Russia; he reposed in peace in 1508; his relics rest in the monastery of Sora; and his Rule of the skete life is a spiritual and practical work of the first order. The resting of the relics is worth setting beside the testament above, in which he asked that his body be thrown into a waste place for the animals and the birds to tear: neither of the other calendars read for this folder says where the relics came to lie. The day the Prologue keeps him on is a month from the 7 May the Romanian and the Greek keep, and not a conversion of style; both days stand, each on its own church's row, and neither was moved to meet the other.
