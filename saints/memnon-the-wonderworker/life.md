# Memnon the Wonderworker

Memnon gave himself to God from his youth, the life says, and became a pure dwelling of the Holy Spirit; for he subjected the body to the spirit, putting the passions to death by a hard manner of life and many labours, and he was set over the monks. For a life like the angels' he received from God an abundant gift of wonderworking, healing incurable diseases and doing many miracles.

In a dry place he brought out a spring of water by his prayer. A ship caught in a storm and going down in the swell of the sea he delivered from drowning many times, appearing to those aboard. Locusts that had come down and were destroying every kind of fruit and grass he drove away. And other wonders past telling he did, and for this he was called the wonderworker.

He struggled in the desert for many years and pleased God by his amended life, and went rejoicing to the Lord whom he had loved. Nor did the wonders stop at his departure: from his grave healings of every disease were given, and at the calling of his name the locusts and every harm of the devil were driven off. The page gives no year, no country and no monastery by name.

A different Memnon, the centurion who suffered with Severian and is kept on 20 August, stands elsewhere in this corpus and is not this man.

The Greek calendar keeps him on 28 Απριλίου, a day earlier than the Romanian, and saint.gr's notice adds what he did with the monastery's money. Whenever the farming people of his district fell into great straits, it says, Memnon carried them help out of the monastery's savings, of which he had been made abbot; and he would go to other cities as well, collecting contributions, and with those he made up what was wanting to the relief of the suffering. Of the locusts it says only that swarms of them once covered the villagers' fields, that the people could do nothing and watched the ruin of their crops in tears, and that he prayed fervently and obtained the deliverance of the place.

At his death, men, women and children ran to accompany his funeral with the warmest tears and the most heartfelt prayers. The page notes that in some synaxaristes his memory is repeated on 19 May, and it prints one hymn only, a kathisma, which is neither a troparion nor a kontakion and was therefore not taken.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, with the Romanian calendar and not with the Greek, which keeps him a day earlier; and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović gives him in four sentences, of which one is the century. From his youth Memnon gave himself to fasting and to prayer, and cleansed himself so far that he became a dwelling of the Holy Spirit. He healed sick men who could not be healed, and did many other wonders. He appeared in the storms at sea, and saved ships from destruction.

**He fell asleep in peace in the Lord in the second century**, the book says, and passed over into the heavenly courts of his Lord. That century is the first date this folder has carried: doxologia's life says in so many words that it gives no year, no country and no monastery by name, and the Greek notice gives no year either. The Prologue has nothing of the spring brought out of a dry place, nothing of the locusts, and nothing of the monastery's savings carried to the farming people; the appearing in the sea storms is the one wonder all three accounts tell.

The Russian calendar's own reading of him is three short paragraphs and it is the fullest account any of this folder's sources gives. He struggled from his youth in the Egyptian desert and reached the victory of the spirit over the flesh by hard fasting; made abbot of one of the Egyptian monasteries, he governed the brethren wisely and carefully, helping them by prayer and by counsel without slackening his own struggle against temptation. By unceasing prayer and labour he received the gift of discernment, and the page names three things done at his prayer: a spring of water opened in the desert, the locust that was destroying the crops died, and men in shipwreck who called on him were saved. After his death, it says, the mere calling of his name drove off the locust and broke any device of the spirits of malice. It still does not name the monastery.
