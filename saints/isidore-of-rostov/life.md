# Isidore of Rostov

Isidore, whom saint.gr calls Tverdislov — faithful to the Word — was born in Germany of rich parents and brought up in a Roman Catholic setting, and was later converted to the Orthodox faith. From his youth he lived an ascetic life and carried in his heart a great compassion for people. Desiring the Kingdom of God, he left his father's house, distributed his property to the poor, and began to live as a wanderer. At last he reached Russia and decided to live at Rostov.

There he lived in the snow and the cold, bearing every insult, and settled in a rickety wooden hut he built himself. He chose a way of life unintelligible for the Name of Christ, the kind the Apostle Paul describes in his first letter to the Corinthians. He passed his time in unceasing prayer, allowing himself little sleep or rest, standing awake all night and glorifying God; by day he went the rounds of the city, acting as a fool. Like Job of old in his patience, the page says, he was while still alive both an earthly angel and a heavenly man — a compassionate soul, pure in thought, with a wakeful heart, a faith that knows no shame and a true love without hypocrisy — and in his lifetime God counted him worthy to work wonders.

He fell asleep in peace in 1474, and the Christians learnt of his falling asleep only when, passing outside his hut, they smelt the fragrance his holy relic gave off. On the place of his burial the church of the Ascension of the Lord was built, and his relics are kept in it. He bears the name Tverdislov, the page explains, because he spoke continually of Jesus Christ. He is not the sailor [Isidore](/saints/isidore-of-chios) whom the same day keeps.

The Ohrid Prologue, read for the Julian 14 May, tells his wonders at a length the Greek page did not, and dates his death ten years later than it does. He was a German by origin, it says. Coming to Rostov he came to love the Orthodox faith, and not only became a member of the Orthodox Church but took upon himself the heavy struggle of folly for Christ. He spent his nights in a hut of branches which he had built himself in a marsh. Great and fearful wonders this saint showed, both in his life and after his death.

Two of them the book tells. A merchant who had been thrown out of a ship and was drowning in the sea saw Isidore come to him walking upon the water, and was brought by him to the shore. And in the house of the prince of Rostov all the vessels of wine dried up at once, when the prince's servants drove Isidore away from the door and would not give him even the cup of water he had asked for.

When he died — in the year 1484, on the fourteenth of May, in his own hut — the whole of Rostov was filled with a wonderful fragrance. The Greek page puts that death in 1474 instead, so the year now stands as the two of them and neither is chosen; the day the book gives is the day all three calendars keep him on. And the merchant whom the Blessed had saved at sea raised him a church on the place where his hut had been.

The Russian church calendar keeps him on the same menologion day, 14 мая ст. ст., calls him Твердислов — a by-name none of the other calendars here gives him — and dates him 1474, which is the earlier of the two years this folder already carries. It gives him no life. Behind its line stands a name page and nothing else: a heading, «Исидор Твердислов, Ростовский, Христа ради юродивый», two days, and a link to an article on the site which was not opened for this folder. The second of the two days, 23 мая, is marked «(Ростов.)», the synaxis of the saints of Rostov, which is a company's feast and not a day of his own.

What the Russian does give him is a service. The day page prints five hymns under his name — three troparia and two kontakia — and the folder had none at all before. They are all about the same two things: a life of chastity and patience finished well, and a body kept unharmed after death that pours out healings on those who come with faith to his tomb and to the shrine of his relics. One of the kontakia asks him to keep those who observe his memory from all malice and from the serpent's snare. All five are copied into this folder with the tones the page gives them.
