# Peter of Alexandria

Peter, archbishop of Alexandria, was brought up from childhood by his predecessor in that city, whom doxologia's life names as the blessed Thomas, and was raised to the archiepiscopal throne after him, in those days when the persecution from the pagan emperors was cruel and hard to bear, when the prisons were narrow for the martyrs of Christ and their blood watered the cities, the streets and the fields.

In such fearful times, full of hard straits and affliction, he governed the Church of God with great patience and unspeakable labour. By his teaching and by the pattern of his own courage, unconquered in the faith, he strengthened many who were weak of soul and fearful, kept them from falling, and brought a great multitude to the martyr's crown. At last he too was driven out for Christ, and going through Tyre, through Phoenicia and through Palestine he strengthened his reasoning sheep by letters and filled them with the grace of the Holy Spirit.

Fearing that somebody might be frightened by the torments into denying Christ, he lifted his hands day and night in prayer for them. Then, returning to Alexandria, he served those who were shut in the prisons for the holy faith, whose number was six hundred and sixty, among them a multitude of priests and clerics, all of whom were killed with various torments; and at their suffering, endured to the end, he rejoiced in spirit.

While he was shepherding the flock well there appeared in Alexandria a ravening wolf in sheep's clothing, Arius the heretic, who began to sow the tares of his accursed teaching in the middle of the wheat, blaspheming the divinity of our Lord Jesus Christ. The good shepherd often stopped that cunning wolf's mouth, disputing with him, rebuking him and warning him not to spoil the good confession of the Holy Trinity given spotless to the Church; but he stayed uncorrected, a second Judas, and would not bend to righteousness. So Peter cursed the blasphemer, cut him off from the Church and cast him out as worthless.

Driven from the flock, Arius hid himself like darkness before the light, and did not dare come near such a shepherd, whom no cunning or artful words could deceive; and the Church in Alexandria grew and multiplied, for the tyranny could no longer bar the way to Christ of people who sought salvation and who put not only their goods but their lives in peril to run to his teaching.

When the pagan emperor Maximin, who then ruled the eastern parts and was living at Nicomedia, learned that many were turning from the idols to Christ by Peter's teaching, he sent tribunes against him; and they, seeing the great uproar of the people, kept him in the prison beside the church and wrote to the emperor, who ordered him beheaded. When the people saw armed soldiers coming to the prison to take him out and kill him, they cried out with one voice that the soldiers should kill them all first.

Arius, learning that the archbishop who had cut him off from the apostolic Church was in bonds and was to be killed for Christ, came cunningly and had his friends beg the saint to forgive him. Peter, weeping and sighing, answered that they did not know for whom they were pleading: they pleaded for one who wanted to pull down the Church of Christ. Not by his judgement, he said, but by God's was Arius condemned, for he had not sinned against a man but against God, blaspheming the mystery of the Holy Trinity, on which the Cherubim and Seraphim do not dare to look.

For God had shown him what was in Arius's heart. Taking aside the priests [Achillas](/saints/achillas) and [Alexander](/saints/alexander-of-alexandria) he told them how the Lord had appeared to him by night as a child whose garment was torn, and how in fear he had asked: *Saviour, who has torn your garment?* — and the Lord answered: *Arius the shameless has torn it.* Therefore he charged them not to receive Arius, and told them that Achillas would go up to the throne after him and Alexander after Achillas. At that all who were pleading for Arius fell at his feet and did not dare press him further.

Then Peter, seeing that the Christian people in their zeal would not let the emperor's envoy take him out of the prison to death, and fearing a war between the Christians and the soldiers, arranged his own end. At night the tribune came with the soldiers and dug secretly through the back of the prison, a hole wide enough for one man, since it was not possible to come at the doors for the crowd of people; and so he went out to them.

When they came to the place of execution, called Boukolou, where the Evangelist [Mark](/saints/mark-the-evangelist) had earlier finished his martyrdom, he asked the soldiers' leave to go down inside the apostle's tomb. The soldiers, put in awe by his goodness, stood numb and unmoving, and not one of them had the courage to kill him, but each ordered another to draw his sword and none could; at last, because the day was coming on, it was done.

They took him up and carried him to the place called Leukas, on the western side of the city, and buried him there with reverence on the twenty-fourth day of November, in the cemetery he had built himself. But before the burial they brought him into the cathedral to the holy throne, and setting his head on his body they placed him in the episcopal chair. Seeing it, all the people stopped weeping and were comforted — for while he lived he had never been willing to sit in that chair, but in the place below it.

The clergy of that time, not knowing the reason, had been offended and had murmured at it, and when the priests asked him privately why he so slighted the great dignity of the high priesthood and sat below when he was vested, he answered: *Do not force yourselves to sit in the holy throne, for fear and trembling take hold of my soul when I come near it, since I see a divine and shining power sitting in it. For joy and for fear I am amazed, not knowing what to do, and so I sit in the lower place, and even then with measureless fear, lest the people be offended and condemn me.*

So for that reason they set his holy relics in the holy throne, that the people might rejoice, since in his life, humble-minded as he was, he had not sat in it. When that was done the bishops brought Achillas near the throne, laid the saint's omophorion on him and consecrated him patriarch, as the great Peter had commanded; and Peter they buried with great honour and love, with shrouds and precious myrrh as was fitting, in the tomb he had built for himself, where many signs and wonders were done, and are done to this day.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 24 November, beside [Clement of Rome](/saints/clement-of-rome), with whom he shares the day's hymns. saint.gr says he combined the most fervent zeal with a higher and brilliant education, and quickly took an important place in the Church of Egypt, succeeding Theonas as archbishop of Alexandria. In 306 he presided over a synod that condemned and deposed Meletius, bishop of Lycopolis; and Meletius, to take his revenge, denounced Peter to the emperor Maximian Galerius when that emperor proclaimed a persecution of the Christians. Then the pious archbishop showed his great and brilliant conduct: with his arrest by the eparch of Alexandria in prospect, he called the worthiest presbyters of his archbishopric, [Achillas](/saints/achillas) and [Alexander](/saints/alexander-of-alexandria), announced to them that his end had come and appointed them his successors. Afterwards, so that the Christian people gathered before his door should not notice the arrest, he went out and gave himself up to the soldiers through a hole opened in the back of his house, and they took him secretly to the place of execution and beheaded him in November 311, after he had shepherded the Church of Alexandria twelve years. Fragments of a treatise of his on the Godhead survive, the page adds, and canons from his work on repentance, written when he was leading back Christians who had not been able to stand firm in the persecutions; and it was Peter's pious sharpness of sight that had made out the heretical leanings of the then young deacon Arius, whom he excommunicated and afterwards received again on his declaring repentance and asking pardon publicly.

The Охридски пролог makes him the disciple and follower of the holy Theonas, archbishop of Alexandria, and says that he was for a time a teacher in the famous philosophical school of Origen. He came to the archiepiscopal throne in the year 299 and ended as a martyr at the tomb of the apostle Mark in 311, which is the year this folder already held from the Greek calendar; the accession is new to it and is recorded as the floruit of an episcopate, not as a correction of anything.

He governed the Church in a very hard time, the book says, when violence was done to the faithful from outside by unbelievers and from inside by heretics. In his time six hundred and seventy Christians suffered at Alexandria, and whole families were often led out to the place of execution and killed there together. It was then too that Arius was troubling the faithful with his crooked teaching, and this bishop cut him off from the Church and cursed him in this world and in the one to come. In prison the Lord himself appeared to him.

This book keeps him on the Julian 25 November, one menologion number later than the 24 November the Romanian and the Greek calendars give him, and both days stand here as they were read.
