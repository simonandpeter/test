# Andrew the Stratelates

Andrew was an officer in the Roman army in the reign of Maximian (284–305), loved by the troops for his courage, his unbeaten record and his justice; a Syrian by birth, the Prologue says, serving in Syria. When a great Persian army invaded the Syrian provinces the governor Antiochus gave him command of the Roman forces with the title Stratelates, commander-in-chief. He chose a small band of brave soldiers and went out to meet the enemy. His men were pagans, and he himself was not yet baptised, but he believed in Jesus Christ, and before the battle he told them that the pagan gods were demons and could give no help in the fight — that if they all called on the one true God, Christ the Lord, the enemy would scatter before them like dust. They did, and the Persian army was utterly destroyed.

When he came back to Antioch in triumph, envious men denounced him as a Christian and the governor summoned him to trial; Andrew declared his faith openly, was tortured and thrown into prison, and the governor wrote to the emperor in Rome. The emperor, knowing Andrew’s standing with the people and the army, ordered him freed — and watched for another pretext, not the faith, to kill him. Andrew learned the order by revelation, and with his faithful soldiers, two thousand five hundred and ninety-three of them, went to Tarsus in Cilicia, where all were baptised by the bishop Peter; pursued there too, they withdrew far into the Taurus mountains of Armenia, and in a gorge, as they were at prayer, the Roman army overtook them and cut them down to the last man — none would defend himself, all longed for a martyr’s death for Christ. Where their blood flowed in a stream a spring of healing water broke out, and the bishop Peter came secretly with his people and buried them there.
