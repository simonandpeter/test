# Andrew the Stratelates

Andrew was an officer in the Roman army in the reign of Maximian (284–305), loved by the troops for his courage, his unbeaten record and his justice; a Syrian by birth, the Prologue says, serving in Syria. When a great Persian army invaded the Syrian provinces the governor Antiochus gave him command of the Roman forces with the title Stratelates, commander-in-chief. He chose a small band of brave soldiers and went out to meet the enemy. His men were pagans, and he himself was not yet baptised, but he believed in Jesus Christ, and before the battle he told them that the pagan gods were demons and could give no help in the fight — that if they all called on the one true God, Christ the Lord, the enemy would scatter before them like dust. They did, and the Persian army was utterly destroyed.

When he came back to Antioch in triumph, envious men denounced him as a Christian and the governor summoned him to trial; Andrew declared his faith openly, was tortured and thrown into prison, and the governor wrote to the emperor in Rome. The emperor, knowing Andrew’s standing with the people and the army, ordered him freed — and watched for another pretext, not the faith, to kill him.

Andrew learned the order by revelation, and with his faithful soldiers, two thousand five hundred and ninety-three of them, went to Tarsus in Cilicia, where all were baptised by the bishop Peter; pursued there too, they withdrew far into the Taurus mountains of Armenia, and in a gorge, as they were at prayer, the Roman army overtook them and cut them down to the last man — none would defend himself, all longed for a martyr’s death for Christ. Where their blood flowed in a stream a spring of healing water broke out, and the bishop Peter came secretly with his people and buried them there.

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 19 August. Doxologia.ro's life sets him under the pagan Maximian, when a governor of Syria named Antiochus, a fervent servant of the idols, held the emperor's authority over the whole province with orders to torture and kill every Christian, and had many tribunes and Roman troops under him. Among those tribunes was Andrew, the servant of Christ, like a sweet-smelling lily among thorns: for a time he kept his faith secret, and then confessed the name of Christ before everyone. Though still unbaptised he had a strong faith and a burning love for Christ, serving Him day and night in prayer and fasting and keeping from everything displeasing to God, and God gave him great power and victory in war, so that no one in all the companies was his equal in valour. The life ends at the martyrs' burial place, where a spring rose whose water cast an unclean spirit out of a cleric who drank from it, and then healed all the sick who were brought there; and it says that bishop Peter, whom Seleucus was seeking to kill, did not go back to Tarsus but into Isauria.

The Greek calendar keeps Andrew on the same day, 19 August, and tells the story from the soldiers' fear. saint.gr says they were gripped by it because the enemy was numerous and had many victories to his credit, and that the command of the small body of soldiers had been given by the commander-in-chief Antiochus to a brave Christian officer, Andrew. Andrew assured his soldiers that the triumph of victory would be on their side if they all called with real faith upon the almighty God of the Christians, since every achievement of Christians is brought about «σὺν τὴ δυνάμει τοῦ Κυρίου ἠμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χρίστου», with the power of our Lord Jesus Christ.

And so it was: his words raised the soldiers' morale, and they crushed the enemy's great host. Antiochus praised the feat publicly, but when he learned that the soldiers had been drawn by Andrew to Christianity he sent a thousand men to disarm them and send them home — and Andrew, after talking with them, drew those to Christianity as well. Enraged at that, Antiochus sent a special detachment under trusted officers and killed all the Christian soldiers: two thousand five hundred and ninety-three of them. The page names not one of the company, and it records a portion of Andrew's relic at the Lavra of Saint Alexander Nevsky in Saint Petersburg.
