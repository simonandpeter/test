# Quadratus

Quadratus was one of the Seventy Apostles. He preached the word of God in Athens and in Magnesia, where he was also bishop, and the life the Romanian synaxarion keeps for him is built around one image: Sirach's morning star among the clouds. The clouds are the darkness of the pagan cities; Quadratus shone in it, overturned the unclean sacrifices, broke the idols, and emptied the temples by his prayer - and as the morning star brings the sun up behind it, so he brought the great light of the faith into souls that had sat in the shadow of death.

The darkness, the life says, always hates the light. Seeing their idols trodden down, the idolaters raised a persecution against him and would have stoned him as another Stephen; struck with the stones, he was kept alive by the grace of Christ. Then they bound him and set out to kill him by hunger, keeping bread from the man who had been feeding their own souls - and it is they, the life observes, who were starving, with the famine that is not of bread but of hearing the word of the Lord. Unable to kill by hunger a man fed from above, they put him at last to many torments, and he finished the contest of martyrdom in the reign of the emperor Hadrian.

His body was laid in Magnesia, and pours out healings on the infirm.

The Greek calendar makes him a great apologist, after Peter’s counsel to be ready always to give an answer for the hope that is in us, with meekness and fear. A modest, wise and very learned man, skilled in argument, he became bishop of Athens while its schools of philosophy still flourished, enlightened many and silenced the philosophers, who, unable to answer him, drove him out of the city by force. Unbowed, he went on to Magnesia in Asia Minor and preached the Gospel there boldly, and wrote an apology for Christianity to Hadrian, whose answer was to have him killed. Its verse has him stoned by fools for refusing to give honour to stones.

The Russian calendar keeps him on 21 September old style as an apostle of the Seventy, about 130, and its two lives tell the story shortly. He preached at Athens and at Magnesia — which they place on the eastern peninsula of Thessaly, where the Greek page has Asia Minor — and was bishop of Athens; a raging crowd set on him to stone him, he was kept alive by God, and he was thrown into prison and died there of hunger, and his body was buried at Magnesia. In 126 he wrote an apology for Christianity which, the first life says, so moved Hadrian that the emperor ordered that no one be condemned without evidence. The historian Eusebius knew it in the fourth century, and only the passage he quotes survives: the Saviour’s works were always plain because they were true, and those he healed and raised were seen long after he had gone, some of them living into the writer’s own time.

The Ohrid Prologue, for the Serbian calendar, tells the same on the same day: one of the Seventy and a disciple of the great apostles, bishop first at Athens after Publius and then at Magnesia, learned in worldly wisdom and rich in the grace of the Spirit, the morning star among the clouds; stoned, then imprisoned and given no bread until he gave up his soul; and the writer of a defence of Christianity for Hadrian, which moved the emperor to order that Christians not be persecuted without particular charges. It has him suffer about 130 and be buried at Magnesia, where he suffered.
