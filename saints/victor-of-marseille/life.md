# Victor of Marseille

The emperor Maximian, reeking with the blood of the Theban legion and of
many other martyrs he had massacred through Gaul, came to Marseilles, the
most numerous and flourishing church of those provinces, breathing nothing
but slaughter, and his coming filled the Christians with fear. In that
consternation Victor, a Christian officer of the troops, went by night from
house to house visiting the faithful and inspiring them with contempt of a
temporal death and love of eternal life; he was surprised at it and brought
before the prefects Asterius and Eutychius, who urged him not to lose the
fruit of all his services and the favour of his prince for the worship of a
dead man, as they called Jesus Christ. He answered that he renounced those
rewards if he could not enjoy them without being unfaithful to Jesus Christ,
the eternal Son of God, who had been pleased to become man for our salvation
and had raised himself from the dead and reigned with the Father, God
equally with him. The court heard him with shouts of rage; but the prisoner
being a man of rank, the prefects sent him to Maximian himself, whose
incensed face did not daunt him, and the tyrant, finding his threats
useless, had him bound hand and foot and dragged through every street of the
city to the blows and insults of the mob, every heathen thinking it a crime
not to offer the martyr some indignity — which was meant to frighten the
Christians and instead encouraged them. Brought back bruised and bloody to
the prefects, who thought his sufferings had weakened him and pressed him
again to adore their gods, he said, I despise your deities and confess Jesus
Christ; inflict on me what torments you please. The prefects disagreed only
about the torture; Eutychius withdrew and left him to Asterius, who had him
hoisted on the rack and cruelly tortured a long while; the martyr, lifting
his eyes to heaven, asked patience and constancy of God, whose gift he knew
it to be, and Jesus Christ appeared to him on the rack holding a cross, gave
him his peace, and told him that he suffered in his servants and crowned
them after their victory. When the torturers tired he was thrown into a dark
dungeon, and at midnight God visited him by his angels: the prison was
filled with a light brighter than the sun and the martyr sang with the
angels the praises of God, and three soldiers who guarded him, Alexander,
Longinus and Felician, seeing the light, fell at his feet, asked his pardon
and desired baptism; he instructed them as time allowed, sent for priests
that same night, went with them to the sea-side and led them out of the
water as their godfather, and came back with them to his prison. In the
morning Maximian, told of the guards’ conversion, had all four brought into
the market-place; the mob would have had Victor bring his converts back to
the gods — I cannot undo what is well done, he said, and turned to them: you
are still soldiers; behave with courage, God will give you victory; you
belong to Jesus Christ; be faithful; an immortal crown is prepared for you.
The three persevered and were beheaded at once; Victor prayed with tears to
be joined with them in their happy death, but after being exposed to the
insults of the whole city like an immovable rock lashed by the waves, beaten
with clubs and scourged with leather thongs, he was carried back to prison
for three days, commending his martyrdom to God with many tears. Then the
emperor had a statue of Jupiter with an altar and incense set beside him and
commanded him to offer; Victor went up to the profane altar and kicked it
down, and the emperor had the foot chopped off on the spot, which the saint
suffered with great joy, offering God these first fruits of his body; a few
moments later he was condemned to be crushed to death under the stone of a
hand-mill, and when part of his body had been crushed the mill broke; he
still breathed a little, and his head was cut off. The four bodies were
thrown into the sea, cast ashore, and buried by the Christians in a grotto
hewn in the rock, where, the author of the acts says, they are honoured with
many miracles. In the fifth century Cassian built a great monastery by his
tomb, whose church, the most ancient in all France, keeps his relics; part
of them went to Paris, to the chapel that under Louis VI became the royal
abbey of St Victor — the cradle, Rollin called it, of the university of
Paris. Butler rests on the saint’s genuine acts, which some ascribe to
Cassian, published by Bosquet, and on Tillemont, Ceillier, Fleury and the
Bollandists, and keeps him on 21 July. The Prologue, which the Serbian
calendar keeps on 23 August of the old style and which is the line this
entry stands under, says only that Victor suffered at Marseille in the third
century, converted his guards in prison, and died on a cross — a different
end from the acts’, and both are left as their sources give them.
