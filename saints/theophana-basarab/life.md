# Theophana Basarab

Theophana was born at the beginning of the fourteenth century, the daughter of Basarab I the Founder, the first prince of Wallachia, and of his wife Marghita. She was given the name Theodora at baptism and brought up, doxologia's life says, in an educated household and in love of God and of her neighbour. In 1322 she married Ivan Alexander, then despot of Loveč and afterwards tsar of Bulgaria, and they had three sons and a daughter: Michael Asen, Ivan Sratsimir, Ivan Asen and Vasilisa.

She raised her children herself, the life says, putting into them the love of Christ, prayer, humility and wisdom against the offices they would later hold. In 1331 she followed her husband to Tarnovo, the capital of the tsardom, where the relics of Saints Philothea and Paraskeva then lay, and where she often went to pray. She was hard tried: two of her sons died in war, and her grief at the burial of Prince Ivan Asen is drawn in a miniature of the Manasses Chronicle, which shows her as a mother weeping for her son.

She read the Fathers constantly, and out of that reading, the life says, she drew her strength. She founded at Loveč, then at Tarnovo and later at Vidin centres where copyists translated and wrote liturgical manuscripts and works of the spiritual life in Church Slavonic of the Middle Bulgarian recension. With her husband the tsar she built monasteries and churches and restored others, in a time when hesychasm — the prayer of Jesus held in the heart, with attention — was being taken up by monks and by lay people alike.

In her later years she went to Vidin, where her son Ivan Sratsimir had been given the rule of the tsardom, and there she was a good counsellor to his wife Anna, who was also a Romanian of the house of Basarab. The two raised the spiritual life of the city and set copying going at Vidin as well; one of those books, the Vidin Miscellany, survives, and it contains lives of holy women.

By tradition Theophana spent the last years of her life at the monastery of Albotina, cut into the rock not far from Vidin. She died at a great age — a popular tradition says she was killed by the Ottomans who attacked Vidin in 1396 — and was buried with great honour and wept for by the whole people. The inhabitants of the Bulgarian and Serbian Timok keep her memory to this day, the life says, and keep her feast on 28 October each year.
