# Eutropius of Cappadocia

When Theodore Tiron had finished his passion and died a martyr in the city of Amasea, his companions — soldiers and close friends — were left in prison for Christ: Eutropius, [Cleonicus](/saints/cleonicus-of-cappadocia), and [Basiliscus](/saints/basiliscus-of-amasea), Theodore's nephew. Eutropius was brother by the same mother to Cleonicus and of Cappadocian stock; and for love's sake all three called one another brothers. Publius, the governor who had tortured Theodore, had perished, and in his place came Asclepiodotus, a Phrygian, cruel in habit and godless in mind.

He had authority from the emperor Maximian to force Christians to sacrifice and to destroy with torments those who would not. Sitting in judgement, he had the record of Theodore's trial read out, asked where his companions were, and — seeing the temple of the goddess they called Hera, mother of the gods, which Theodore had burned — roared like a lion and sent soldiers to fetch the three from the prison. The saints rose and went out with joy, comforting the prisoners they left behind.

Eutropius, who was very fair to look at, answered the governor that his pleasing name was Christian, refused to draw the others into obedience, and told him to stop his slander and not hope to frighten him with threats. He was struck across the mouth, and all three were stretched out by four soldiers each and beaten with rods; but a great earthquake shook the whole court, and the soldiers who were torturing them begged the governor to be relieved of the work. Later gold and costly clothing were set before Eutropius, and he called the governor a cunning deceiver and an evil serpent and would not touch them.

In the prison the three prayed together, and there was thunder and an earthquake so great that the foundation of the pagan temple was shaken. They were tortured again, with iron claws and with mustard mixed with salt upon the wounds, and at midnight the Lord appeared to them. In the morning Basiliscus asked for a sentence of death for himself as well and was refused; Eutropius and Cleonicus were led out of the city to the crosses made ready for them, and, having prayed for the Church and for peace for the Christian people, were crucified. A voice came from heaven calling them to eternal rest, and they gave up their souls on the third day of March. Velonic, a citizen of Amasea, anointed the body of Eutropius with myrrh and carried it with honour to a village of his own about eighteen stadia from the city.

The Greek calendar keeps the three of them together on the same day, on one page, and tells the martyrdom at length. saint.gr puts them at Amasea of Pontus — not Cappadocia, which is the epithet this folder carries from doxologia — makes them soldiers and kinsmen of [Theodore the Recruit](/saints/theodore-the-recruit), and sets them in the years of the emperor Maximian, under the governor Asklepiodotos, who arrested them and tortured them hard; but the Lord and the great-martyr Theodore appeared to them and they were made whole. In prison the three young men lost neither their courage nor their faith, and by their preaching and their bearing they brought many of their fellow prisoners to the true faith, so that Asklepiodotos had them brought before him again. He began to flatter Cleonicus with promises of gifts and honours; the saint laughed, mocked the sickness of the idols, and while a sacrifice was being performed brought down the idol of Artemis by prayer.

They were scourged for it, and the Lord appeared again, with the great-martyr Theodore at his side, in the sight of the crowd of idolaters watching; the three were healed of their wounds on the spot and many who saw it were baptised, whom Asklepiodotos ordered beheaded. Blind with rage, he had boiling pitch poured over the three, and it fell instead on the executioners and burnt them, while the martyrs were untouched. Then he sentenced Eutropius and Cleonicus to the cross and Basiliscus to prison; the two spent their last night in prayer, and the Lord appeared to them once more to give them heart. On the third of March, the page says in the year 308, Eutropius and Cleonicus were crowned by their death on the cross, while Basiliscus stayed shut in prison and died there some years later — and for him saint.gr sends the reader to the twenty-second of May, where it keeps his memory again.

The Serbian church keeps him on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović is where the attempt to buy him is told. The three were companions of [Theodore the Recruit](/saints/theodore-the-recruit), and when Theodore had made his glorious end they were left in prison behind him, and went long unsentenced because the emperor's governor in Amasea was replaced. When the new one came, more inhuman than the man before him, he had the three brought out. All three were young men: Eutropius and [Cleonicus](/saints/cleonicus-of-cappadocia) brothers born of one mother, and [Basiliscus](/saints/basiliscus-of-amasea) a kinsman of Theodore — but all three, for brotherly love, were as brothers born.

Standing before the governor they said that as the Holy Trinity is indivisible, so were they indivisible in faith and inseparable in love. All flattery was wasted on them, and so were the attempts to buy Eutropius. He was asked first to dine with the governor, and refused out of the Psalm, that blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the ungodly; then he was offered a hundred and fifty litrai of silver, and refused that too, reminding the governor that Judas lost his own soul for silver.

After every attempt and every torment the two brothers were sentenced to the cross and Basiliscus to the sword. The two were crucified on two crosses, and gave thanks to Christ that He had counted them worthy of the death He Himself had died; and all three went into the kingdom of joy, where their commander Theodore, glorified by Christ before them, was waiting. The Prologue puts their suffering in the year 308, which is outside the interval recorded here; the year is kept as a disagreement and nothing was written over.

The Сретенский monastery’s calendar keeps the three together on the Julian 3 March — «Мчч. Евтропия , Клеоника и Василиска (ок. 308).» — and its register line for the day calls them «Мучеников Евтропия и дружины его, Клеоника и Василиска», Eutropius and his company. Its life is much the fullest of the four read for them. It puts the suffering at Amasea in Pontus in Asia Minor about 308, makes Eutropius and [Cleonicus](/saints/cleonicus-of-cappadocia) brothers, and makes [Basiliscus](/saints/basiliscus-of-amasea) the nephew of the great martyr Theodore the soldier, whose memory it puts on 17 February; the three were friends.

After Theodore’s martyrdom they were in prison, and by their preaching brought many of the pagans shut up with them to the faith. Publius, who had tortured Theodore, perished ingloriously, struck down by the wrath of God, and Asclepiodotus was made governor in his place, no gentler than his predecessor. Learning that the friends of the martyr were still in the prison, he had them brought before him, and before the new governor they confessed Christ as firmly as before. They were beaten without mercy until their bodies were one wound.

Under the torture Eutropius prayed aloud: «Даждь, Господи, нам терпения совершенного в ранах сих ради венца мученического» — give us, Lord, perfect patience in these wounds for the martyr’s crown, and come to our help as you came to your servant Theodore. The Lord himself appeared to them with angels, and Theodore with them, saying that the Saviour had come to their help so that they might be assured of eternal life. The soldiers and many of the bystanders were counted worthy to see it too, and began begging the governor to stop the torture; seeing the crowd stirred and ready to believe, he had the martyrs taken away.

Then he invited Eutropius to dinner and proposed that he should sacrifice to the gods in public and stay a Christian in his soul, and Eutropius refused him. Next day they were brought into the pagan temple to be forced to sacrifice, and Eutropius prayed that the whole frenzy of paganism might be destroyed and that on that spot Christians might offer the bloodless Sacrifice to the true God. The last word of the prayer had barely sounded when an earthquake began; the walls of the temple fell and the image of Artemis broke with them, and above the noise a voice came: your prayer is heard, and on this place there shall be a house of prayer for Christians.

When it was over the governor, hardly recovered from his fright, had tall wooden stakes sunk in the ground, the martyrs tied to them and boiling pitch poured over them; and the pitch ran off their bodies as water off marble and scalded the torturers instead. The onlookers were appalled, and the governor in his obstinacy ordered their bodies torn with iron hooks and their wounds galled with mustard mixed with salt and vinegar, and they bore that too with a wonderful steadiness. The last night before the execution they spent in prayer, and the Lord appeared to them again and strengthened them.

On the morning of 3 March Eutropius and [Cleonicus](/saints/cleonicus-of-cappadocia) were crucified, and [Basiliscus](/saints/basiliscus-of-amasea) was left in prison; on 22 May he was beheaded in the town of Comana and his body thrown into the river, and the Christians found his remains and buried them in a ploughed field, and a church was raised in his name at Comana afterwards. That second day is this calendar’s own and this corpus holds the one the day page gave. The year is where the hosts part and both readings are now carried: doxologia’s reign of 286 to 305 and this calendar’s «ок. 308». The day prints no hymn under any of the three names, and the register line is all it adds besides.
