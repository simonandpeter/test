# Andronicus of Antioch

In the days of Theodosius the Great there was a man at Antioch named Andronicus, a goldsmith, who married [Athanasia](/saints/athanasia-of-antioch), the daughter of another goldsmith named John. They lived honourably, divided their wealth in three — one part to the poor, one to the adorning of the church, one for the needs of the house — and were loved in the city for their gentleness. They had a son, John, and a daughter, Mary; after the two children they lived as brother and sister, visiting and washing and feeding the sick, and kept Wednesday and Friday in fasting and prayer.

In the twelfth year of their life together Athanasia came home from the morning singing and found both children groaning, and they died. She would not go back into her house but sat weeping at the grave, and at midnight the martyr Julian appeared to her in the form of a monk and reproved her sorrow. She told her husband what she had seen, and was comforted. Andronicus put his house and his goods into his father-in-law's hands, freed all his servants, and one night the two of them took a little for the road and left Antioch unknown to anybody.

They went to Jerusalem and to the holy places, then to Alexandria to venerate the martyr Menas, and there they came to the elder Daniel of Sketis, who placed Athanasia in a women's monastery and kept Andronicus with him. Andronicus laboured twelve years in the monastic life. Then he asked leave to go up to Jerusalem again, and on the road he met a monk who called himself Athanasius; he did not know his own wife, and the two went on together, venerated the holy places, returned to Alexandria and to the martyr Menas, and asked to live together in silence.

They lived so another twelve years, until Athanasius fell ill and died. When the brothers came to bury the body they found that it was a woman's, and Abba Daniel found the letter she had left under her pillow, read it and gave it to Andronicus, who only then knew that Father Athanasius had been his wife. He died soon afterwards, and the two nearby sketes, the eighteenth and the one whose monks wore white, each claimed his body; in the end he was buried beside Athanasia.

The Greek calendar keeps the two together on the same day, 9 October, and sets them in the year 540. saint.gr says they were virtuous spouses of Antioch in Syria, well off and living according to the will of God, and that Andronicus worked as a money-changer in the city. Two children were born to them of that marriage, and both died; and the grief of it drove the two parents to seek consolation in a pilgrimage to the Holy Places. From there they went on to Egypt, where by a common decision they became monastics and entered monasteries — Andronicus the monastery of the abba Daniel, and his wife Athanasia the women's monastery of the Tabennesiotes — and there, having passed the rest of their lives in asceticism, they fell asleep in peace. The same page records a portion of the relics of each of them at the monastery of Kykkos in Cyprus.

The Russian calendar keeps the two of them on one line on its own 9 October, «Прпп. Андроника и супруги его Афанасии (V)», and the plain service icon stands immediately before it, so the rank on this row is the pair's. The Russian puts them in the fifth century where the Greek page gives the year 540; this folder records that and does not settle it. Its life says they lived at Antioch and that Andronicus was a craftsman who divided what he earned into three — one part to the church, one to the poor, and one kept for his family.

When the Lord took their son and their daughter from them, the two of them resolved to give themselves wholly to God's service, helping the poor and the sick. Soon they went to Alexandria, and he entered a skete while [Athanasia](/saints/athanasia-of-antioch) entered the women's monastery of Tabennisi. After twelve years of the ascetic life he set out for Jerusalem to venerate the holy places, and fell in with a fellow-traveller, a monk called Athanasius. It was Athanasia, who had foreseen the dangers of the road and dressed as a man. Neither knew the other, because the long years of their labours had changed how they looked.

Coming back from Jerusalem the two monks settled in one cell and laboured there many years in silence. After Athanasia died a note was found that she had left, which told who she was. Andronicus died soon after her.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two of them on the Julian 9 October under one heading, which names [Athanasia](/saints/athanasia-of-antioch) as his wife, and it tells a shorter story than the other books. Citizens of Antioch in the days of [Theodosius the Great](/saints/theodosius-the-great); Andronicus a goldsmith by trade; both of them very devout and labouring without ceasing to walk in the way of the Lord; and a third of what he earned given to the poor, a third to the church and a third kept to live on. Two children were born to them, after which they agreed to live as brother and sister — and then, by God’s unsearchable providence, both children died in one day.

They were in great grief until the holy martyr Julian appeared to Athanasia at the graveyard and comforted her with the news that their children were in the kingdom of God and were better off there than with their parents on earth; this book is the first of the four to say what the consolation was. Then both left everything and went to Egypt and took the monastic habit, he with the elder Daniel in Scetis and she in a women’s monastery at Tabennisiot. The entry knows nothing of the second pilgrimage, of the monk who called himself Athanasius or of the letter under the pillow: it says only that they pleased God by many years of ascetic labour and passed into the eternal kingdom of Christ, Athanasia first and Andronicus eight days after her.
