# Hilarion the New of Pelecete

Hilarion became a monk in his youth, carrying his cross and following Christ crucified, and subduing the passions of the body by abstinence. He surpassed all the monks in good works, and, shutting himself in a dark cell, passed many years there undisturbed and was enlightened with dispassion. For this he was counted worthy of the priestly order as well, and became abbot of the monastery called Pelecete, in Asia near the Hellespont.

He worked chosen miracles: the creatures that damaged the crops he rebuked and drove with a word out of the fields and gardens; he stilled the hail by prayer and watered the thirsty ground with rain; he divided the current of the river, as the prophet Elisha did; he healed a man's withered hand, gave a blind man his sight and granted healing to the lame; he cast out devils; and he filled with many fish the nets of fishermen who had laboured in vain.

Venerable Joseph the hymnographer writes of him, in the eighth ode of the canon, that he endured persecutions from the tormentors for honouring the icon of the Saviour, and calls him a martyr. One account has him living in the reign of Leo the Armenian, 813 to 820, who trampled the holy icons; others hold that he lived in the reign of Leo the Isaurian, 717 to 741, and of his son Copronymus, 741 to 775, which was many years before Leo the Armenian, and that he suffered for the holy icons at the time when Copronymus's commander — the life names him «la Hanodracon» — fell without warning with an army upon the monastery called Pelecete, on Great and Holy Thursday of the saving Passion, while the divine Liturgy was being served.

Entering the church and the sanctuary boldly, he ordered the chanting to stop and threw down the holy and life-giving Mysteries of Christ. Then he took the chief monks, forty-two of them, and bound them in irons; the rest he tortured with terrible beatings until their bodies were torn; others he smeared on the beard and face with pitch and set alight, and others he had their noses cut off. Then he burned the monastery and the church, and sent the forty-two fathers he had bound into exile in the country on the edge of Ephesus, where he shut them in an old bath-house and tormented them to death.

In that time Hilarion too, as the elder among those fathers, holding the office of abbot, is known to have suffered much and to have given up his soul into the hands of God for the holy icons.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and settles rather less than it seems to. saint.gr says the venerable Hilarion was abbot of the monastery of Pelekete at Triglia and was marked out for his ascetic character, his love of God, his gift of almsgiving and his spiritual struggles, and that for this God endowed him with the gift of foresight; it then gives a single year for his repose, 754, and heads him «(; - 754)». That year falls inside the eighth-to-ninth-century interval this folder already records, and it does not resolve the disagreement the Romanian life sets out between the reign of Leo the Armenian and that of Leo the Isaurian and his son, so it is recorded as the Greek reading and the interval is left alone. The Greek page also prints two apolytikia for him where doxologia prints one troparion: a proper in the plagal first tone, on the cheerfulness of his ways and the glittering of the virtues, and the common of a venerable father in the plagal fourth, which carries his own name in its last line. Its second couplet fixes the day and nothing else: «Ὀγδόῃ Ἱλαρίωνα κιχήσατο εἰκάδι πότμος» — on the twenty-eighth, fate overtook Hilarion.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the same menologion day and adds the company of his exile. He was abbot of the monastery of Pelecete near the Hellespont. He shone like the sun with the Spirit of God, healed sicknesses in men and drove out evil spirits. In the time of Leo the Armenian the iconoclast persecution arose, and this man of God suffered in it: with forty of his monks he was sent into exile near Ephesus, and there he came to his end in prison and passed over into the Kingdom of Christ.

The entry puts that death in 754 — two generations before the emperor it has just named, so the page contradicts itself as plainly as the Romanian life this folder was built from sets out the same disagreement without settling it. The year falls inside the interval this folder already holds, which is wide for exactly that reason, so nothing here was narrowed and nothing was changed.
