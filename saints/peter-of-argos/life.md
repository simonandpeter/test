# Peter of Argos

Peter came of Constantinople and was born about the middle of the ninth century. His parents were marked out not only by their earthly wealth but by their piety and their charity, saint.gr says; their names are not known, and all that is known is that in an atmosphere of virtue and sobriety, of love and almsgiving, of reverence and piety, they brought up their five children in the instruction and admonition of the Lord: Paul, Dionysius, Peter, Plato, and their daughter. Parents and children together chose the ascetic and monastic life with a godly zeal and withdrew to a monastery.

There Peter, from a young age and undistracted by worldly or other cares, gave himself with all his strength to asceticism and the struggle for virtue, and was shown to be the severe ascetic of the desert, adorned with humility, sobriety, love of truth, sympathy, charity, patience, self-restraint and prayer. He studied the Scriptures and the Fathers, and the wisdom outside as well, the old Greek letters, having Photius the Great, patriarch of Constantinople, for his model.

When the patriarch Nicholas the Mystic learned his virtues, a man endowed with wisdom, capability, foresight and decision, he wanted ardently to raise him to the episcopate, and called him to fill the vacant metropolis of Corinth. Peter hesitated to exchange his beloved quiet life for the turmoil of the world, and with modesty declined the honour, thanking him and refusing. The patriarch then turned to his brother Paul and made him metropolitan of Corinth, but did not stop trying to persuade Peter to accept the priesthood; and rather than grieve the patriarch, Peter decided to go down to Corinth, whose throne his brother adorned, and settle quietly near him.

When the bishopric of Argos, which depended on the metropolis of Corinth, fell vacant, the people of Argos and of Nauplion applied to bishop Paul and asked insistently for Peter as the bishop of their region; and he gave way and accepted. As bishop he became a pattern to the faithful in everything. He taught unceasingly by word and by deed, supported those who wavered, fed the hungry, healed the suffering, and bought back captives of the Saracens; and God rewarded his virtues and answered his prayers for his flock with wonders. Like another Elias he fed the people of the region with a little flour in a time of famine; he ransomed a young captive of the Saracens by his persistent prayer; he healed a woman possessed. He was learned and wise, and an instructive and eloquent teacher, to which his seven surviving homilies bear witness.

For three days his sweet voice admonished and blessed his children from his bed, and with prayer and the sign of the Cross he fell asleep in peace about the year 925. His face seemed as though it lived and slept, lit with light. Priests and a great crowd of people buried the holy body with feeling and reverence on the left side of the church of the Dormition of the Mother of God, and his relics became the occasion of many wonders. But on 21 January 1421 the Latin bishop of Sigountonane seized the body to carry it to Rome. Part of his relics is now in the metropolitan church of Argos that bears his name, and he is the city's patron.
