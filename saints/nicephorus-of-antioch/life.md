# Nicephorus of Antioch

In Antioch the Great of Syria there was a priest named Sapricius and a townsman named Nicephorus, and so great was the friendship and love between them that others took them for brothers of one mother. When they had lived honourably a long while in that friendship, the enemy who hates what is good envied them and sowed such enmity between them that afterwards they would not so much as meet; and they hated one another with an unjust hatred, which grew by the devil's working as great as their love had been before.

After many days in that quarrel Nicephorus came to himself and knew that the evil was from the devil. He asked friends and neighbours to go to Sapricius the priest and beg him to forgive a man who was repenting and to receive him back into his first love, for the Lord's sake; the priest would not. Nicephorus sent others; Sapricius would not even listen to them. The third time he drove the messengers away and would not be moved to pity. So he hardened his heart, the life says, forgetting the Lord's words: *Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.*

Then Nicephorus went himself, fell at his feet and said: «Forgive me, father, forgive me for the Lord's sake.» But Sapricius would not so much as look at him, having no mercy, no love and no fear of God, though as a Christian and a priest he was bound to have forgiven his brother even before he was asked. Nicephorus went away from him ashamed, and did not obtain pardon.

At that time a great persecution of the Christians arose suddenly in Antioch, in the reign of Valerian and Gallienus. Sapricius the priest was seized and brought for questioning before the governor, who asked his name, whether he was a cleric, and of what people he was; he answered that he was called Sapricius, that he was a priest, and that he was a Christian, and that the Christians have Christ God for their emperor. He was sentenced, and by Nicephorus's own words to him afterwards he had endured many torments and had woven himself a heavenly crown by them.

But at the last, for the sake of temporal life and glory, he renounced the eternal life and went away from the Lord. Nicephorus, hearing those wretched words, begged him with tears: «Do not do this, O beloved brother, do not deny our Lord Jesus Christ, do not lose the heavenly crown which thou hast woven for thyself by suffering many torments. The Master Christ stands at the door, and will show himself to thee at once.» Sapricius would not listen at all, but hastened to his own destruction.

Seeing that Sapricius had fallen wholly from the holy faith and denied Christ, Nicephorus began to cry aloud to the executioners: «I am a Christian and I believe in our Lord Jesus Christ, whom Sapricius has denied. Cut me down in his place.» They did not dare kill him without the governor's leave, and all marvelled that he offered himself to death of his own will, crying that he was a Christian and would not sacrifice. When the governor heard it he ordered Sapricius released and this man put to the sword; and so the head of Nicephorus was cut off for Christ in the place of Sapricius, on the ninth day of February, and he went rejoicing to Christ the Lord.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and heads him «(; - 257)». saint.gr tells his story as one of forgiveness refused: he was closely bound to a priest named Sapricius, who came to hate him bitterly on a slanderer's word, and Nicephorus asked him again and again for the reason and for his pardon, in vain. When the great persecution of 257 took Sapricius among many bishops and priests, Nicephorus ran and fell at his knees and begged him in tears, kissed his wounds after the scourging, and asked for his blessing even at the last moment; and Sapricius, unyielding, pushed him away. Left without the strength of divine grace, the page says, Sapricius broke at the block and asked to sacrifice to the idols, and when Nicephorus ran up to beg him to take back his denial the executioners, exasperated, beheaded Nicephorus instead — so that he was granted the martyr's crown and Sapricius the mark of dishonour.

The Serbian church keeps him on the same day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović says at the outset what it reads his life as showing: that God rejects pride and crowns humility and the love of a brother. At Antioch, it says, there lived two close friends, a learned priest named Sapricius and Nicephorus, an ordinary plain townsman, and their friendship somehow turned into a dreadful mutual hatred. Nicephorus tried more than once to be reconciled with the priest, who would have none of it.

When the persecution came, Sapricius was condemned and led out to execution, and Nicephorus followed him along the road begging that at least before his death he would forgive him and they would part in peace — forgive me, martyr of Christ, if I have sinned against you in anything. Sapricius would not so much as look at him, and walked on calmly and proudly to his death; and God, the Prologue says, seeing the hardness of the priest's heart, would not take his sacrifice or crown him, and secretly took the grace from him. At the last moment he denied Christ before the executioners and declared he would bow to the idols, so blinded was he by hatred. Nicephorus implored him not to do it, not to lose the heavenly crown; it was all for nothing. Then Nicephorus called out to the executioners that he was a Christian himself, and that they should cut him down in Sapricius's place. They told the judge, the judge ordered Sapricius released and Nicephorus beheaded, and he laid his head on the block with joy. The Prologue dates it 260, in the reign of the emperor Gallienus.
