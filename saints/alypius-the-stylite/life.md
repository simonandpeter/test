# Alypius the Stylite

Alypius, the servant of Christ, had his homeland in the city of Adrianople, which is in the parts of Paphlagonia. The Lord chose him out of his mother's womb: when she was near her time she saw in the night a vision in which she held in her hands a very beautiful lamb with two lit torches on its horns, burning brightly; and when the child was to be born a great divine light filled the house — a sign of the shining of his good works, for he was to be a light of the world.

After the birth, falling asleep again, she saw all the people of that city gathering in her house and going round the newborn child with psalms and hymns; and she kept these visions in her heart, saying: *What will this child be?* Not long after, her husband died, and she remained a widow in fasting and prayer, unwilling to take a second husband, living for Christ in purity and giving herself and her son into his keeping, for he is the father of orphans and widows.

When she had weaned the boy she brought him to the church and gave him to Saint Theodore, bishop of that city, who knew in the spirit the grace of God in the child, loved him greatly and taught him the divine Scripture. As he grew and advanced in wisdom beyond others he became beloved of God and of men for his good character and his great good works, being full of the fear of God, of humility and of gentleness, showing in his youth the wisdom of grey hairs. So he was made steward of the church, and then ordained deacon, and served God in faith and reverence.

After a time he longed to live apart in solitude, to serve God the more earnestly in stillness. He told that longing to his blessed mother, who like the prophetess Anna did not depart from the church, serving day and night in fasting and prayer; she had divided all her goods among the poor and become a bride of Christ, a deaconess. He said to her that he wished to go into the parts of the East, where he heard that many saints lived in the wilderness in stillness, and asked her prayers that the Lord would direct his way.

His bishop Theodore had need to go to the emperor, and Alypius, as a cleric of the church, had to travel with him; so he went, doing his hierarch's will, and brought him as far as Chalcedon. As the bishop was boarding the ship for Constantinople, Alypius went into a church there beside the sea, prayed, sat down and slept a little — and a most beautiful maiden appeared to him, shining like the sun, and said: *Rise quickly, Alypius.* Wondering at her beauty he asked who she was, and she answered: *I am [Euphemia](/saints/euphemia-the-great-martyr), the martyr, the servant of Christ. Rise; and if you will go to your homeland, I will go with you, and, God willing, will be your fellow traveller and helper.*

He woke and saw nobody, but found his heart full of spiritual joy and knew that God willed him to return to stillness. So he left the bishop and went back to his homeland, with the help and the prayers of the great martyr Euphemia travelling unseen beside him. Coming to Adrianople, to his place of quiet, he resolved to build a church in Euphemia's name; and having no money for it — neither gold nor silver nor copper, but being poor in body and in spirit, following the Lord who became poor for us — he asked some citizens he knew, and they gave willingly all that was needed.

He hallowed a place in the middle of the pagan graves and dug a foundation for the church of the martyr Euphemia. As it was dug, two men appeared to him again in priestly vestments, as they had once before; one held a censer and censed the foundation, marking out what the church would be, while the other sang *Hosanna* about the place. Who they were is not known; but after a time there were found there the incorrupt and fragrant relics of men in the very likeness of those Alypius had twice seen, and at his order they were laid in that same church.

Before the church was consecrated the ranks of the demons, seeing that where their haunt had been there would be a dwelling of saints, came against his cell and the new church with a great shouting and noise, meaning to overturn it from the foundation and to frighten the holy man away. They cried with many voices and raged like angry warriors or like beasts stirred up; but the soldier of Christ stood at once to prayer, and armed with that as with an unconquerable weapon he overcame their power, and they fled in shame like dust scattered by the wind.

When the church was consecrated, people began to come from the city to hear his profitable teaching. Then, arming himself more strongly against the unseen enemies, he went up on a pillar, likening himself to Simeon the first stylite, and stood on it as a watchman, keeping his eye from afar on the ranks of the demons and fighting them manfully day and night. One night the wicked spirits began to throw stones at him and wounded him badly; and bearing the blows he told them that the stones they threw would witness their shameless malice before his Christ at his second coming, that he reckoned their stoning a childish game, and that he would throw down the little boards which were the covering over his head, so that they could reach him more easily.

Hearing that, and knowing the unconquered sufferer was ready to endure all things for God, they fled the place altogether; and some people passing by that night met the demons fleeing and wailing: *Alypius has driven us out of our dwelling; where shall we go, having no place anywhere?* So Alypius threw the covering down from the pillar and stood there with heaven for his roof, bearing bravely every change of weather and air — winter, heat, rains, hail, snow, frost and ice.

Thus he made himself a martyr of his own will, not for one year but for fifty-three years he suffered on that pillar, standing as though nailed to the Cross. Crowds gathered to him, men and women, old and young, to hear profitable words from him and to be healed of sicknesses of soul and body, and some wished to live beside him. So he ordered two monasteries built by his pillar, one for men and one for women, on either side of it; and he in the middle, on the pillar, standing like a lamp on a stand, lit both monasteries with his teaching and with the pattern of his angelic life.

He defended them with his prayers and gave them the laws and rules of monastic life, so that they might keep themselves carefully from the devices of the demons, and he charged the women above all never to be seen by men's eyes. In the convent of virgins lived the blessed Alypius's mother, together with her daughter Maria, his sister, and other well-born women of that city, among them two, Euphemia and Eubula, who left their goods, their children, their kindred, their friends and all this empty world and its pleasures, put on the angelic habit and became like the angels in their lives.

His mother, hearing his teaching, was put to shame and begged him earnestly to clothe her in the monastic habit; and once clothed she struggled twice as hard, adding labours to labours, and after many years in the monastic life she pleased God well and passed to him. And her son became so pleasing to God that even in his lifetime he was lit with heavenly light: often a most shining pillar as of fire appeared above his head and lit the whole place, sometimes by day and sometimes by night when there was thunder and lightning. Many of the faithful who lived in holiness were counted worthy to see it, and to some who saw the wonder from afar it seemed that the saint's pillar was burning with immaterial fire.

He worked many miracles by the grace of Christ: he healed the sick, drove demons out of men and foretold what was to come. Fourteen years before his end his legs were struck with grievous sores, so that he could not stand on them; and so he lay on one side until his death, and when his disciples wanted at times to turn him onto the other side he would not let them, but endured like another Job, giving thanks to God, to whom he departed with joy. After his passing his holy relics gave many healings.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 26 November, and gives him a country and a childhood. saint.gr makes him a man of Adrianople in Paphlagonia who lived in the sixth century, and reports the tradition that when Alypius was about to be born his mother saw in a dream that she was holding a white lamb with three lighted candles on its horns, which meant the virtues the child would have. His parents gave him a Christian upbringing, which in him bore fruit a hundredfold; he had a great fortune, and he spent it on the poor and the suffering of his district, because his pleasure was to fulfil the law of God that urges Christians to be compassionate, loving as brothers, tender-hearted and courteous. Once he was left entirely destitute he withdrew to the desert and lived the ascetic life; and reports say, the page adds, that he stayed on top of a pillar for fifty years — or by other accounts fifty-three, or sixty-six — for the sake of his discipline and under every kind of weather. The fame of his virtue brought other souls to him looking for a peaceful refuge, and to those people he was a tender spiritual father, guiding them with his counsel and holding them up by his example. He died in peace in 608, having lived a hundred years, or by other accounts a hundred and twenty.
