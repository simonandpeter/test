# Spyridon of Trimythous

Spyridon was born towards the end of the third century on Cyprus. He was a shepherd with a wife and children, and spent what he had on his neighbours and on the homeless; for this, his life says, the Lord gave him the gift of wonderworking, so that he healed the incurably sick and cast out demons.

After his wife died he was made bishop of Trimythous in Cyprus, under Constantine the Great, and did not alter his manner of living for the office — he joined the pastoral work to the same deeds of charity as before.

Church historians record that he sat at the First Ecumenical Council in 325, where he disputed with a Greek philosopher defending the Arian teaching. He answered not with argument but with the plain confession of one God who made man from dust and ordered all things visible and invisible by His Word and Spirit, and of that Word as the Son, born of a Virgin, dead and risen for our salvation, one in essence with the Father. The philosopher told his companions afterwards that he could refute proofs with proofs, but that the old man's words carried a power no one could answer, and he was baptised.

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 12 December of its own reckoning, where doxologia.ro's line reads «Sfântul Ierarh Spiridon, Episcopul Trimitundei». Its troparion for him gathers the things he is remembered for into four clauses: that he was shown a defender of the first council and a worker of wonders, that he spoke with the dead woman in her grave, that he turned the serpent into gold, and that when he sang the holy prayers he had angels serving with him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 12 December, and begins by correcting where he was born. saint.gr has Spyridon born in 270 in the now-occupied village of Assia, or Askia, in Cyprus — and not at Trimythous, today's Tremetousia, as many write — into a family of shepherds who were somewhat well off; and although he was educated enough he did not change his trade, but went on being a shepherd himself. In character, it says, he was simple, good and full of love for his neighbour. On Sundays and feasts he would often take the shepherds and lead them to the churches, and afterwards explain to them the reading from the Gospel or from the Apostle; and God blessed him to be often a protector of widows and orphans.

He married a devout wife and had a daughter, Irene; but his wife soon died, and to heal that wound Spyridon gave himself still more to the teaching of the divine word. After much pressure he was ordained priest, and he was so true a priest of the Gospel — blameless, sober, temperate, decent, hospitable, apt to teach, as Paul asks — that when the bishopric of Trimythous in Cyprus fell vacant, people and clergy chose him bishop by acclamation. From that place, the page says, he went so far forward in virtue that God counted him worthy to work many wonders.

At the First Ecumenical Council at Nicaea in Bithynia, in which he took part, the authority of his holy and upright life routed the Arians and he was shown to be one of the brilliant defenders of the Orthodox faith. Indeed, as the tradition the page reports has it, having spoken for a little he then made the sign of the cross, and with his left hand, in which he held a tile, said for a figure of the Holy Trinity: in the name of the Father — and made fire appear above the tile, the fire by which it had been baked; and when he said, and of the Son, water ran down below, the water with which the clay of the tile had been kneaded; and when he added, and of the Holy Spirit, he showed in his palm only the earth that was left.

Spyridon fell asleep, the page says, on 12 December of the year 350.

In 648 Cyprus was facing great raids from the Saracens and the relic was carried to Constantinople by the emperor Justinian and laid in the church of the Holy Apostles beside the relic of the Augusta Theodora; it stayed in the queen of cities until the priest Gregory Polyeuktos, a few days before the fall, took the two relics and carried them through Serbia, Thrace and Macedonia to Paramythia in Epirus, hiding them in sacks of straw and telling anyone who asked that it was fodder for his beast. For three years he wandered from place to place until he reached Corfu in 1456, where he believed they would be safe, and there he left the saint's relic to a fellow refugee, the priest George Kalochairetis.
