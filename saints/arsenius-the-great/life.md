# Arsenius the Great

Arsenius was born at Old Rome in 354, of Christian and right-believing parents, and was brought up in learning and in the fear of God. Coming to a man's estate he proved exceedingly wise, for he went through the whole training of rhetoric and of philosophy and knew Greek and Latin well. Then, despising the vanities of this world and leaving Hellenic wisdom outside, he entered the ranks of the clergy in the service of God, seeking the spiritual wisdom that the Apostle James praises, calling it pure, peaceable, gentle, well subject, full of mercy and good in fruits.

He was ordained deacon of the great church at Rome, where he lived in purity and in soundness of mind, labouring to be a worthy dwelling of the Holy Spirit. In that time Theodosius the Great reigned in the east and Gratian at Old Rome in the west. Theodosius had two sons, Arcadius and Honorius, and was looking for a teacher who would give them not only human wisdom but divine — who would make them practised in the outward Hellenic philosophy and in the amended Christian wisdom that leads to what pleases God. He sent through all the parts and countries under his rule to find such a man, and could not: his envoys found many wise men whose lives were not pleasing to God, and many pleasing to God who were not learned.

The long body of the life, from his coming to the imperial court to his flight into the Egyptian desert, was not read through here, and nothing from it is set down.

At the close the life describes him: his look was angelic, like Jacob's in the Old Law; he was entirely grey, clean of body and dried up with great abstinence; his beard was long, down to his belt; the hairs of his eyelashes had fallen out from his daily weeping; he was tall and bent with age. He had a blessed end, having lived fifty-five years in the monastic labours, pleasing God with fasting and with prayers. In the desert he spent forty years at a place called Troe, above Babylon; ten years behind the city of Memphis; three years at Canopus of Alexandria; and returning again to Troe he spent two years and fell asleep there in the Lord, being a hundred years old and more. He was a good man, the life ends, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and its account adds to the Romanian one. saint.gr says he was born at Rome and was a deacon of the Church there, of rich and pious parents, and that it was at the prompting of the emperor Gratian and of Pope Innocent that Theodosius took him as teacher to his sons Arcadius and Honorius. At Constantinople he was received with great honours, given the title of patrician and honoured as βασιλοπάτωρ, and everyone admired him for his learning and the modesty of his manner; but he shunned the noise of the city and the luxury of the palace and begged God to free him from those high duties. One day he heard a voice from beyond the world telling him to leave it. He put off his splendid clothes at once, went in disguise to Egypt, entered a Skete and was tonsured, and was told there by God to train himself further in silence and stillness.

His learning and his ascetic bearing, his humility and his virtues made him a spiritual elder among the ascetics of the desert, and the fame of his holiness spread through the whole region, so that many came from the cities to hear him and to take his blessing; even Theophilus, Patriarch of Alexandria, visited him many times to take counsel on theological and church questions. On one of those visits Theophilus and some others asked him for a word for their profit. «If I tell you a word, will you keep it?» he said; and when they answered that they would, he replied: wherever you hear that Arsenius is, do not go near him. To avoid company, and the frequent barbarian raids, and to give himself undistracted to prayer, he left the Skete with his disciples Alexander and Zoilus for Petra near Memphis, and afterwards for Canopus, where he fell asleep in peace in the year 445. When his end was near his disciples asked where and how he would be buried, and the blessed man answered: my children, tie a rope to my feet and drag me to the mountain.

Saint Nicodemus the Hagiorite describes his appearance too — dry of body and tall in stature, the beard long as far as the belly, the look of his face angelic and venerable, like that of the Patriarch Jacob. The Greek page keeps three of his sayings. The first is the reminder he used to make to himself never to forget the reason he lived and had gone into the desert, which is deification, the end of every Christian's life. The second is a prayer: my God, do not abandon me, for I have done nothing good before thee, but of thy goodness grant me to make a beginning. The third is a counsel, that we should spend all our diligence on having the inward work according to God, and so overcome the outward passions — a saying Gregory Palamas quotes many times in his homilies. saint.gr adds that he is patron of Paros and that a fragment of his relics is in the monastery of Kykkos in Cyprus.

The Serbian church keeps him on this same menologion day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović tells the court and the flight at more length than either calendar this folder was built from. He was born of a patrician family at Rome, it says, and well schooled both in the worldly sciences and philosophy and in spiritual wisdom. Leaving all the vanity of the world he gave himself to the service of the church and became deacon of the great church in Rome: unmarried, withdrawn, silent and given to prayer, meaning to live his whole life so.

But God's providence directed his way otherwise. The emperor Theodosius took him as the tutor and teacher of his sons Arcadius and Honorius and made him a senator, surrounding him with great wealth and honours and luxury — all of which pressed on Arsenius's heart more than it satisfied it. It happened once that Arcadius committed some fault and Arsenius punished him for it; and when Arsenius learned that the offended Arcadius was plotting a heavy revenge on his teacher, he put on beggar's clothes, went down to the sea shore, took ship and sailed to Egypt.

When he reached the famous Scetis he became the disciple of the famous [John Colobos](/saints/john-colobos) and gave himself to the ascetic struggle. He reckoned himself a dead man; and when somebody brought him word that a rich kinsman had died and left him all his estate, he answered that he had died before his kinsman, so how could he be his heir. Withdrawn into a desert cell as into a tomb, he plaited baskets of palm leaves all day and prayed to God all night, avoided men and all talk with men, and came out of the cell only on feast days, to church, for communion.

So as not to grow idle he often put the question to himself: Arsenius, why did you come into the desert? He spent fifty-five years as a dweller in the wilderness, and for all that time he was a pattern to monks and the glory of monasticism in general. He lived a hundred years in all, the Prologue says, and ended peacefully after long labour and a martyrdom laid on himself of his own will, **in the year 448** — where the Greek page gives 445, so that his death now reads 445 or 448, with neither year chosen and the hundred years left as the book's own round figure rather than turned into a third reading.
