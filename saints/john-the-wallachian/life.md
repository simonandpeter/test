# John the Wallachian

John is called new because he suffered after 1453, when Constantinople fell to the Turks, and Wallachian because he was of Wallachia, that is the Romanian Land. The foreign peoples of old called the Romanians Vlachs or Wallachians, a people of Latin descent, and the memory of it is kept to this day in the name of the Holy Metropolis of Ungro-Wallachia.

He lived in the middle of the seventeenth century. Not long after the pious reign of Matei Basarab, Mihnea came to the throne of Wallachia, whom the country nicknamed the Turk-turned. Though he claimed to be a Romanian he was the son of a Greek moneylender; drawn from childhood to the ways of the Hagarenes he fled his parents, went to Constantinople and attached himself to Sinan Pasha, saying that he was the son of Radu Vodă, and lived forty years among the Turks and in their law under the name Gioan Bey. Having paid the sultan many purses of gold he became prince, and oppressed the people with taxes; the country stood against him, and he could keep his throne only by calling in the Tatars several times, who came in a horde, laid the districts waste and carried off multitudes of captives.

At last, unable to meet the Ottoman greed and wanting some gain for himself, he refused the tribute, joined two other voivodes and rose against the Turks. The sultan Mehmed's answer made a great destruction among the Christian people and carried off a great multitude of men, women and children, caught in lassoes and dragged bound behind the horses and the carts; this ruin happened in the year 1659, in the month of November or December.

Among those taken then was the youth John, born and raised in the Romanian Land of parents settled in faith, piety and good husbandry. He was fifteen years old, beautiful of face, bright in the eye and well made in body. While they were still on the road one of the pagans who were driving the Romanians into slavery bought him, with an evil purpose, and began to force him to defile his body with the sin first devised in Sodom. John resisted, and armed himself with prayer and the sign of the cross.

The man said in anger: are you so proud before me, and forget that you are nothing but a slave? John answered: I am the slave of Christ alone, but how wide my freedom is inside that divine slavery. The man said he had bought him with a price of gold and would do with him what he liked; John answered that Christ was his master, who had bought him with a price of blood, and that He does with him what is needful. When the pagan made to tie him to a tree and force him, John — sickened in his heart, and unwilling to be shamed before God — armed himself with the memory of the young David who overcame Goliath and of the soldier Nestor who threw down the pride of Lyaeus, watched for his moment and killed him.

The others noticed their companion missing, questioned him, learned the truth, put him in chains and took him the long road to Constantinople. It was winter, and the frost froze the youth's naked body; he bore it manfully, thinking it better that his body should burn in the blaze of winter than his soul in the blaze of Gehenna. Through the snowed-up passes of the Balkans and the Rhodopes the blizzard whipped his cheeks and bent his back, but his spirit stayed upright, though he guessed what waited at the end of the road, knowing that the killing of a Turk could not go unpunished.

At Constantinople his guards brought him to the dead man's wife and told her all that had happened, and she brought him before the vizier for judgement. John confessed the truth: that in killing the pagan he had done nothing but defend the purity of a true Christian. The vizier gave him into the power of the dead man's wife, to do with him what she would.

The young widow was taken at once with his beauty, brought him to her rich house and said to him: you are in my power; since you killed my husband, you will take his place and be my husband. From a slave I make you free, from a poor man I raise you rich. But to be my husband you must come over to my law; and nothing is easier than to deny Christ and become a worshipper of Allah. Look about you at the gold and the silver, the silks and the precious stones, and look at me beside you, who am ready for the marriage.

The temptation was not a light one — to change irons for silk, hunger for full tables, the road's weariness for comfortable rest, the lash of the blizzard for the breath of the zephyr, bleeding among mountain rocks for gliding in a caique on the Bosphorus. But John was full of virtue and would not bend, and answered her boldly: the purity of my soul I will keep and to your law I will not go over; the undefilement of my body I will guard and I will not marry you. She told him of Gioan Bey, a young man of his own people who had grown up in the courts of the serai and had come to be rich and a prince in his own country. He answered that he did not know what had become of their prince, who had fled over the mountains for fear of them, but that the people called him Mihnea the Turk-turned and with that shameful name he would be remembered; and that he did not want to be ashamed of his own name.

He remembered Joseph the son of Jacob before the wife of Potiphar in Egypt, and the memory strengthened him beyond measure. The woman tried every art she knew, for she was tormented by passion and had no rest, and the temptations lasted nearly two years and a half, while the youth grew to his full body; and he did not move from the firm foundation of his Orthodox faith.

At the last, seeing herself so refused, she gave him up to the eparch of the city, who threw him into prison; and there he was put to so many and such fearful torments that a man would be terrified only to think of them. They lasted several days, and even then she came to the prison every day with flatteries and promises. He stayed unmoved as a diamond, looking only to the Lord Christ, knowing that the purity of soul and of body must be kept whole, as a sacrifice well-pleasing before heaven.

When it was plain that they laboured in vain, they asked the vizier's leave to kill him, and the vizier ordered the eparch to do it. The men who had tortured him took him to Parmak-Kapi, near the Bezesten, and put him to death by hanging; and he went to the place of his martyrdom giving thanks to God. So in the year 1662, on the twelfth day of the month of May, the ever-blessed youth received the crown of victory from the Lord's hand. His life and his passion were written by John Karyophylles, a learned Greek of his own time, and printed at Venice by [Nicodemus the Hagiorite](/saints/nicodemus-the-hagiorite), after which they passed into the Greek synaxaria and then into the Romanian.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same civil day and adds the sequence of hands he passed through. saint.gr has him born in Wallachia of a noted and noble family, and seized at fifteen by the Tatars who invaded Wallachia under the sultan Mehmed to put down the rising of the lord Michael the Voivode. He tried to escape, killing a soldier, and was caught by others and handed over at Constantinople to that soldier's widow. She brought him before the vizier, who after questioning him gave him back to her to deal with as she pleased; and when she failed to make a Muslim of him she gave him up to the prefect, who failed in turn and ordered his death by hanging. He was hanged on 12 May 1662 at Parmak Kapi in Constantinople, and three days later Christians bought his honoured body back and buried it with reverence. His martyrdom, the page adds, was written by John Karyophylles.
