# The Forty-five Martyrs of Nicopolis in Armenia

When Licinius held the East — the pagan emperor whom the great Constantine had received into the empire — he ordered throughout his dominion that Christians who would not worship the gods be tortured and put to death by every kind of death, and their goods taken to build temples and baths and to renew the cities. When that order reached Nicopolis in Armenia, instruments of torture were made ready; and as many of the faithful were being seized and led off, some forty and more servants of Christ gathered and agreed not to wait until the pagans took them, but to go of their own will to the tribunal, confess the name of Christ and give themselves up.

The chief men among them, the page says, were Leontius, Mauricius, Daniel, Antoninus and Alexander, men notable in birth and in learning and good in their lives. They stood together before Lysias, governor of Armenia, and declared that they were Christians. The governor wondered at the single mind of so many men and at their willingness to be tortured, and asked where they were from and who had taught them not to worship the gods.

Alexander answered that they were of that place — some from the city and some from the villages — and that this earth was their country, but their Father was Christ in heaven, who taught them to worship neither lying gods, deaf and dumb, nor the work of men's hands. The governor asked where their Christ was, and whether He had not been crucified and died; and Leontius answered that He had died willingly and had risen and ascended, that the governor's gods were dead and did not live, and that theirs lives for ever and had taught them to die for Him that they might live with Him without end. They pressed the governor on what a god must be — just, pure and sinless — against what he himself said of Zeus.

The page's long account of their torments is not all reproduced here. It ends with a great pile of wood set alight and the martyrs thrown into it, some still living and some already dead; and when those who threw them in counted, one was missing, and they looked for him. Sisinius, lying beside the rock from which water had sprung at his prayer, called out, Here I am; take me and carry me into the fire — and they took him and carried him in.

When the fire had gone out the servants searched the ashes for the bones that were left, gathered what they found into a sack and threw them into the river Lycus. The river kept them as a costly treasure, holding them together in a shallow place near the bank, and believing men came and found them at once without trouble, and kept them all in an honoured place until the pagan emperor Licinius had perished. When Constantine reigned alone and the Church was given freedom, the bones were shown to all and a church was built in their name; and healings were given from the holy bones and from the spring that Sisinius had drawn out with his prayer.

These forty-five suffered on the tenth day of July at Nicopolis in Armenia, under Lysias the governor, while Licinius ruled in the East. The page gives no year.

The Greek calendar keeps them on the same day, 10 July, and its page gives the year and four of the names. saint.gr says the Forty-five were martyred at Nicopolis in Armenia, and that among them were the leading men of the city, Daniel, Mauricius, Antony and Leontius. When in the year 315 the emperor Licinius issued an edict against the Christians, these forty-five went to the duke of their own accord and declared themselves Christians; asked by Lysias who had persuaded them not to sacrifice to the gods, they answered, «Ο Χριστός είναι εκείνος που μας δίδαξε και μας έπεισε να μη λατρεύουμε θεούς ανύπαρκτους». The angry duke had them shut up bound hand and foot with neither bread nor water, and they passed the night at prayer, blessing the King of glory. Brought out of prison and asked whether they had repented and come back to the gods of the state, they answered with one mouth «χριστιανοὶ ἐσμέν» — we are Christians. Lysias then had their hands and feet cut off and had them thrown into the fire, and so they were counted worthy all together of the unfading crown of martyrdom.
