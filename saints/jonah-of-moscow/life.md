# Jonah of Moscow

doxologia.ro's calendar names him and gives no life. Its line for 31 March is «Sfântul Ierarh Iona, Mitropolitul Moscovei și al Întregii Rusii» — the hierarch Jonah, Metropolitan of Moscow and of All Russia — and the page the calendar links for him carries no Viață and no troparion: only icons, places of pilgrimage, articles, texts by the saint, photographs and the day's Gospel.

So the one thing the Romanian source states of him is his see and his day, and this entry records that and nothing more. The Russian calendar, which is the place his life would be read, has not been checked here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and says plainly that it has no life of him. «Γνωστός στη ρωσική Εκκλησία, άγνωστος στους Συναξαριστές», saint.gr writes — known to the Russian Church, unknown to the synaxaristae — and in place of a narrative it offers a comparison: he is for the Russians what St Mark Eugenicus, 1375 to 1461, is for us, which sets him among those who held the Orthodox side against the union of Florence. It heads him «(; - 1461)», the same year in which it puts Mark's death. What it does record is how he came to be sung: his service was composed by the hierodeacon Theophilus Paschalides and published at St Petersburg in 1897. It adds that his memory is repeated on 15 June, and prints no hymn of its own.

**The Russian church calendar keeps him on the Julian 5 October with the synaxis of the hierarchs of Moscow**, seventeen names in one line under one polyeleos service, marked «(Моск. Свт.)» and not expanded: Peter, Theognostus, Alexis, Cyprian, Photius, Jonah, Gerontius, Joasaph, Macarius, Philip, Job, Hermogenes, Tikhon, Peter, Philaret, Innocent and Macarius — two and a half centuries of metropolitans, then the patriarchs, then the metropolitans of Moscow and Kolomna, ending with a man shot in 1937. He is the sixth of them, and his index entry carries five days in all — 31 March for his repose, 27 May for the translation of his relics, 15 June, this synaxis, and «15 декабря (интронизация)», the day of his enthronement. That is as many days as any name in this corpus holds, and one of them is neither a death nor a translation. No year is printed on that page either; the year this folder now holds came from the Ohrid Prologue, with saint.gr's heading agreeing.

The Ohrid Prologue is the first of the sources read for him to print a life, and it prints a full one. He was from the province of Kostroma, and became a monk in his twelfth year; he lived a long time as a monk in the Simonov monastery at Moscow, and in the days of the metropolitan Photius he was made bishop of Ryazan. When Photius died, Jonah was chosen metropolitan and sent to the patriarch at Constantinople to be consecrated and confirmed; but a certain Isidore, a Bulgarian by birth, got ahead of him, reached Constantinople first, and was consecrated metropolitan of Russia, so that Jonah went back to his throne at Ryazan. Isidore's ill-intentioned tenure ended badly: he went to the Council of Florence, came back to Moscow only after three years, and they all rose against him as an apostate from Orthodoxy and drove him out, and where he ended his life is not known. Then Jonah came to the metropolitan throne, a good and wise shepherd, a great wonderworker, a man who saw what was hidden, a guide of souls; and when the Agarenes surrounded Moscow he drove them off by his prayer.

In old age he wished that some illness might find him, so that he might suffer and be fully cleansed by suffering before going into the other world. At his prayer God let a wound come on his leg, which had been shown beforehand in a vision to a certain priest; and of that wound he died, and went among the citizens of heaven on 31 March 1461 — the day he is kept on, and the year this folder had not held until now. Many wonders happened over his relics. One man who could not speak was brought to them: he kissed the saint's hand, and, as he told it afterwards, that hand took hold of his tongue and he felt a sharp pain; and when it let his tongue go and he turned to his own people, he spoke as though he had never been mute at all.
