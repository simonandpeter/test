# Xenophon the Founder

Xenophon came of a well-known and well-off family and was given a good education. The Romanian page, which follows Moses the Athonite's book on the saints of the Holy Mountain, sets him in the tenth and eleventh centuries and makes him a contemporary of Athanasius the Athonite; it reports that in Athanasius' own life it is told how Athanasius healed Xenophon's brother by a miracle, the brother having been ill with a cancer.

According to the tradition, the page says, Xenophon came to the place where his monastery now stands, venerated in a little church of Saint Demetrius, and there found an icon of Saint George which had been thrown into the sea during the iconoclasm so that it might be saved. He therefore dedicated his monastery to the memory of the Great Martyr George, in 998.

His life was marked, the account says, by discernment, by almsgiving and by humility, and before all by his justice, for which he was called "the just". Near the end of his life he gave up the abbacy in favour of his brother Theodore, so as to be at rest until his blessed end, in 1018. He was canonised by the Ecumenical Patriarchate on 24 November 1997, his first feast was kept on 23 and 24 April 1998, and a full service in notation was composed for the occasion. His memory is kept on 24 April.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr's account is built round an icon. Xenophon lived at the end of the tenth century and practised the ascetic life on the Holy Mountain; he came of a distinguished and wealthy family and had a considerable education, and he was a contemporary of Athanasius the Athonite, the builder of the Great Lavra — in whose life it is witnessed that Athanasius healed Xenophon's brother Theodore, who was suffering from the incurable disease of cancer.

When Xenophon came to the place where the monastery that bears his name now stands, he found, according to the tradition, a small church of the great martyr Demetrius the Myrrh-streamer and the wonderworking icon of the great martyr [George](/saints/george-the-trophy-bearer). That icon had been at Constantinople in the time of the iconoclasm, when the holy icons were being burned by the impious and the heretics, and had been thrown into the fire with others; but the fire was quenched and the icon was found whole and unharmed, to the shame of the impious. Then one of them, the boldest and most insolent of all, took a knife and struck the saint's face on the chin — and at once the blood that ran from the wound showed the saint living and wonderful, and rebuked the heretics' error mightily; and that, the page says, is to be seen to this day.

After what had happened those impious men fled, and a pious and God-loving Christian took the icon with fear and reverence and carried it to the shore; and there, having prayed fervently to God to save it, he cast it into the sea. So the icon of George came to the monastery of Xenophon, to be the support and consolation of the monks and the boundary-marker and guardian of that blessed place. When Xenophon saw the holy icon and learned of its strange arrival by sea, he raised at his own expense and with much labour a new and most magnificent monastery, which he dedicated to George the Trophy-bearer, and made it a school of virtue, a training-ground of asceticism, a divine dwelling of obedience and humility, a house of chastity and above all a house of almsgiving and love.

By his discernment and the fullness of love in his heart he drew the grace of the All-Holy Spirit upon himself; and so we see him coming as a peacemaker into the disputes and frictions among certain monasteries of Athos at the beginning of the eleventh century, over boundaries and encroachments, and settling them by getting justice applied and all the interested parties satisfied. His famous «ασφάλειες» — his contracts and agreements — survive, and in them, the page says, are vividly sketched the great wisdom of his mind, the gentleness of his heart, the peacemaking of his disposition, the justice of his judgement, and his rich and profound learning and cultivation; and by all the fathers he was called the just. Towards the end of his life he resigned the abbacy in favour of his brother Theodore and lived quietly, awaiting in peace the hour of his departure to the Lord; and the Lord gave him rest in peace, the Greek page says, between the years 1018 and 1035.
