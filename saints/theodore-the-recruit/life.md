# Theodore the Recruit

In the reign of Maximian and Maximin, pagan emperors, a great danger lay on the peoples who believed in Christ. Dreadful orders went out, and with them governors — tyrants, cruel and without humanity — through the whole of their dominion: a Christian willing to deny Christ was to have great honour and office from the emperors, and one who stood firm and would not sacrifice to the Greek gods was to suffer torments and a bitter death. Some confessed openly and died; some denied; some fled to the mountains and the caves; and some passed for pagans and were Christians in secret.

Theodore was one of these last, a Christian of long descent, out of the country of Amasea, of the place called Mialon. He was not afraid to confess, the life says, but held that it was not yet God's will that he should do it openly, and he looked for a sign. He had been numbered in the company called the Tiro for his understanding and his courage, and was set at the head of the company called the Legion; and when it went east under its general Bringas to the place called Euchaita, he found his sign there.

Some forty miles from that place was a forest, and in it a dragon, so that nobody dared take the road. Its dragging had torn the trees up around it, its hissing shook the whole district, and men had left the fields and vineyards they held nearby for fear of it. Theodore rode in to look for the beast, and resting from the search he fell asleep. A woman named Eusebia came by at that hour, woke him in tears and begged him to go: she was a Christian, she said, who had held land there from her parents and was leaving it now for fear of the beast, which she had seen kill many men before her eyes.

He told her not to fear and not to weep, and said that for the kindness of waking him God would give her back the inheritance she had. Then he crossed himself, rode on into the forest, met the dragon as fire came out of its eyes, and threw his spear so hard that it went through the head; the beast hissed, coiled and writhed, and died there. He came out of the forest rejoicing, for he had reasoned that having beaten that dreadful beast he would beat the power of the devil, who is the beast of the mind: the sign was given, and it was God's will that he should confess.

When the army and Bringas its general came to sacrifice to the idols, the rest went and sacrificed, and Theodore stayed in his tent; and so it was known that he was a Christian. Set before the whole army and asked whether he alone was a Christian, he answered that he was a Christian from the first, that he believed in his Christ and would sacrifice to Him, and that to their dissolute gods he would not sacrifice at all. Bringas gave him some days to choose what was good for him. He spent them in prayer, and in walking behind the Christians who were being taken off to prison, calling out to them to endure and not to deny Christ the King; and then he found his opportunity and burned down the temple of the mother of the gods by night.

A servant of the temple named Cronides had seen him set the fire, and, fearing the blame would fall on himself, seized him and brought him to the governor Publius. Theodore did not deny it: he had lit wood to burn stone, he said, and that was the whole of their goddess and her power, for fire touches her and burns her. He was beaten for the answer, and then shut in prison to die of hunger with the doors sealed. That night the Lord appeared to him and told him to take no earthly food or drink. The guards heard singing through the sealed doors and saw through the window a great crowd in white robes singing with him; but when the governor came and opened the prison he found no one inside but Theodore, bound in irons, and fear fell on him and on all who were with him.

Brought out again and offered a seat beside the judges if he would sacrifice, Theodore said that though they burned his body with fire, tore it with torments, cut it with swords or gave it to the beasts, he would not deny the name of his Christ while the breath was in him. He was hung up and scraped, and at the last condemned to the fire. A Christian named Cleonicus stood hidden there and wept to see it, and the saint called to him: Brother Cleonicus, I am waiting for you; come. Then he prayed, asking Christ to receive his small martyrdom, and leapt into the middle of the fire praising God.

The flame stood about him like a candle and did him no harm at all, and he gave his soul into the hands of God; and the writer who was a witness says that he saw that honourable soul go up towards heaven like lightning. Eusebia, the woman who had woken him in the forest, bought his relics at a great price and carried them to her own country, Euchaita, where she kept him as her helper in every need, and the sick of that place and the world beyond it had him for a physician of soul and body.

The page goes on to what he did after his death. Julian the Apostate, remembering that Christians fast above all in the first week of the Great Fast, ordered the eparch of Constantinople to have every food sold in the city secretly defiled with the blood of the sacrifices, so that the Christians should be made to share in them unawares; and the great martyr was sent to Eudoxius, then patriarch, and the plot was undone.
