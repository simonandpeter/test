# Pamvo of Nitria

Pamvo lived in the desert of the mountains of Nitria, in the parts of Egypt, to a deep old age. Many great fathers bear witness to his holy life, and the Romanian page is openly a compilation: its own closing note says the life was gathered from Palladius's Lausiac History chapter 10, from the priest Rufinus's book on the sayings of the fathers, from the Paradise of Heraclides chapter 1, from Socrates Scholasticus book IV chapter 18, and from the Egyptian Paterikon.

[Anthony the Great](/saints/anthony-the-great) said of him that the fear of God had made the Spirit of God dwell in him. Abba Pimen said: Three things I saw in father Pamvo — fasting every day, silence, and the work of his hands. Theodore the Studite bore witness that Pamvo should be called blessed as he deserves, in deed and in word, as a high father.

Socrates Scholasticus writes that at the beginning of his monastic life Pamvo, being unlettered, went to one of the brethren who was a teacher, wanting to learn the psalms of David. When he heard the first verse of the thirty-eighth psalm — I said, I will take heed to my ways, that I sin not with my tongue — he would not hear the rest, but went away saying that this one verse was enough for him if he could learn to fulfil it in deed, and he did not go back to the teacher.

Six months later the teacher saw him somewhere and asked why he had not come back. I have not yet learned to carry out that verse in deed, Pamvo answered. Many years afterwards one of the fathers who knew him asked, Brother, have you learned that verse now? — and he answered: I have been learning it for nineteen years without stopping, and I have only just got the habit of doing in deed what that verse teaches. Unlettered as he was at the start, he was afterwards given wisdom by God and had understanding enough of the divine Scriptures.

The Romanian page's collection of his sayings and wonders is a very long one and is left here unsummarised. At the end, near his death, he said that from the day he settled in that place he had never spent a day without working something with his hands until he was tired; that he could not remember ever eating for nothing bread given him by anyone, but ate the bread he had himself from the labour of his hands; and that no word had come out of his mouth that he should be ashamed of or repent of in that hour — and that he was going to God as one who had not so much as begun to live in a way pleasing to God and fitting for a monk. Saying this he gave his honoured and holy soul into the hands of God, without any bodily pain.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 18 July, and saint.gr places him. It calls him the ornament of the ascetics of the mountain of Nitria and a contemporary of Saint Melania and Saint Isidore, and then says that he was great in everything: in piety, in speech and in silence, in counsel, in forbearance and in strictness, in experience, in knowledge, in wisdom, in simplicity, and generally everywhere. It names three of the disciples he raised up — the bishop Dioscorus, Ammon, and John the nephew of Dracontius.

Although he was always abstinent and hard on himself, the page says, his health was vigorous and he was never once ill in his life; and his death was not merely quiet and painless but glad, going to the heavenly Father. His spiritual children buried him, glorifying God who had counted them worthy to be taught by so great an ascetic. No year is given anywhere, and there is no hymn. The same Greek day keeps a second man of his name, [Pambo the Recluse](/saints/pambo-the-recluse), who lived a thousand years later and is a different saint.
