# Anthimos Vagianos of Chios

Anthimos — Argyrios Vagianos in the world — was born on 1 July 1869 in the
parish of St Luke at Livadia on Chios, the youngest of the three children of
poor and virtuous peasants, Constantine and Argyro; from childhood he was
marked by a holy boldness, by piety and by love for Christ and the Church,
and as a child he was granted visions of the Mother of God promising him her
protection. The family’s poverty let him finish only the elementary school,
and he learned the shoemaker’s trade to keep himself. On 23 August 1889 he
went up to the skete of the Holy Fathers Nicetas, John and Joseph of Chios
on Provateion Oros, the sheep mountain, so that the icon-painting monks
there might restore the family icon of the Mother of God “She who is quick
to hear”, which he had from his mother; I saw those blessed fathers, he
remembered, and my heart burned with longing for the monastic life. The
elder of the skete, the monk Pachomius, once the spiritual father of
Nektarios of Aegina, became his guide and confessor. Back in the village he
built himself a hut on its edge and gave himself to ascetic labours under
Pachomius’s rule, and the elder, admiring his obedience, silence and love of
prayer, told his monks that this young novice was already a true monk and
would be a great father. The icon he kept by him all his life was his one
helper, his inspiration in the good fight, his comfort in trials, and worked
wonders for those who visited him in his solitude. In 1898 he was told to
settle in the skete for good and was tonsured with the name Anthimos, and by
the holiness of his life soon became superior of the convent of St
Constantine, whose spiritual father was the elder Pachomius, working at any
task, the building of the house included. His weak health soon gave way, and
since he needed doctors he built, at the elder’s direction, a modest lonely
cell on his family’s land at Livadia, where he lived as if in the monastery,
earned bread for himself and his aged parents at the last, gave alms to the
needy, and fought the demons that raised a fearful din at his night prayers
— he loved to pray at night sitting in the hollow of the old olive beside
his cell, and once, by the Mother of God’s help, passed nineteen days and
nights without sleep, with a crust of bread and a little water every second
day, until at the end he was rapt among the choirs of angels, repeating
without ceasing, Lord, have mercy. On 25 July 1909 the igumen Andronicus
tonsured him into the great schema at the skete of the Holy Fathers.

The people of Chios saw in him their spiritual father, but the metropolitan
of the day, Jerome, would not have him ordained, on the ground of his lack
of schooling, until his kinsman and godfather Stephen Diomataris took him to
Adramyttium for a short course of study. On 7 November 1910 Metropolitan
Joachim of Ephesus ordained him deacon and the next day Bishop Alexander of
Myrina priest, with the rank of archimandrite and confessor, in the church
of St Anne at Smyrna, amid signs — an earthquake during the ordination,
lightning, thunder and rain, the lamps of the iconostasis swinging in one
rhythm and one of them falling before the astonished people — and soon
afterwards he healed a man possessed. Chios received him as an angel of
comfort; the envy of some of his fellow clergy sent him in 1911 to the Holy
Mountain, where he learned the cenobitic life more deeply, and in 1912
Metropolitan Jerome, on his return, made him priest of the leper hospital.
There, far from the noise of the town, he served daily, prayed and read,
shared the lepers’ food, taught them and cared for them with great love, and
turned a place where human misery corrupted souls into an image of paradise:
many of his charges came to the true way and some became monks, the best
known of them the monk Nicephorus. By a divine revelation he found the icon
of the Mother of God “the Helper”; anointing with oil from her lamp and the
elder’s sign of the cross healed hundreds, and thirty-eight possessed were
freed by his fasts and prayers before it. When the Greeks of Asia Minor were
driven out by the Turks in 1914, many nuns and laywomen found shelter with
him and became the first sisters of the monastery he was moved by the icon’s
wonders to found as a home for the orphaned nuns and a dwelling of the
Mother of God’s grace — she had healed him of an incurable illness, and in
thanks he clothed her icon in silver as in white garments; she was for him,
he said, a cool, shady tree in whose shade he rested. In 1927 he was allowed
to build in the Frankomachala quarter, once the Turks’, drew the plan
himself, and against those who called the work useless and untimely began it
at the cost of his labour, tears and prayers: the foundation was blessed on
Meatfare Sunday, 19 February 1928, the building began the next day, the nuns
giving their savings and the income of their handiwork and working with
their hands while the laity gave money and labour, and the monastery of the
Panagia Voithia opened on 30 November 1930. The wonders of the Mother of God
and the elder’s humility brought new girls to it until the sisters numbered
eighty-eight; its first abbess was the humble nun Eupraxia Glyka (died
1956), and his own sister Calliope was tonsured there as Callinice. In the
years that followed, under the discerning abbess Martha Petinaki (died
1986), the house flourished through unceasing spiritual struggle, want and
sacrifice; the elder served and laboured in it daily, a father to nuns and
laity alike and a confessor famed as a saint, and countless signs followed
his prayers — bodily healing for every sick person who came, and for every
visitor, sick or penitent, some comfort, spiritual counsel or healing herbs,
but above all his tearful prayer before the icon, with sixty or seventy sick
on some days. He ordered that after his death the ministry pass to his
gifted disciple the nun Bryene Kontozoglou, abbess from 1986 to 2005, to
whose patient labour the handwritten volumes of his teachings are owed. For
more than thirty years he served the salvation of souls and the healing of
bodies, and when he grew too old for work with his hands he shut himself in
his cell and begged the Lord to let him help his neighbour in any way to the
last moment of his life. Having given the community counsels full of divine
wisdom he departed to the Lord on 15 February 1960, at 6.35 in the morning,
aged ninety-one, and the whole island mourned him. His precious relics,
streaming myrrh, were found on 3 September 1965, the day this entry stands
under; the wonders he worked daily brought the Church to his canonisation on
14 August 1992, the first hierarchical liturgy in his honour was served in
the monastery on 15 February 1993, and on 16 August of that year the first
church in his memory was opened in his old cell on the family land near St
Luke’s at Livadia. In the monastery families are strengthened, the young
turn to the life in Christ, the sick are healed, the possessed cleansed, and
healthy children are born to once-barren couples who have eaten the leaves
of the laurel bush planted there in his memory and named Anthimos; a copy of
the icon and a portion of his relics are kept in the chapel of the
university hospital at Patras. The Greek calendar keeps him on 15 February,
and on 3 September for the finding of his relics.
