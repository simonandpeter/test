# Gorgonia

Gorgonia's homeland, doxologia's life says, was a city of Caesarea called Nazianzus. Her father was named Gregory and her mother Nonna, a Christian from her parents and her grandparents before them; her father had been born to servants of idols and spent a long time in that error himself, until Nonna used every means she could find to bring him to the Christian faith — for he was a man of good understanding and good will even before he knew the truth, and God would not leave so good a soul unlit.

After his baptism Gregory shone so far in the virtues that he was made bishop of Nazianzus, his own city. Of Nonna, her son [Gregory the Theologian](/saints/gregory-the-theologian), Gorgonia's brother, wrote that she never turned her back towards the eastern part of the church, and never spoke there during the divine service or at any other time unless there was great need. From such a mother, and with such a father and teacher, Gorgonia became another Nonna in her reverence towards God.

Her parents married her, and yet she so far outdid the women of old who are praised for their chastity that she joined to marriage the virtue of virginity, and showed that virginity alone does not unite a person to God, nor marriage bind them to the world; it is the mind that governs both well or ill. Her husband she brought to the faith, so that she had in him not a lord set over her without order but a fellow servant; and her children, and her children's children, she made fruits of the Holy Spirit by her counsel and her example.

She wore no gold, the life says, put no false hair on her honoured head, clothed herself in no costly stuff, did not shine with pearls or diamonds and never covered her face with paint. The only colour she loved was the one that modesty and reverence bring to the faces of devout women, and the only ornament she wanted was the one that comes of abstinence and fasting; what she desired was to adorn her soul with good Christian habits and good works.

At the end, after a long vigil and much prayer, a vision was given her that showed on what day she would die, so that death should not find her unprepared; and the one thing still wanting to her — that her husband should be baptised — she asked of God and received. When the appointed day drew near she fell ill, said to her husband, her children and those she loved what a woman who loves husband and children and brothers should say, and taught them about the heavenly life, so that her last day became a kind of feast.

Her kinsfolk and others stood about the bed in deep silence, none of them daring to ask her anything, for it did not seem right to honour with weeping one who was leaving the world with the marks of holiness on her. Her father, standing close, saw her lips move a little, leaned his ear to her and heard her saying a verse of David's psalm fitted to that hour: «Mă voi culca și voi dormi cu pace» — *I will lie down and sleep in peace*. That, the life says, was the proof of the boldness and the holiness with which she went out to Christ. The page gives no year for her, and prints no hymn.
