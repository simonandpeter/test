# Probus of Tarsus

In the reign of Diocletian and Maximian, when Numerius Maximus the proconsul judged at Tarsus in Cilicia, three Christians were seized in the city of Pompeiopolis — [Tarachus](/saints/tarachus-of-tarsus), Probus and Andronicus — and brought before him. When Tarachus had been taken away in chains and Probus was set before the judge, he was asked his name first of all; and he answered that the first and most honourable name he had was that of a Christian, and the other, put on him by men, was Prov.

His father, he said, was from Thrace, and he himself had been born at Perge in Pamphylia. When the proconsul offered him honour from the magistrates and the friendship of the court, he answered that he wanted no honour from rulers and did not desire their friendship, for he had left his glory and the abundance of his wealth in order to serve the living God. They stripped him, stretched him on the ground and beat him with ox sinews.

The centurion Demetrius, pointing to the blood running on the earth, told him to spare himself, and he answered that his body was in their midst but the torments did not shake him in his faith. Turned over and beaten on the belly, he called out for God’s help; asked by the judge where his helper was, he said that his help helped him and would help him, and that he thought nothing of the torments and would not bend to the judge’s will because of them.

At the later hearings he was hung head downwards and his thighs burned with heated spits; wine and the meat of the sacrifices were forced into his mouth, and he said only, «Știe Domnul și vede silnicia pe care o rabd» — the Lord knows and sees the violence I endure. Red-hot nails were driven into his hands, and he thanked God that his hands too might suffer for the Name. When he told the proconsul that his great power had not only maddened but blinded him, his eyes were put out; and he said the eyes of his body were taken, but the living eyes of faith could never be taken from him.

The rest, the page says, was written by three faithful men — Macarie, Felix and Verie — who stood hidden at the games a thousand paces outside the city. When the martyrs were carried in, for their wounds would not let them walk, the crowd turned its face away and wept and began to cry out against Maximus, and many left. The beasts loosed on them would not touch them: a bear that had killed three men that day came up and licked [Andronicus](/saints/andronicus-of-tarsus)’s wounds, and a lioness sent from Antioch by Herod ran about seeking to escape, then came to the martyrs, bent her knees and lay down before Tarachus, and when he pushed her off and provoked her she stayed beside him like a gentle sheep. So, the page says, these holy martyrs made their end.

Maximus left soldiers to guard the bodies, ordering that they be mixed with the bodies of the condemned so that Christians should not know them and steal them. The three men prayed, and at once the earth shook with thunder and lightning and a storm of rain, and when they came near the fire was out and every soldier had fled. The night was very dark, and three lights like stars appeared above the relics; they took them up in secret and followed the lights over the mountain, and laid them in a cave cut in the rock, securing the entrance. Going back into the city they found the guards put to death by Maximus.

The Greek calendar keeps the three of them on the same day, 12 October, and saint.gr adds where each came from and how they answered. Probus was from Pamphylia, Andronicus from Ephesus and Tarachus from Illyria; all three were soldiers, it says, truly most devout and thoroughly grounded in Holy Scripture, and they were arrested by Maxentius the eparch of Tarsus while they were in the desert, in the persecution under Diocletian and under Flavian governor of Cilicia. Brought before him, each of the three answered in turn, and Probus said that he was a Christian and that nothing drew him so much as to suffer ten thousand torments for Christ and to pour out his blood for him. Struck down by their answers, the page says, Maxentius had the skin flayed from [Tarachus](/saints/tarachus-of-tarsus)'s head and the eyes of the other two put out, and at last, unable to bear such humbling, beheaded all three.
