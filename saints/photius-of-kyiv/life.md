# Photius of Kyiv

Photius was a Greek from Monemvasia in the Peloponnese. He entered a monastery as a boy and was tonsured by the elder Acacius, a great ascetic and afterwards metropolitan of Monemvasia. In 1408, when he was at Constantinople with the patriarch on his metropolitan’s business, the Russian see stood empty after the death of [Cyprian](/saints/cyprian-of-kyiv) in 1406, and the patriarch Matthew chose Photius for his learning and holy life. He was consecrated metropolitan on 1 September 1408 and came to Rus’ a year later. He spent half a year at Kyiv, from September 1409 to February 1410, setting in order the southern dioceses that lay in the Lithuanian principality; seeing that the metropolitan’s throne could not stay in a land falling more and more under Catholic Poland, he followed the earlier metropolitans who had moved to Vladimir and then to Moscow, and came to Moscow at Pascha 1410.

For twenty-two years he led the Russian Church through wars, feuds among the princes and Tatar raids — in the spring of 1410, when he had come from Moscow to Vladimir, the khan Edigu sent a force under the prince Talych to take the metropolitan himself prisoner — and raised the spiritual standing, the means and the beauty of the churches of the Moscow see, enough to send large help to the impoverished patriarchate of Constantinople.

The separate Lithuanian metropolia, set up at Prince Vytautas’s insistence, was abolished in 1420, and that year he visited the returned dioceses and greeted them with a long letter of teaching. He wrote against the strigolniki heresy of Pskov, which ceased in 1427, and left an order for the election and consecration of bishops (1423), a teaching on the dignity of the priesthood and its duties, and a spiritual testament telling his life; the all-Russian chronicle compilation of about 1423 was made under his direction.

On 20 April 1430 an angel told him of his coming death, and he died at the time foretold, on the feast of the Placing of the Robe of the Theotokos, 2 July 1431. His relics were found in 1471, and two of his sakkoi are kept in the Armoury of the Moscow Kremlin.

The Greek calendar keeps him too, on 2 Ιουλίου, and claims him as one of its own: «Ο Άγιος Φώτιος Μητροπολίτης Μόσχας, ήταν Έλληνας και καταγόταν από την Μονεμβασία της Πελοποννήσου» — Photius, metropolitan of Moscow, was a Greek and came from Monemvasia in the Peloponnese. saint.gr gives his tenure as metropolitan of Moscow from 1408 to his death in 1431, and titles him of Moscow rather than of Kyiv.

It adds one thing the Russian notice does not: that his relics lie incorrupt in the cathedral of the Dormition of the Theotokos in the Kremlin. The page prints no hymn for him and nothing else of his life.

**The Russian church calendar keeps him on the Julian 5 October with the synaxis of the hierarchs of Moscow**, seventeen names in one line under one polyeleos service, marked «(Моск. Свт.)» and not expanded: Peter, Theognostus, Alexis, Cyprian, Photius, Jonah, Gerontius, Joasaph, Macarius, Philip, Job, Hermogenes, Tikhon, Peter, Philaret, Innocent and Macarius — two and a half centuries of metropolitans, then the patriarchs, then the metropolitans of Moscow and Kolomna, ending with a man shot in 1937. He is the fifth of them, and his index entry carries five days in all, including «10 октября (Волын.)», a Volhynian commemoration.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him on the Julian 2 July, the same menologion day the Greek calendar keeps him on and not the day the Russian calendar does, and its whole entry is four sentences. A Greek by origin, it says, which both the other books say too. He governed the Russian Church wisely twenty years, where the Russian life says twenty-two. He reposed in the year 1430, where the Russian life gives a full date, the second of July 1431, so the folder now reads 1430 or 1431, widened to hold both books rather than deciding between them.

And a week before his death, this book says, an angel of God appeared to him and announced the exact time of his going out of this world. The Russian life has that warning too and sets it on the twentieth of April 1430, two and a half months before the death it records rather than a week; both are written here and neither is chosen. Its heading calls him metropolitan of Moscow, as the Greek page does and as the Russian life, which has him of Kyiv and of all Rus, does not — a third book on the see, and the two readings stand side by side in this life as they did before.
