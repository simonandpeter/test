# George the Trophy-bearer

George was a Cappadocian by birth, the son of well-known Christian parents, and was taught the faith from his childhood. His father ended his life as a martyr, and his mother took the boy to Palestine, where her own family and her estates were. He grew up handsome and uncommonly strong, was enrolled in the army and set as tribune over a famous company; and after he had shown his courage in war Diocletian honoured him with the rank of comes and of voievod, at twenty years of age, not yet knowing that he was a Christian. His mother had died by then.

Doxologia's account opens with the emperor's oracle at the temple of Apollo, which told him it could no longer foretell anything because the just on earth were binding its power; when Diocletian asked who the just were, his servants answered that they were the Christians, and the persecution that had barely stopped was raised again. The emperor called his proconsuls, governors and the whole senate to Nicomedia to settle it. George was standing beside him, and on the first day he saw that nothing would turn their counsel.

So he gave away to the poor all the gold and silver and clothing he had by him, freed the slaves who were with him, and appointed that his lands in Palestine be shared out and their slaves freed as well. On the third day, when the sentence against the innocent was to be confirmed, he stood up in the middle of that great assembly with an open face and asked the emperor and his counsellors how long they meant to turn the law against men who had wronged no one. The long account of his trial and his sufferings follows.

At the end he stood before Diocletian again and told him plainly that gods who could not help themselves, and who would not endure the presence of the servants of Christ, were no hope for an emperor. At that the empress Alexandra came forward, confessed Christ before them all, fell at the martyr's feet and reproached the tyrant's folly; and when her husband spoke to her she turned away and gave him no answer. Diocletian made no further enquiry and sentenced them both to the sword.

The soldiers led them out of the city. On the road the empress's strength failed her, she asked to sit a little, turned her head to the wall and gave up her spirit; and George, seeing it, glorified God and went on with great joy. At the place appointed he prayed that his soul be delivered and that the sin of those who had wronged him not be counted against them, and then bent his head under the sword. He was beheaded on the twenty-third day of the month of April. The page gives no year, and none is set down here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, with a rule of its own for the years when the day falls before Pascha: saint.gr heads his page «Ειδικός υπολογισμός. Εορτάζει στις 23 Απριλίου εκάστου έτους. Εάν όμως η εορτή πέσει πρίν το Πάσχα, τότε εορτάζει την επόμενη μέρα του Πάσχα (Δευτέρα της Δικαινησίμου)» — kept on 23 April every year, but if the feast should fall before Pascha, then on Bright Monday. Its page gives him years, which the Romanian one did not: born about 275 in Cappadocia of Christian parents, dead in 303. His father died a martyr for Christ when George was ten, and his mother then took him with her to her own country, Palestine, where her estates were; at eighteen he enlisted in the Roman army, and though young he discharged his duties perfectly and was admired by everyone for his bearing, so that he was quickly promoted and given the title of count, and Diocletian esteemed him greatly.

What the Greek calendar adds around him is a whole day's company. Its entries for 23 April tie to his passion his mother [Polychronia](/saints/polychronia-mother-of-george), the poor farmer [Glycerius](/saints/glycerius-the-farmer) whose ox he raised, the two generals [Anatolius](/saints/anatolius-the-general) and [Protoleon](/saints/protoleon-the-general) who confessed at the sight of his torments, [Athanasius](/saints/athanasius-once-a-magician) who had been among the magicians, and — on the day before — the empress [Alexandra](/saints/alexandra-the-empress), who confessed during his martyrdom and died in prison two days before he did. [George of Shenkursk](/saints/george-of-shenkursk) is kept on this day for another reason: the Russian ascetic fell asleep, the tradition says, on the feast of his own patron. The page lists twelve Greek towns that hold him patron and ten places where portions of his relics rest, from Iviron, Xenophontos and Zographou on Athos to the Lavras of Moscow and St Petersburg and Roman Catholic churches at Venice, Ferrara and Rome.

The Serbian church keeps the whole company on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović heads the entry with the great martyr alone — „Св. вел. муч. Ђорђе.“ — and names the rest in its body. George was born in Cappadocia, the son of rich and devout parents; his father suffered for Christ and his mother moved to Palestine. Grown, he went into the army, reached the rank of tribune in his twentieth year, and in that rank was in the service of the emperor Diocletian. When the emperor began his terrible persecution of the Christians, George stepped before him and confessed boldly that he was a Christian too.

The emperor threw him into prison, ordered his feet set in the stocks and a heavy stone laid on his chest; then had him bound to a wheel with boards of great nails set under it and turned on it until his whole body was one wound; then buried him in a ditch with only his head above the earth and left him there three days and three nights; and then had a magician give him a deadly poison. Through all of it George prayed without ceasing, and God healed him on the instant and saved him from death, to the great wonder of the people. And when by his prayer he raised a dead man, many received the faith of Christ.

Among them, the entry says, were the emperor's wife [Alexandra](/saints/alexandra-the-empress), the chief priest of the idols [Athanasius](/saints/athanasius-once-a-magician), the farmer [Glycerius](/saints/glycerius-the-farmer), and [Valerius](/saints/valerius), [Donatus](/saints/donatus-23-april) and [Therinus](/saints/therinus). At the last the emperor condemned George and his own wife to be beheaded with the sword; the blessed Alexandra gave up her spirit at the place of execution before the sword came to her, and George was beheaded in 303. The wonders at his grave are without number, the entry says, and so are his appearances, in dreams and waking, to those who have remembered him and asked his help from then to this day.

The entry ends with what it reads in all of it: that having caught fire with love for Christ the Lord, it was no hard thing for George to leave everything for the sake of that love — rank, wealth, imperial honour, friends and the whole world — and that for that love the Lord rewarded him with a crown of unfading glory in heaven and on earth, with life eternal in His kingdom, and with the power and authority to help in straits and troubles all those who glorify him and call upon his name. The 303 agrees exactly with the year this folder already carried, and Cappadocia with the birthplace; the twentieth year and the tribune's rank, the stocks and the stone, the nailed wheel, the three days in the ditch and the poison are the entry's own additions to what was read here before. **His mother is not named** — only „мајка му“, his mother, who moved to Palestine — so `polychronia-mother-of-george`, whom the Greek calendar names, **took no row from this day**.
