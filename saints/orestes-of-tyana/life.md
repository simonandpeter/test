# Orestes of Tyana

While the pagan Diocletian reigned in Rome, a commander named Maximinus came to him and asked leave to go through the parts of Cilicia and Cappadocia and to seize and torment the Christians there with every kind of punishment — to burn them in fire, drown them in water, break their bodies and their bones, kill them with the sword, give them to the beasts — so as to wipe the memory of those who would not worship the great gods from the earth. The emperor praised him and gave him that power not only in Cilicia and Cappadocia but in the whole of his empire.

Taking his commission, Maximinus set out with pride and great force against an innocent Christian people, like a ravening wolf against sheep. Coming into the parts of Cappadocia he entered the city called Tyana, and as he asked after the Christians he heard from the townspeople of a physician named Orestes, that he was a Christian and taught the people every day, diligently, that all should honour Christ.

At the end, when Orestes had answered that he was ready to endure every torment, having in his heart the sign of his Lord Jesus Christ, the tormentor in his rage ordered twenty iron nails, each a hand's breadth long, to be brought and driven into his heels. Then he ordered him tied to a wild horse and dragged until he died; and the horse, driven very hard, pulled him over the roads and the fields, through thorns and over sharp stones, and in those torments Orestes gave up his spirit into the hands of the Lord, having been dragged twenty-four stadia from the city.

The tormentor ordered his body thrown into the river called Fiva. Afterwards a man bright as the sun appeared in that place, gathered the relics of the saint, and buried them on the mountain near the city of Tyana. The Romanian life gives no year; it places him by the reign of Diocletian and by nothing else.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 10 November, and gives the trial in dialogue. saint.gr has Orestes of Tyana in Cappadocia, and says certain hagiological sources make him a physician; in the period of Diocletian's persecution, which it puts at about the year 289, the governor Maximus pressed him in every way to deny Jesus. When the pressure would not bring him to embrace the idols they stripped him, beat him and scourged him for punishment, and then threw him into prison for seven days. After the seventh day they took him to a temple of the idols to offer sacrifice and pray; and the governor asked him why he refused to embrace the worship which their revered emperors followed with such reverence. Orestes did not hesitate to answer with courage that he was a willing subject in all that concerned political and earthly things, but that beyond those he could not accept any other emperor than the one true God. Then Maximus ordered his ankles pierced with an iron chain, which they tied to an unbroken horse; the horse was let loose and set off at a frantic gallop dragging the saint behind it, and stopped only after it had covered twenty kilometres. So he gave up his spirit to the Lord.
