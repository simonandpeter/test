# Andrew the Fool for Christ

saint.gr says his life was written by Nicephorus, presbyter of the Great Church of Constantinople, about the middle of the tenth century, in the reign of Constantine VII Porphyrogennetos. Andrew himself came from Scythia and lived in the days of the emperor Leo VI the Wise, whose reign the page dates 886 to 912. From childhood he had been sold as a slave to a protospatharios and general of the East named Theognostos, a virtuous and devout man who came to love the boy so much that he treated him as a son and saw to his careful and God-fearing education.

What drew Andrew above everything else was the sacred letters, and in particular the lives and the martyrdoms of the Christian contestants. His zeal for them was such, the page says, that he was called «σαλός», a fool, because it pushed him again and again into enduring mockery, humiliation and heavy abuse, and into acts that are judged unbalanced and eccentric. But he bore the degradations, comforted by the fact that he often succeeded in bringing strayed souls back to the straight road.

He was marked out too for his love of man and his doing of good: he did not only share what he had with the poor but gave away what he had himself and went hungry and naked, and to those who reproached him for his excessive almsgiving he would recall the Lord's words that what is done to the least of his brethren is done to him, and say that in the face of every man, and most of all of a suffering brother, he saw Christ.

At an all-night vigil in the church of the Blachernae he saw the Mother of God in the heavens, praying and covering the people with her precious veil. The page tells two wonders of his hiddenness. Going secretly, as was his custom, to the church of the All-Hymned Mother of God in the left portico of the forum of Constantine, he stretched out his right hand and sealed the gates with the sign of the Cross, and they gave way at once; a servant boy who had followed him unnoticed saw him afterwards hanging in the air before the ambo at his prayer, and was seized with terror, saying to himself that those who are truly fools call this servant of God a fool. Andrew met him on the way out and asked him only to keep what he had seen.

Another time, towards the end of Lent, while the people of the reigning city were acclaiming Christ with palms and hymns, he saw an old man of fair appearance entering the church of the Holy Wisdom, followed by a crowd with palms and crosses that shone like lightning and a sweet and saving melody; the old man held a harp and struck its strings with the singers. Andrew leapt for joy and spoke of David and of the Lady who intercedes, and the learned men standing by laughed at him and went away, for he had said it because he saw David himself with the other prophets come there.

So he lived in the love of God and reposed in peace at sixty-six years old. Myrrh and incense at once gave out their fragrance in the place where he gave up his spirit, and a poor woman living nearby smelt it; the Lord, who knows each man's judgements and the hidden achievements of his saint, removed the relic. saint.gr adds, on the authority of Nikodemos the Hagiorite, that Andrew wrote many prophecies which were never published and which lie at the monastery of Iviron, and it prints the prayer he is said to have made before his blessed repose.

**The Russian church calendar keeps him on the Julian 2 October and the Greek on 28 May** — two days for one man, and the Russian day is the one that explains itself: it is the eve of the Protection of the Mother of God, the feast of the vision he was given in the church at Blachernae. The Russian also gives the year the Greek page does not, 936, so this folder has a date at all because of this reading. It calls him «блж.», blessed, where the Greek has ὅσιος, and its index of names heads him «Андрей Константинопольский, Христа ради юродивый».
