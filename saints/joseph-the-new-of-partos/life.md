# Joseph the New of Partoș

Joseph the New was born at Ragusa in Dalmatia — Dubrovnik — in 1568 to
well-off parents: his father John, a Venetian shipowner, drowned in a
shipwreck when the boy was twelve, and his mother Catherine, from Lemnos, was
known for her piety. Jacob at baptism, he was taken after his father’s death
to Ohrid, where his mother’s brother was a rich merchant; he was well
schooled, and spent five years with a hieromonk cousin of his mother at the
monastery of the Theotokos there. In 1588 he came to the Holy Mountain and
settled at Pantokrator, where he was tonsured and named Joseph in the great
schema and ordained priest two years later, training himself in obedience,
prayer and study with calligraphy for his handiwork, at which he excelled. He
lived afterwards at the Great Lavra, Chilandar, Xeropotamou and Vatopedi,
where he stayed longest and, his biography says, worked his first miracles,
healing many of the monks by prayer and his right hand laid on their brow.
From Vatopedi he was chosen abbot of St Stephen at Adrianople, a dependency
of the Lavra, for six years; back on Athos he was abbot of Koutloumousiou
and left it many spiritual children, and then withdrew to Vatopedi again for
quiet.

There, in 1650, at eighty-two and after sixty-two years on the Mountain, he
was chosen metropolitan of Timișoara for his virtue. A Romanian scholar,
Damaschin Udrea, happened to be at Vatopedi on pilgrimage; Joseph asked him
to teach him Romanian, which he did not know and would need, and learned it
well in three months, so that at his enthronement he spoke to his flock in
their own tongue. Consecrated at Constantinople by Patriarch Parthenius II,
he reached his diocese with letters, an escort, hardships and miracles, and
for three years shepherded it — a great defender of Orthodoxy, teaching and
consoling with the warmth of his faith and the gentleness of his heart,
healing the sick and the paralysed above all, founding a church school for
the clergy, building new churches and reopening closed ones, on good terms
with every ruler, the Turks included, whose language he knew well. Twice his
prayer brought rain that saved the city from fire, which is why in Romania
he is the patron of firemen. In 1653 he resigned his throne and withdrew to
the monastery of the Archangels at Partoș, and died there three years later,
on 15 August 1656, the monastery’s bells ringing of themselves; three
bishops, many clergy and thousands of the faithful buried him, and the
miracles went on. His teacher Damaschin, whom he had ordained deacon and who
followed him to the end, wrote his life in 1701. The honour paid him was
forgotten when the region was conquered and Partoș destroyed; Metropolitan
Vasile revived it in May 1956 by taking up his relics, which gave out an
unspeakable fragrance, and moving them to the cathedral of the Three
Hierarchs at Timișoara, and that October the Romanian Patriarchate
recognised his sanctity, three hundred years after his death, and set his
memory on 15 September. The Serbian Patriarchate keeps him on 27 May, and
Vatopedi on 17 August. The Greek and Romanian calendars keep him on
15 September, the Romanian with a troparion and kontakion that call him the
unconquered defender of the land of Banat.
