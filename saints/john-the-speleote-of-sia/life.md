# John the Speleote of Sia

About three kilometres north-east of the village of Sia, in the district of Nicosia in Cyprus, a ruined church stands beside a stream, and the people call it Ai Giannis. The first church on that spot, the Greek synaxarion says, was built not for the Theologian, to whom the villagers have since dedicated a new church further north, but for a local saint, the venerable John the Speleote, also called Potamitis; the ruin that stands there was rebuilt in 1870. Some ten metres east of it is a cave cut into the rock by the stream, with a stone ledge he used as a bed and a narrow seat; he sheltered there from the rain, above all in winter, and the bed and the little knife with which he dug the cave are still shown.

Five holy monks lived in that country, the entry says, and three of them were revealed: Therapon, Eutychius and John. About 700 there was a Roman settlement there called Pampoulia, and the Alaman saints came to it from Palestine, the five among them; John passed by Sia, dug his cave, lived in it and took his name from it. After a fire in 2002, three priests found an old icon of him forgotten in a corner of the cave; it is shown only on his feast. The entry tells of healings in recent years — a boy of fifteen from Kornos with leukaemia, a young man from the Kokkinochoria paralysed by a fall from scaffolding, who was told in a dream to lie on the saint’s stone bed and rose healed, and the two infant children of a man from Lympia, lifted clear in a road accident by a shining old man in white. His head was found at the monastery of Kykkos, whose bishop Nikephoros gave a portion of it to Sia, where it is kept in the church of the Panagia Chryseleousa and carried out only on 26 September, when he is kept together with the Theologian. The Greek calendar prints no hymn for him.
