# Abraham the Recluse

Abraham was born of Christian parents and from his youth busied himself with the work of the church, listening to the divine Scriptures and learning them eagerly. His parents loved him greatly and pressed him to marry; he would not, and being forced many times he at last gave way to their will. On the seventh day after the wedding, as he sat in the house with his wife, the longing for God rose in his heart like a light, and he got up and went out without telling anyone.

Led by God he left the city, found an abandoned house about two stadia off, and settled there gladly. His parents and kinsfolk searched everywhere and after seventeen days found him in his cell at prayer; he asked them not to wonder but to glorify God who had delivered him from a vain world, and to leave him there in quiet. They said Amen. He shut the doors and left one small window for his food, and so lived, and his mind was enlightened, and word of him went everywhere, for he was given the word of wisdom and of consolation.

Ten years after he left, his parents died and left him much property. Rather than break off his prayers and his stillness he asked a friend to divide the whole of it among the poor, and so was left without care. He owned on earth one garment, a cassock, a small vessel to eat and drink from and a mat to rest on, and in all his fifty years of monastic life he never changed his rule. There was near a certain city a very large village of pagans, from the least to the greatest, and nobody could turn them to God: presbyters, deacons and whole companies of monks had tried and given up.

One day the bishop, sitting with his clergy, remembered Abraham and said he had never in his life seen so perfect a man in everything good and pleasing to God, and that he would set him as priest in that pagan village, since with patience and love he might bend their hearts. He went with the clergy to the cell. Abraham was grieved and begged to be left to weep for his own sins, being weak and unworthy; the bishop answered that sitting there he saved only himself, and there he would save many, and that he had renounced everything but had no obedience. Weeping, Abraham said, "God's will be done: I go for obedience."

He came to the village, saw the people held in delusion and serving idols, and sighed and wept. What he then endured lasted three years: he was bound, beaten, driven out, dragged by a rope about his neck, stoned, left hungry and thirsty, and once thrown down for dead. He was never angry with them, never said a word back and never lost heart, but added to his love and longing for them, entreating and teaching the old as fathers, the young as brothers and the children as sons, while they insulted and mocked him.

Then one day the whole village gathered, small and great, astonished at his patience, and said among themselves that a man who had borne such torments without leaving them or speaking one evil word must truly be sent to them from the God he was always speaking of; and that the weakness of their own gods had been shown, since they could do him no harm when he broke them. They ran with one soul to the church crying glory to the God of heaven, and Abraham, seeing them, rejoiced, and his face shone like the morning light. He baptised them all that day, about a thousand souls.

For a year after their baptism he read them the Scriptures day and night and taught them of the kingdom and of paradise, of the fire of gehenna, of righteousness, faith and love, and they received it like good ground. Then, seeing how greatly he was loved and honoured, he feared that his mind might be tied to an earthly care and his monastic life changed. He rose in the night, prayed God to keep that good flock he had won, signed himself with the cross and went away secretly to another place. The bishop, when the people came weeping to him, sent many men to search and they did not find him, and he ordained worthy men of the village presbyters and deacons and went his way.

Abraham heard all this and rejoiced, and went back to his old cell, built a second small one beside the first and shut himself in. The devil, raging with envy, came to him with words of praise: a light like the sun shone in his cell at midnight and a voice as if from God called him blessed above all men, and he knew the deceit at once and drove it out with the name of Jesus Christ. After that satan came with an axe to cut the cell down, and it stood whole; set his mat burning under him, and he walked on the flame; came in the shape of a young man to upset his dish, and sang the whole of the psalm of the blameless, and got an answer only when the meal was finished.

Abraham had a brother in the flesh with one daughter. When her father died the child was left an orphan and was brought to her uncle, being seven years old, and he set her to live in the outer cell while he kept the inner, with a little door between them, and through it he taught her the Psalter and the other holy books. She lived as a monastic, in fasting, prayer and all the labours of that life, and he often prayed for her with tears that her mind might be sound and not bend to earthly vanity; her father had left her property enough, and he ordered all of it divided among the poor. For twenty years she lived beside him like a clean lamb and an undefiled dove.

After twenty years the devil set snares for her, so as to reach Abraham through her. There was a monk who was a monk in name and had no works, who came as if to profit by the saint, saw the girl and burned for her, and tempted her about a year until, with satan's help, he entered her cell and deceived her. Struck with horror at what she had done, she tore her clothes and beat her face and would have strangled herself, saying that she had killed her own soul, angered God, and cast her holy uncle into shame; and asking where now was the teaching of her uncle and of his friend Ephrem, who had told her to keep her soul undefiled for the immortal Bridegroom. She went off to another city, changed her dress and went into an inn.

Abraham saw in a vision a great and terrible dragon come hissing to his cell, swallow a dove and go back to its place; he woke in grief, fearing a persecution or a division in the Church. Two days later he saw the same dragon come, lay its head under his feet and burst, and he put in his hand and took the dove out of it alive and undefiled. Then he called the girl twice, saying he had waited two days to hear the praise of God out of her mouth, opened the little door and did not find her, and understood the vision, and wept: the wolf had carried off his lamb. He prayed the Saviour of all the world to bring his little ewe Mary back into the fold of life.

Two years Mary lived away from him while he prayed day and night for her. After two years somebody told him where she was and how she was living; he sent an acquaintance to make sure, and then had a soldier's clothes brought and a horse, put on the clothes and a high cap to cover his face, took one gold piece with him and rode off. At the inn he looked about for her, then said to the host with a smile that he had heard a beautiful girl was there and asked to be shown her. The host, seeing him grey-haired, laughed in his heart and supposed his errand was fornication.

She was brought. When he saw her in her harlot's finery he was near breaking into weeping, but held it in, lest she know him and run. They sat and drank, and this wonderful man began to jest with her; and she got up and embraced him and kissed his neck, and smelt the sweet odour that came out of his clean body, worn with ascetic labour. Remembering the first days of her continence she sighed and wept and cried, "Woe is me!" The host said she had lived with them two years and he had never heard a word of sorrow from her; she answered that she would have been happy to have died before those years. Abraham, not to be recognised, said harshly, "Now you remember your sins, when you have come to me?" — and gave the gold piece to the host for a good supper.

He who for fifty years had never eaten bread to the full nor drunk water enough now ate meat and drank wine to save a soul that was lost, and the life stops to wonder at it. After the supper she said, "Sir, get up, let us go to bed and rest," and he said, "Let us go." In the room he sat on the rich bed and told her to shut the doors and come and take off his shoes; and when she came near he kissed her and caught her hands and held them fast so that she should not escape, and threw off the soldier's cap, and wept: "My daughter Mary, do you not know me? Am I not the man who raised you? Where is your angelic look, your continence, your weeping, your vigil, your bed on the ground? From the height of heaven you have come down into the pit. Why did you not tell me when you sinned, so that I might have taken the penance for you with my beloved Ephrem?"

She became like a lifeless stone in his hands, afraid and ashamed, and he went on entreating and teaching her until midnight: had he not come here for her sake? He would answer to God for her on the day of judgement; he would take her penance on himself. She said she could not look at him for shame of her deeds, and how should she pray to God, being defiled. "Your sin be upon me," he said; "let God ask it of me, and do you only listen to me and let us go to our place." At last she laid her head on his feet and asked all night what she could ever repay him. In the morning she asked what to do with the little gold and the clothes she had; he said to leave it all there, for it belonged to the enemy.

They set out at once. He put her on the horse and led her, walking in front, rejoicing in his heart like a shepherd who has found the lost sheep and carries it home on his shoulders. At their place he shut her in the inner cell where he had lived before and took for himself the cell Mary had had. She put on a hair garment and lived in monastic life with humility and weeping, with abstinence and meekness, calling on God for help and repenting with such earnestness that our repentance, says the life, is a shadow beside hers. God had mercy on his servant and forgave her, and as the sign of her forgiveness gave her the gift of healing the sicknesses of those who came to her.

Abraham lived ten years more, and seeing her great repentance, her tears, her labours and her prayers, was comforted and glorified God. Then he came to his end and fell asleep in the Lord, being seventy years old from his birth. Almost the whole city gathered when he died, and all came eagerly to his honourable body, and the sick were healed. [Mary](/saints/mary-niece-of-abraham) lived five years after her uncle's departure in great abstinence, praying to God with tears day and night, and passed over in peace.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 29 October, together with his niece, and saint.gr adds the shape of his working life. A champion of self-restraint and of the spiritual exercises, it calls him: he left the great fortune he had inherited to the poor and gave himself wholly to the service of God and of his neighbour, living in a desert place where he prayed and studied the holy writings, and going out from there into various cities to preach the word of God. His faith, his love and his patience often calmed barbarous hearts and drew to the cross souls that had grown altogether wild. Past seventy he kept all the vigour of that apostolic work, and his age itself let him give himself to the saving of sinful women. He died at a great age, serving God faithfully to the end.

The Russian church calendar of the Сретенский monastery keeps him on this same day with [his niece](/saints/mary-niece-of-abraham), gives the pair the year neither the Romanian nor the Greek page gave them — «(ок. 360)» — and names the place, the village of Hidana near Edessa, and the hand that wrote his life: Ephrem the Syrian, his countryman and contemporary. It adds the shape of his years. He left his parents' house in the flower of his age for a desert place, and when they died refused the inheritance and asked his kin to give it to the poor; then, his faith being tried, he was appointed presbyter to one of the pagan villages of Mesopotamia, and laboured three years there, pulling down the idol-house and building a church, bearing insults and heavy beatings from the idolaters, until the villagers believed and he baptised them himself.

Having done that he went back to his own desert, and the calendar tells two of the devil's attempts on him there. Once at midnight a light shone in his cell and a voice said, blessed are you, blessed as no other man is; and he answered that he was a sinful man who hoped in the grace of his God and was not afraid. Another time the devil came as a young man with a lighted candle, singing the psalm *blessed are the undefiled in the way*, and the old man crossed himself and asked: if you know that the undefiled are blessed, why do you trouble them? To vanquish them and turn them from every good work, it said; and he answered that it won its victories over those who had left God of their own will, and that from those who loved God it vanished like smoke before the wind. After fifty years of that life he died in peace. The day page prints a troparion and a kontakion for him, the first the common of a venerable father with his name in its last line, the second his own.
