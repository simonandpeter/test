# John (Maximovitch)

John Maximovitch is held by Orthodox Christians everywhere, doxologia's life says, to be one of the greatest saints of the twentieth century. Showing several forms of holiness at once, he was a great theologian inspired by God and a fool for Christ, a zealous missionary hierarch and a defender of the poor, a perfect ascetic and a loving father to orphans. Like Moses he led his flock out of bondage, bringing them from China to the free world.

He was born on the estate of Adamovka in the flourishing region of Kharkov in southern Russia, on 4 June 1896, to Boris and Glafira of the noble family of Maximovitch, and at his baptism was given the name Michael in honour of the archangel. The most illustrious man of that old family had been the hierarch John, metropolitan of Tobolsk, a writer and spiritual poet who sent the first Orthodox mission to China and was numbered among the saints in 1916.

The life says that he received from God the power to heal suffering souls and bodies, and that he knew and answered people's thoughts before they had spoken them; that what drew people to him was not his many miracles but the power of the love of Christ. In a Catholic church in Paris a priest told the young that there was no need of arguments for holiness, since a saint was walking the streets of Paris that day — Saint John the Barefoot. In the European hospitals he was known as the bishop who could pray all night for a dying man, and he was called to the bedside of Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox and Jew alike.

The whole Orthodox world called him the wonderworker of Shanghai, of Western Europe and of North America. In an age of spiritual exhaustion and of great social upheaval he chose to live as the great ascetics of the Thebaid and of Palestine had lived. On 2 July 1994 the glorification of Archbishop John of San Francisco, a hierarch of the Russian Orthodox Church outside Russia, took place in America; and he continues, the life says, to pray for and to visit those who call on him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same 2 July, and saint.gr's long notice adds a handful of particulars. It names his parents Boris and Glafyra, and says he was a descendant of the aristocratic Maximovitch family, one of whose members was proclaimed a saint in 1916 — the hierarch [John Maximovitch, metropolitan of Tobolsk](/saints/john-of-tobolsk), whose relics the page says remain incorrupt at Tobolsk to this day.

It tells the childhood as the Russian sources do: a sickly, quiet, very gentle boy who dressed his toy soldiers as monks, collected icons and religious books, read the lives of the saints and stood long at prayer in the evenings; the eldest of five, and so their first teacher in the faith; and so impressive in his Christian life that his French Catholic governess was baptised Orthodox. At eleven his parents sent him to the military school at Poltava, where he met Bishop Theophan of Poltava, who marked him deeply.

The page gives the dates of the course after that: the military school in 1914, the law faculty in obedience to his parents, flight to Yugoslavia with his family in 1921 and the theological school at Belgrade, reader in 1924 and deacon and monk in 1926 at the monastery of Milkovo, and election as bishop of Shanghai in 1934 although he did not want it because of his difficulty of speech. It closes with the opening of his tomb in the autumn of 1993 and the body found incorrupt, the formal proclamation on 2 July 1994, and — because he flew so often — his patronage of those who travel by air.

His own church keeps a second day for him, and it is not his death: «Обретение мощей свт. Иоанна, архиеп. Шанхайского и Сан-Францисского (1993)», the finding of his relics in 1993, twenty-seven years after he died. The Russian calendar's index of names is also the one that gives him the epithet in full — «Святитель Иоанн (Максимович) Чудотворец, архиепископ Шанхайский и Сан-Францисский», John the Wonderworker — and keeps both see-cities in the title, where the Romanian and Greek pages name him more simply.
