# Theodore (Ivanov)

Theodore Mikhailovich Ivanov was born in 1895 at Tobolsk to Michael and
Elizabeth Ivanov, a deeply religious family in which hard work and the
wish to help a neighbour were the first virtues: the Ivanovs buried the
kinless at their own cost and fed the hungry, and the children kept up the
works of mercy when grown — the martyr’s sister Eulalia helped the old in
an old people’s home for many years, and the hospitable house of Claudia
and Eulalia was always open to the priests who served at Tobolsk. The
father was courier and watchman at the state archive and earned extra by
sewing men’s clothes; when he died in 1911 ten children were left on
Elizabeth’s hands, the youngest ten months old, and she took her husband’s
place at the archive and did needlework besides — left a widow with
children and no means, Eulalia wrote of her, she managed to raise them all;
meekness, humility and above all faith in God and hope in Him helped her
bear every hardship. From childhood Theodore served in the cathedral; the
cathedral was cold, and sleeping in it he caught bad chills more than once
(others say the illness came after a hunt with his father), so that at
thirteen he fell ill with rheumatism of the joints and a year later was an
invalid, his legs paralysed. For the first years the pain was so bad that
at times he cried out, but it did not embitter him: his family never heard
a coarse word from him. In 1916, at the solemn canonisation of John of
Tobolsk, Alexandra Dulepova, an acquaintance of the imperial family,
visited him and proposed to his mother that the boy be shown to Dr
Derevenko, the heir’s physician; the doctor said the diagnosis had been
right and the treatment wrong — the medicine given first should have been
given second — and that had he been free to take the case the boy would be
walking about the room with a stick. Then Dulepova arranged that at the
festal service of the glorification, on 10 June 1916, the last canonisation
of the synodal era, Theodore should be laid to the relics of the saint;
there was a great crowd, bishops, priests, pilgrims from all Russia, and
during the singing of “Praise ye the name of the Lord” he was laid to the
holy relics, and from that time the pains in his legs ceased, though he
never stood again: twenty-eight years he lay bound to his bed and bore his
cross with patience.

For his deep faith the people held him in great respect and many visited
him — monks, priests, bishops; sometimes at the vigil in the cathedral,
when the anointing began, the bishop would anoint a few, leave a priest in
his place, and go to Theodore to anoint him and then the whole household.
He watched over the souls of his own: you look at no one, only pray, he
told his sisters going to church, and then come and tell me everything,
which Apostle and which Gospel were read — and they stood, Eulalia said,
not daring to stir for fear of missing a word. People came to him for
counsel and comfort before the revolution and far more after it, as the
churches closed one after another and the priests were arrested — sixteen
churches were shut in Tobolsk in 1929, and the empty churches were filled
with the dekulakised, thirty thousand of them by the summer of 1930. In
the first years after the revolution Eulalia was summoned by the organs and
pressed to give evidence against her brother; when every argument was
exhausted a revolver was put to her temple, and from somewhere deep a
prayer rose in her, I am with you, and no one against you — she did not
sign. Working later as a secretary in the executive committee, she fled a
superior who locked his office and tried to force her, ready to leap from
the windowsill; she ran home to her brother in tears, and he said two words
that strengthened her for life, so that she never wept again, not even
burying her mother and her husband. Of the exiled clergy Archimandrite
Sergius (Serebryansky), confessor of the Martha and Mary convent in Moscow,
exiled to Tobolsk in 1923 for his support of Patriarch Tikhon, and Fr
George Skripka visited him, and many clergy wrote to him for counsel: more
than 180 letters from all ends of Russia were found at his arrest. Raisa
Redikultseva remembered being taken to him as a child — very, very
handsome, she said — and how he told her mother, you have two children, a
son and a daughter; the daughter is all right, but the son … and shook his
head; and the brother took to drink and later drowned. He sent a gift to a
nun of the John-Vvedensky convent who died suddenly soon after, and a woman
who came with them he would not receive: you have come to me as to a
fortune-teller, and I am no fortune-teller. The house was watched and those
who came to it were harried, so people came at night; his brother
Vyacheslav had been shot merely for knowing a young man the authorities
suspected. The authorities warned him again and again to stop receiving
people: how can I forbid it, he said; I cannot drive people away.

In 1937 the Tobolsk NKVD began wide arrests — a hundred and thirty-six in
one month, many priests and believing peasants among them; on 30 August
Archbishop Artemius (Ilyinsky) of Tobolsk was shot with eighteen or
nineteen others. In August the authorities warned Theodore once more that
he would be arrested if people did not stop coming; soon they came with a
search and took his books and letters, and that evening his sister and her
husband found him glad and calm: they were here, and they will come again
very soon; but why pay them any attention. A few days later the NKVD came
for him. Fedya, guests have come for you, said his sister. Well, I am
always glad to receive guests, he answered meekly, and when told he was
being taken: since you have such an order, I submit to the authorities. His
mother went for the one suit he owned, kept for his burial, and was
stopped: it will not be needed. A stretcher was readied and he showed them
how to lift him, hands locked behind his head, another holding the legs,
stiff as sticks but not without feeling; the one who took his legs felt the
ascetic’s enormous strength, cried out in fright and dropped him, and his
sister caught the legs and shouted at them — monsters, what are you doing
to a sick man? — do not upset yourself, he said lovingly, it is bad for
you. Laid on the stretcher he prayed and said: my dears, mother and sister,
do not wait for me and do not trouble; they will not tell you the truth
anyway. Pray. Do not weep for me and do not look for me. He asked his
sister for her little red cap — I do not much like red, but let it be a
keepsake, for as long as the memory lasts — put it on and was carried to
the prison, where he was laid in the cell face to the wall, that he should
see no one, and forbidden to speak. His sister and her husband Gennady
Khilkov, a choir director from Samara exiled to Tobolsk for the faith,
carried parcels to the prison for some days; soon Gennady was arrested too
and never returned. In prison Theodore was asked nothing, never carried to
interrogation, never visited by an investigator, and none of the hundred
and thirty-six arrested with him was asked about him; the whole charge
rested on a character reference from the town soviet — a religious fanatic
whose flat was a hotbed preparing an armed rising against the Soviet
power, who kept and spread leaflets calling to revolt, corresponded on the
preparation of a bandit rising and passed himself off as a saint — and the
indictment made all hundred and thirty-six members of a White Guard
organisation preparing to overthrow the Soviet power by arms when Japan
attacked the USSR. On 11 September 1937 the troika sentenced him to be
shot, and he was shot that day in the Tobolsk prison and buried on its
grounds; the Russian calendar keeps him on 30 August of the old style, 12
September, and in the synaxis of the saints of Tobolsk. At the same time
the nun Raphaela of the John convent was arrested — shortly before the
search she had brought two church books to his house, saying, you are
sick; they will not come to search you — and she was soon shot. After his
martyr’s death Theodore has not ceased to intercede for those who turn to
him, and to this day many receive healing by his prayers, above all those
whose legs are diseased. The short life is by Igumen Damascene (Orlovsky);
the Holy Synod numbered Theodore among the new martyrs and confessors of
Russia on 7 October 2002.
