# Jeremiah

Jeremiah was of Anathoth, the city of the priests in the portion of Benjamin, about three miles from Jerusalem, and his father was Helkias, a chief among the priests. The Romanian life says he was chosen and sanctified a prophet from his mother's womb, as the Lord told him; that on the witness of Ignatius the God-bearer he was a virgin all his life; and that he began to prophesy while still a child, saying to God that he did not know how to speak, for he was a boy — and the Lord touched his mouth with his hand and put his words there. The word of the Lord came to him in the thirteenth year of Josiah, king of Judah, when he was fifteen years old.

The life then follows the fall of Jerusalem: Pharaoh Necho against Josiah, Jehoahaz set up and put down, Jehoiakim's tribute, and Nebuchadnezzar carrying off the vessels of the house of God. Jeremiah was nearly condemned to death by the priests and the false prophets, and was saved when the elders recalled the days of Hezekiah, and again when Ahikam the son of Shaphan held them back. He was put in irons, and dictated to [Baruch](/saints/baruch-the-prophet) the book that Jehoiakim burned, and then a second and longer one.

Under Zedekiah he wore a wooden yoke on his neck before the kings' envoys; the false prophet Hananiah broke it; and at the last the princes had the prophet let down on ropes into the pit of Malchiah, where there was no water but mire, and he stood in it up to his neck until an Ethiopian of the court, the life calls him Avdemeleh, had him drawn out. He carried the tabernacle and the Ark to the mountain where Moses had looked out on the land of promise, and hid the Ark in a cave there, sealing the door with letters written in the stone by his finger.

After the city fell, the people would not hear him and took him down into Egypt against his will, and Baruch with him. He lived there four years, held in great honour for his holiness and for his good works, killing the asps and the crocodiles that destroyed the people by his prayer. He foretold that the king of Babylon would come upon Egypt, and the Jews settled there, angry at him, stoned him to death. In the same year the king of Babylon came and the whole company perished as he had said. Long after, his relics were carried to Alexandria by Alexander of Macedon. The page gives no year for any of it.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, and saint.gr's notice fills in the beginning. He was born, it says, probably about 650 BC at Anathoth, a small town of the tribe of Benjamin north-east of Jerusalem; his father was a priest named Chelkias, and he was brought up strictly in that priestly household and studied the prophets before him, Isaiah and Hosea. Very young, about twenty-three or twenty-five years old, he was called by God to the prophetic office, and he shrank from it, pleading the weakness of his youth, until God promised him not rewards or honours but the most precious thing of all, His help.

The page sets his public work out in four periods: under Josiah before the reform, under Jehoiakim and up to Zedekiah, under Zedekiah, and after the fall of Jerusalem and the king's captivity. Against the false prophets who promised that the stolen vessels of the temple would come back he put a yoke on his own neck, and when Hananiah broke the wooden yoke he answered that God would set iron ones on their necks. The rulers threw him into a miry pit because his words unnerved the fighting men, and Abdemelech had him drawn out of it. Of his ascetic life the page keeps one detail: he wore nothing but a linen girdle.

At the end, taken to Egypt against his will with the people who fled there, he preached to them too and foretold their ruin; and he was stoned by his own countrymen at Taphnas in Egypt, or, as some rabbinic tradition has it, carried off captive with [Baruch](/saints/baruch-the-prophet) by Nebuchadnezzar in an invasion of Egypt in 568 BC. His synaxis, saint.gr adds, used to be kept in the church of the Apostle Peter near the Great Church.

The Serbian church keeps him on this same menologion day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović is the longest of the three accounts and the one with the prophecies against the kings in it. He was born, it says, six hundred years before Christ in the village of Anathoth not far from Jerusalem, and began to prophesy in his fifteenth year in the time of king Josiah. He preached repentance to the king and to the magnates and to the false prophets and the priests, and in that king's time he barely saved his life from the murderous hand of the embittered magnates.

To king Jehoiakim he foretold that his burial would be the burial of a donkey — that he would be thrown out dead beyond Jerusalem and his body dragged long over the ground without a grave — and it was for that prophecy, this book says, that Jeremiah was put in prison. Unable to write there, he called [Baruch](/saints/baruch-the-prophet), who stood at the little window of the prison while the prophet dictated to him. When the prophecy was read out to the king, he snatched the paper in his anger and threw it into the fire; but God's providence brought Jeremiah out of the prison, and the prophetic word was fulfilled on Jehoiakim.

To king Jeconiah he foretold that he would be carried off to Babylon with his whole household and would die there, and so it soon came about. Under Zedekiah he put a yoke on his own neck and walked through Jerusalem, prophesying the fall of the city and slavery under the yoke of the Babylonians, and he wrote to the Jewish captives in Babylon that they should not hope for a return, for they would remain in Babylon seventy years — which also came to pass.

In the valley of Tophet below Jerusalem, where the Jews had offered their children in sacrifice to the idols, he took a sound pot in his hands and broke it before the people, prophesying the swift breaking of the kingdom of Judah; and soon the Babylonians took Jerusalem, killed Zedekiah, plundered and destroyed the city, and cut down an enormous number of Jews in that same valley of Tophet, on the very ground where the children had been slaughtered to the idols. The book calls the broken vessel a pot when he breaks it and a chain when it recalls the place, and that slip is left as it stands.

With the Levites he took the Ark out of the temple and carried it to Mount Nebo — the mountain where Moses died, this book says, where the Romanian life has him look out on the land of promise — and hid it there in a cave; and the fire out of the temple he hid in a deep well. Forced by some of the Jews to go down with them into Egypt, he lived there four years and was then stoned to death by his own countrymen. To the Egyptians he foretold the breaking of their idols and the coming into Egypt of the Virgin with the Child. There is a tradition, the book adds, that Alexander of Macedon himself visited the prophet's grave, and that the body was carried to Alexandria and buried there by his order.

**This book's six hundred years before Christ is fifty years from the Greek page's probable 650**, and neither was chosen: his birth now reads 650 or 600 BC. Neither this page nor either of the others gives a year for his death.

The Russian church calendar keeps him on the same day and tells the course of his service. He was the son of the priest Хелкия of Anathoth near Jerusalem, and he was called to prophesy in the fifteenth year of his age; he refused, pleading his youth and that he could not speak, and the Lord touched his lips and said that he had put his own words into them. For twenty-three years he reproached Judah for turning from the true God to idols, standing in the gates of the city and at the door of the temple wherever the people gathered, and they answered him with mockery and tried to kill him.

To set out before them the servitude to the king of Babylon that was coming, he put on his neck a yoke of wood and then one of iron and walked so among the people. For that the elders threw him into the pit of the prison, filled with stinking mire, where he all but died, and a God-fearing courtier drew him out. When Nebuchadnezzar came, the prophecy was fulfilled, and the king released him from prison and let him live where he would; he stayed in the ruins of Jerusalem and wept for his country. The account carries the tradition from the second book of the Maccabees that he took the ark of the covenant with the tablets and hid it in a cave of Mount Nabau, so that the Jews could not find it again.

The remnant of the people, fearing vengeance after the murder of the governor, resolved to go down into Egypt. He turned them from it, foretelling that the punishment they feared would overtake them there as well; they would not listen and carried him with them by force to Tahpanhes, where he lived four years and was honoured by the Egyptians, because by his prayer he had killed the crocodiles and the other creeping things that filled those parts. When he began to foretell that the king of Babylon would lay Egypt waste, the Jews killed him; and the prophecy was fulfilled in that same year. There is a tradition that two hundred and fifty years afterwards Alexander of Macedon carried his relics to Alexandria.
