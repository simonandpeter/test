# Andrew (Gnevyshev)

He was born on 3 August 1872 in the town of Bezhetsk, the son of a merchant, Ivan Gnevyshev, who dealt in flour and rented a shop in the town. In time he took up trade himself, in flax seed and flax fibre, and became an expert in flax growing; without giving up his own business he worked as a grader for a French firm that kept him as its agent in Bezhetsk. In the first world war he was called up and fought in the ranks until the front fell apart in 1917.

After the revolution the authorities took everything he had — warehouses, shops and houses. The house he lived in with his wife burned in a fire in 1921, and he built a new one on the same ground, which the authorities then took too, so that the two of them had to rent a room; and he never grieved over the loss of any of it: God gave and God has taken away. In 1937 he still had a horse with its winter and summer harness, and that let him work as a carter for a state office. He was a man of deep faith and the warden of the church of Nicholas the Wonderworker, where the vicar bishop of the Tver diocese was then serving, with whom and with whose priest brothers he was on close terms.

The time of mass persecution came, when the local branches of the NKVD were required to draw up a certificate on every churchman and believer for their arrest afterwards. On 3 November 1937 the deputy head of the Bezhetsk NKVD office drew up his, which said that he belonged «to a church counter-revolutionary group made up of former traders and nuns»; and in the night of the second to the third of November he was arrested, shut in the Bezhetsk prison and questioned at once.

Tell the investigation which former traders and clergy you are acquainted with and keep up connections with. He answered that he kept up none at present; that about 1932 or 1933 he had once been invited to the bishop's name-day, and that he had sometimes called at the bishop's flat on church business. Asked what had been talked of at the name-day, he said that while he was there nothing had been said on political subjects or about the Church and the state, and that he had left soon. The investigation has evidence, the investigator said, that at a gathering of clergy you spoke of the collective farms as unprofitable and were indignant at the Soviet regime; do you admit your guilt in this? «I do not admit my guilt in this, and I expressed no such thoughts against the collective farms or the Soviet power.»

The officer did not give up hope that the churchwarden would break under pressure and incriminate himself, and the questioning went on. The investigation has evidence that in September 1937 on the Market Square in Bezhetsk you carried on counter-revolutionary agitation against the constitution and the elections to the Supreme Soviet, calling on people not to vote; do you admit your guilt? «I do not admit my guilt in this, and I declare that I carried on no counter-revolutionary agitation against the constitution.» The investigation demands truthful evidence of your counter-revolutionary activity for the last time. «I declare that I shall not tell the investigation about counter-revolutionary activity at all, since I never carried any on.»

On 27 December 1937 an NKVD troika sentenced him to ten years in a labour camp. After the change of the NKVD's leadership and the shooting of Yezhov his wife began to petition for his release, and the NKVD left the sentence as it stood. He died on 7 March 1941 in the Kargopol camp in the Archangel region, and was numbered among the new martyrs and confessors of Russia for veneration throughout the church at the Jubilee Council of Bishops in August 2000.
