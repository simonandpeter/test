# Alexander (Medem)

Alexander was a layman, a landowner's son who spent the last thirteen years of his life being arrested. He was born in 1877 at Mitava in the Kurland province, the son of a senator who had held many high offices and had been governor of Novgorod — a man, the calendar says, of whom the people kept the kindest memory, who drove out alone to the disorders of 1905, walked into the middle of a rioting crowd, took off his cap and began to speak quietly until it fell silent and listened.

His father had bought an estate of six thousand desiatinas in the Khvalynsk district of the Saratov province in the 1870s, and of it the son afterwards sold two thousand to the peasants at the lowest price he could. He finished the gymnasium at Novgorod and in 1897 the law faculty of the university of St Petersburg, but the law did not draw him; he had been attached to the land from infancy, took part in nearly every kind of farm work, and came by a great deal of practical knowledge and a deep love of his own country and people. In 1901 he married, and four children were born, a son and three daughters.

He managed the estate until 1918. When the Soviet power confiscated every private holding he rented what land he could work with his own hands, and the family lived poorly, often on borrowed money and sometimes with no horse, the plot thirty kilometres off and reached on foot or by whatever cart was going that way. When the civil war began he and his two brothers agreed between them that, being Russians, they would not raise a hand against their own and would take no part in it.

In 1918 the Bolsheviks arrested him and sentenced him to be shot, and on the eve of the execution let him go home to say goodbye to his family; he was about to go back to the prison in the morning when the whites drove them out of the town, and the sentence lapsed of itself. He was arrested again in the summer of 1919 and held at Saratov, and said afterwards that he had never prayed so well as in a prison where death knocks at the door at night and nobody knows whose turn it is. Arrested a third time in the summer of 1923, he was asked by his interrogator how he would organise a cattle farm, answered in full detail, and was told: I love men like you — only of course we will never let you run anything.

The calendar prints a good deal of what he wrote. To his son, on his coming of age in 1922, he wished the strength to go through his earthly road honourably and save his soul, and set down what he had come to by then: that only a man who believes deeply and sincerely can really be free, and that dependence on God is the one dependence that does not humiliate a man or turn him into a wretched slave but lifts him up. Of his own refusal to take a post he wrote that he could not serve the plunderers of Russia and of the soul of the Russian people, and would sooner starve; of the country, that none of what was said about its flourishing was true; of the pressure on the Church, that it was rising again, and that if it reached his own district he would be the first to go and was not in the least afraid.

In 1928 he was arrested and held at Saratov, and on the close of the investigation was forbidden to live in six large cities; he settled at Syzran, near his own country, a widower by then, with his daughters following him into exile. Hardship and heavy work had told on him, the tuberculosis in his lungs flared up early in 1931, and on 22 February he was moved to the hospital block of the Syzran prison. His daughters, hearing how ill he was, asked for a visit and were told to come the next day; when they came they were told that their father had been buried the day before, and where was refused them. He died in the prison hospital at half past twelve on 1 April 1931, and was buried in his absence from the cathedral of Syzran. He was numbered among the New Martyrs and Confessors of Russia at the Jubilee Council of Bishops in August 2000.
