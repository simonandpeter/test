# Matrona of Thessalonica

Matrona was a servant in the house of a Jewish woman named Pautila, the wife of the governor of Thessalonica. She had been taught the Christian faith from her youth, and her mistress kept forcing her towards the Jewish faith; and because she would not submit, Pautila tormented her cruelly and beat her often. Matrona bore it all readily for Christ, and went secretly to church.

One day Pautila learned that Matrona had been at the Christian church, and asked her why she had not gone to their synagogue. The blessed Matrona answered boldly that since God is in the Christian church, and has departed from the Jewish synagogue, she would go to the church and not to the synagogue. At this her mistress grew angrier still, beat her without sparing, and shut her up bound in a dark room; but the next day the saint was found loosed from her bonds by the power of God, glorifying Christ.

Her mistress beat her again with raw sinews until the blood ran, bound her more tightly, shut her in that room again and sealed the door, so that no one should come in to ease her. The saint stayed four days in that prison without food or drink, God strengthening her. Then Pautila broke the seal and opened the door and found her loosed from her bonds again and standing at prayer; and boiling with anger she beat her with thick sticks and shut her, barely breathing, in the same prison, where the saint ended her life and gave up her soul to God.

That wicked woman threw her body down from the wall, for those houses stood high. The Christians took up the much-tormented body of the martyr Matrona and buried it with honour; and afterwards Alexander, bishop of Thessalonica, built a church in the saint's name and laid her honoured relics in it. As for Pautila, the judgement of God overtook her quickly and as she deserved: from the height of that same wall from which she had thrown down Matrona's body she slipped and fell, and died badly, and so gave up her wretched soul.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and tells the passion at length, with names the Romanian line does not carry. saint.gr makes her the servant of a rich and noble Jewish woman called Pantilla, or Pautilla, the wife of the commander of the garrison of Thessalonica. Every day she went with her mistress to the city's synagogue, and every day she slipped away from it to a Christian church to pray. At one of the Jewish feasts, when they used to eat bitter herbs and unleavened bread, she was late back from the church, and one of Pantilla's slaves denounced her: she was a Christian, and she was deceiving her mistress. Pantilla had her seized, bound and flogged, and Matrona said openly that she was a Christian, and that her mistress had power over her body and her life but could not turn her from what she believed.

She was chained, shut in a cell and the door sealed. Three days later Pantilla came herself to see whether she was alive, and found her loosed from her bonds and standing radiant and singing, without a mark of the beating on her. She had her bound and flogged again, and the martyr, astonished at the cruelty, asked her why she tormented her, and confessed Christ again. After three more days her mistress came back to the cell and saw the same thing: her bonds loosed, her face bright, after fourteen days of hunger. Then Pantilla, in a rage, had her tied to oaken stocks and tortured, and Matrona, worn out by the scourging and her body covered with marks, whispered a few words of prayer and gave up her spirit. Pantilla then ordered a man named Stratonicus to wrap the body in a hide and throw it outside the city walls.

Christians took the holy relic and buried it reverently near the Leophoros, that is the Egnatian Way; and after the persecutions ended Alexander, bishop of Thessalonica, brought the body inside the city and built a church to lay it in. There was also a monastery dedicated to her outside the walls of Thessalonica. Under the Frankish rule, the page adds, her body was carried to Barcelona and laid in a church there which was destroyed during the Second World War. It gives no year for any of it, and its second couplet fixes only the day: «Εἰκάδι ἑβδομάτῃ θάνε Ματρῶνα ἑνὶ εἱρκτῇ» — on the twenty-seventh, Matrona died in prison.
