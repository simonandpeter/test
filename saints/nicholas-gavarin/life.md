# Nicholas (Gavarin)

Nicholas was born on 23 December 1870 in the town of Jakobstadt in the Courland province, the son of a clergyman, John Gavarin. He finished the Riga theological seminary in 1893 in the first class, read one year at a theological academy, and was ordained priest to a church in the town of Grodno. In 1915, when the war moved the front, the clergy of that church were evacuated to Moscow, and he went to serve in the church of Saint [Nicholas](/saints/nicholas-of-myra) na Shchepakh, near Smolensk Square.

A decade after the revolution, in 1930, the authorities began to put out of Moscow those they held to be socially alien, and the clergy came first among them. Having lost his place to live he went first to the village of Kuntsevo and then settled in the settlement of Nemchinovka outside Moscow, and served in the church of the Nativity of Christ there.

That church had come into being in 1918, when the congregation asked a local householder named Nemchinov to give his summer dacha for a church. He agreed on condition that they repair it themselves so that it could serve; the local authorities permitted an Orthodox house of prayer, and the faithful set to work. The dacha had been built sixty years before, with no foundation, a single floor, single doors and windows, and by then was badly decayed. They overhauled it, replaced the rotted right-hand corner of the building entirely, added a belfry and put up a small house beside it for the priest. Even so the temperature inside in the winter months never rose above three degrees.

At the end of 1934 a new chairman of the Nemchinovka settlement council forbade parish meetings and the ringing of bells, and then had the bells themselves taken down. A little later he resolved to close the church and give the building over to physical-culture classes, pleading that the parishioners were few. There were then some seven hundred houses in the settlement and eight thousand inhabitants, of whom two thousand signed as believers under a protest against the closing.

In April 1935 an archbishop living in retirement at Nemchinovka, who had spent half a century as a missionary among the nomads of the mountain Altai and had been a fellow-labourer of the metropolitan [Macarius](/saints/macarius-metropolitan-of-moscow), petitioned Lenin's sister on the parish's behalf. He wrote that he lived there as a private person in the house and at the charge of his son, an archpriest; that he had the blessing of the Patriarchal Synod to serve in the Nemchinovka church and did serve the Liturgy there on feasts, and was ready to lay his bones at its foot; and that the decision to close it rested on a claim that the parish numbered no more than twenty souls, where in fact it numbered two thousand. No answer came, to that letter or to the several petitions the community sent after it, and all that while the services went on.

On 20 September 1935 the final order to close the church was given. The faithful tried for three days to have it reversed, adding that if the church could not be left to them they asked leave to rent a barn for the services, which they had already found; the authorities held to it that the church was to be shut and no other place allowed. The whole clergy of the church, the archpriest, Father Nicholas and the deacon who served with them, had to move to the church of Saint Nicholas in the village of Romashkovo.

After the secret directive of 1937 on mass repression, the investigator of the Kuntsevo section of the NKVD drew up lists of those he meant to arrest and lists of the «witnesses» who could sign false evidence, and drew diagrams of which witness was to confirm which episode of a defendant's anti-Soviet activity; and since the stock of such episodes was small, they were carefully shuffled to give an appearance of credibility. In August 1937 he called in a man of Kuntsevo and proposed that he give evidence against Father Nicholas. The man did not know the priest and refused the part, and the investigator then lectured him at length on the political situation and on the tasks of the party, one of the first of which, he said, was to put every priest in prison — and what you say here nobody will know of, and you will not be called to court.

He handed the man blank interrogation forms to fill in at home. The man took them and did not fill them in, not knowing what to put and fearing the responsibility; the investigator called twice at his flat, and he hid from him. Called in again with a friend, he was given the forms once more and wrote nothing, and when the investigator wrote out a summary and had him copy it, the result did not please him: he tore it up, filled the form in himself, and required a signature, which the man at last gave without reading. The friend was then brought in and shown the same prepared protocol, and when he hesitated the investigator pointed to the man who had just signed, and so got his signature too, also unread. Later he called at the friend's house and had him sign three more blank forms. Both false witnesses met the investigator in the street that autumn and were given spirits in payment for their services; and for the sake of efficiency the investigator took in the chairman of a village council, who knew the local people, to write protocols in the names of witnesses alongside him. Between them the two produced more than fifty such protocols in a short time.

On that evidence Father Nicholas was arrested on 29 August 1937 and shut in the Taganka prison in Moscow. Asked whether he had talked politics with the deacon, he said they had talked of church affairs, of how badly the clergy now lived under the Soviet power, of the news in the papers and of the war in Spain, doubting that the Spanish government forces would beat the fascists, who were strong in arms. Asked whether that meant he took the fascists' side, he said he could not say that: they had thought the fascist troops strong in equipment and organisation, and helped besides by such strong states as Germany and Italy. Asked to tell of his anti-Soviet conversations, he said they had talked of the Soviet power doing wrong in closing churches, persecuting churchmen and crushing them with taxes; asked for more detail, he said he could not, he had forgotten.

Asked his attitude to the Soviet power, he said it was negative, and that he could not be reconciled to it for the oppressions they endured. Told that the investigation had established he belonged to a counter-revolutionary terrorist and insurgent group and had agitated against the government and the leaders of the party, he answered that he had been discontented with the Soviet power over its attitude to religion and to the clergy, but that he had belonged to no counter-revolutionary group. On 15 September 1937 an NKVD troika sentenced him to ten years in a corrective labour camp and he was sent to Ukhtpechlag.

Some of the relatives of those condemned with him complained of the sentence and argued their innocence; a fresh investigation was begun and the witnesses were questioned again, and after holding at first to what they had said, for fear of answering for perjury, they admitted at last that they had slandered the men. The new inquiry of 1939 had to find that all those sentenced in the case had been sentenced unjustly. But since before the godless believing men, and clergy above all, remained the same enemies of the communist power, the sentence against them was not set aside. Father Nicholas Gavarin died in Ukhtpechlag on 24 April 1938 and was buried in a grave nobody marked.

The Sretensky calendar's own line gives none of this: it reads «Сщмч. Николая пресвитера (1938).», a baptismal name, a rank and a year, and the index entry behind it adds the surname, the rank of priest, and one other day, the synaxis of the new martyrs.
