# Ephrem the Syrian

Ephrem rose, the Romanian life says, out of the land of the Syrians. From childhood he chose the good and kept away from the harmful talk of boys his age; he read without ceasing, and above all the holy books, and their study was sweeter to him than any pleasure, so that he said with the prophet: how sweet are thy words to my throat, more than honey. Fasting, vigil, sleeping on the ground, meekness and the owning of nothing went with him all his life, and the gift of teaching was given him besides.

Leaving his own country as Abraham did, he came to the city of Edessa to venerate the relics kept there and to find some man of discourse from whom he might take a pattern of life. At the gate a harlot met him instead, and the two stood looking at each other; and when he reproached her for staring at him she answered that it was fitting for her to look at him, since she was taken out of him and out of his side, but that he should look not at her but at the earth he was taken from. He thanked the woman for the profit of her words, and God with her.

At Edessa another such woman, living near him, was made an instrument of the evil one and pressed him openly. He told her he would sleep with her only in the middle of the city; and when she asked whether he had no shame before the men who would see them, he answered that if she judged the eyes of men able to shame a soul out of a wicked deed, how much more should they fear the eyes of God, who sees all that is done in secret and in the open. She fell at his feet, repented, took him for her teacher, and he had her brought into a convent of women.

From there he was led to Caesarea of Cappadocia and saw [Basil the Great](/saints/basil-the-great), the voice of the Church and the spring of the dogmas, and praised him at length; for with the clear eye of the soul he had seen a dove bright as the sun's rays sitting on Basil's right shoulder and speaking in his ear. The teaching that came to Ephrem afterwards was such a flood that he once said to God: stay, O Master, the waves of thy grace.

Against Apollinaris he used a stratagem the life tells with relish. The heretic had left his books with a woman of his household; Ephrem came to her in the guise of one of that party, borrowed them, parted every leaf, smeared all of them with fish glue, pressed them together until no page could be opened, and gave them back. At the council Apollinaris called for his books, found them one solid block, and withdrew from the debate in confusion.

He ate ashes as his bread, mixed his drink with weeping, and was so shy of praise that he blushed and sweated and fell silent before those who praised him as another man does before his mockers. Of the poor he had great care, and having nothing of his own he opened the hearts and the treasuries of the rich with his speech, for it was not only his word that moved souls to mercy but the sight of him and his gentleness.

Dying, he charged those about him not to sing over Ephrem, not to make a speech of praise for him, not to bury him in a costly garment and not to give his body a grave apart, for he had promised God to lie among strangers, being himself a stranger and a sojourner as all his fathers were. One of the more distinguished of them had a costly cloth ready all the same and was seized by the devil at the bedside; Ephrem, understanding that the sickness was the fruit of the fault, took the man's confession, freed him by prayer and the laying on of hands, and bound him to keep the command. So, the life says, he passed to the heavenly dwellings. It gives no year for any of this.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same twenty-eighth of January, and saint.gr sets his years and his places. He was born at Nisibis in Mesopotamia, probably in 308 or earlier, and flourished under Constantine the Great, under Julian the Apostate and under Julian's successors. From a small age he was taught the faith by Jacob, bishop of his native city, who ordained him deacon; he refused any higher rank. It was he, the page says, who worsted the heretic Apollinarius in debate and brought many heretics back to the piety of their fathers. When by the treaty of 363 Nisibis was handed over to the Persians, he left his homeland for Edessa and practised asceticism on a mountain beside it; in 370 he visited Basil the Great at Caesarea in Cappadocia, and a little later the fathers and ascetics of Egypt. He fell asleep in peace in 373, and his synaxis was kept at the martyrion of Saint Akylina in the quarter of Philoxenos, near the market. Portions of his relic are at the Great Lavra on the Holy Mountain, at Mega Spilaio of Kalavryta, and at the Lavra of Saint Alexander Nevsky in Saint Petersburg.

The Russian calendar keeps him on the same menologion day and prints an interval rather than a year: «Прп. Ефрема Сирина (373-379)». Its troparion for him says what his tears did to the desert and calls him a lamp to the whole world, and its kontakion has him weeping for the hour of the Judgement and rousing the slothful to repentance, a teacher active in deeds.
