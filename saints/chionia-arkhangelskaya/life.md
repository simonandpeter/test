# Chionia (Arkhangelskaya)

Chionia Ivanovna was born on 8 April 1883 at Novy Kopyl in the Voronezh province, the daughter of a priest named John Dmitriev, and she married [Tikhon (Arkhangelsky)](/saints/tikhon-arkhangelsky) when he came out of the seminary. Eighteen children were born to them between 1901 and 1923; nine lived. The calendar gives her the raising of them and says what she taught: to pray, and in every difficulty to turn to the one God. On every feast great and small the children went to church with her, and she accustomed them to fast by the Church's rule — which in the persecutions of the twenties meant fasts that ran into real hunger, the famine that had come over the whole country. In the fasts secular books were put away and only the law of God was read, and the children had to tell their father or mother what they had read; since there was little time free of work, they told it while working, in the garden or the field, or over the knitting of stockings and mittens.

It was she who decided the family's flight in 1928, when the church at Troekurovo was closed and the authorities moved to register her husband as a kulak: rather than wait to be thrown out of the house, pack what was needed and go to Lebedyan. He agreed with her.

Her own interrogation came in 1937, after he had been sentenced. She denied the statements against her and asked to be confronted with the witnesses — I am not afraid of a lie, she said, and my husband spoke against the sectarians himself. The official asked where her husband was, said that he was a counter-revolutionary and should be shot, and demanded to know when she and her family would get out of Kuyman, calling them parasites. She answered that she would go if ordered, and would have gone long since if the orphans had not tied her hands; that her daughter had sold her little house and they were living off it; and then, to the threat of a camp: «Воля ваша. А я жизнь жила, грешила и должна понести наказание за грехи» — as you please; I have lived my life and sinned, and I must bear the punishment for my sins. The official shouted enemy, enemy, a real enemy, told the secretary to write the record, and had her taken back under lock.

Her letter out of prison is in the calendar's life of them, and it is the most ordinary thing imaginable. Get out of Kuyman quickly; hope and pray, God is not without mercy and nowhere leaves his servants without help; pray that he strengthen his servants; thank you for your labours, forgive me. Then, to a daughter: what shall I leave you for a keepsake, I do not know myself — take grandfather's belt for your dressing gown, for the trimming, and whatever else you find. Do not forget God; have the child baptised, by any old woman or yourself if there is nobody, get holy water, or better, Sofya Ivanovna will baptise it at her house, which is what her father always told the grandmother to do; do not leave it unbaptised.

On 31 December 1937 the NKVD troika sentenced her to eight years in the corrective-labour camps, and she was sent to serve it in the prison at Shatsk in the Ryazan region. On 20 May 1938 the prison doctors drew up a report on her health and proposed releasing her, the law allowing it, since she could not manage without help from others. The NKVD representative required that early release not be considered, on account of her sharp statements about the Soviet power.

She was let out at the end of 1944, when her illness was plainly going to kill her. She stayed first with her daughter Julia at Michurinsk, and when another daughter, Vera, arrived she asked to be taken nearer the graves of her own people. They set out on a foul November day and barely arrived, getting across the rotten planks of a bridge by a miracle and nearly going into a deep ravine with horse and cart. She settled at the hamlet of Krivushka, near the village of Tyutchevo, in a small hut her daughter Irina had bought for two pairs of galoshes. By the time she reached the house she was altogether ill and hardly rose from the bed again — and took in sewing even so. She was paid in food, gave part of it to her daughters and kept part by for her own memorial meal, prayed, and made everything ready for her death so as to burden nobody if she could help it. In the last weeks the illness let her eat nothing at all. She died in December, and they buried her in the local cemetery on 22 December 1945.
