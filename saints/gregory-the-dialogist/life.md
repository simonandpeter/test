# Gregory the Dialogist

Gregory the Dialogist, pope of Rome — so called for the beauty of his words — was born in old Rome, of a father named Gordian and of the blessed Silvia his mother, both of good family, honoured and wealthy senators. His house, the life says, was honoured less for its senators than for the persons in it who pleased God: the blessed Felix, third of that name, pope of Rome, was his forebear; Saint Tarsilla, who at her end saw the Lord coming to her, and the blessed Emiliana, who with Tarsilla was found worthy of the everlasting life, were his aunts; and Silvia his mother is honoured among the saints in the Church of Rome.

From his youth he gave himself to virtue and to the pleasing of God, and from his childhood to the learning of books, becoming a chosen philosopher and a man of eloquence, full of understanding and judgement; and for that he was chosen to office in the courts. But his mind and his unceasing desire were not upon worldly things but upon spiritual, and he longed to be a monk. While his parents were alive he put the tonsure off until the fitting time; and though he lived in the world he was a monk in will, in a pure life of virginity.

When his father died he began to divide the great wealth left him in alms and in the building of holy houses. In Sicily he built six monasteries and furnished them with everything they needed, and a seventh in Rome, within the city, in honour of the Apostle Andrew. His own house, which stood near the church of the martyrs Peter and Paul by the hill called Scaurus, he turned into a monastery; and there he laid aside his worldly clothes woven with gold, together with the hair of his tonsure, put on the woollen habit of the monastic order, and was guided by the practised elders Maximian and Hilarion.

Not long after, he was made abbot of that house. His mother Silvia lived near the gate of the Apostle Paul, serving the Lord in her widowhood with fasting and prayers, in the house of the Lord day and night; her food was raw garden herbs, of which she sent some every day to her son. She was very merciful to the poor and the destitute and gave away all she had, to the end; and her son was so merciful that he did not spare even the last thing he had, but gave it at once to whoever asked.

The life tells how, as he sat in his cell writing books, a poor man came to him three times in one day — an angel of the Lord in a poor man's shape — saying that he had been the master of a ship and had lost not only his own goods but other men's. Twice Gregory had six gold pieces given to him; and when he came the third time and the brother who served said there was not a coin left in the store, Gregory told him to give the man clothing or some vessel. There was nothing left but the silver dish in which his mother sent him his lentils.

Another passage tells of a woman who doubted at communion, and how at Gregory's prayer the bread was changed into bloody human flesh before her and before everyone in the church, so that they were confirmed in the faith that under the form of bread is the true Body of Christ and under the form of wine his true Blood; and how at his prayer again it took back the look of bread, and she communicated in fear and without doubting.

He adorned the Church of Christ not only with a life like the angels' and with wonders but with many writings needful to the right faith; and when he wrote, the Holy Spirit appeared in the shape of a dove flying above him, which his archdeacon Peter, a man of virtue, was often found worthy to see. His writings, the life says, are in four books, full of profit, holding short accounts of the lives of the saints who struggled in the land of Italy and much teaching useful to the soul. He shepherded the Church of God thirteen years, six months and ten days, and passed to the Lord in the reign of Phocas the Tyrant (602–610).

It is written of him also that by his prayers he delivered Trajan, emperor of Rome (98–117), who was an idolater, from everlasting torments many years after his death. John of Damascus, in his word for the departed, tells that Gregory, going once over the stone bridge, stopped there on purpose and prayed to the Lord who loves souls for the forgiveness of the emperor Trajan's sins, and at once heard a voice from God saying that his prayer was heard and pardon given to Trajan, but that he should make no more prayers for pagans. doxologia prints no text of a hymn for him, only a sung recording.
