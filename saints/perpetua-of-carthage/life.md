# Perpetua of Carthage

The acts of the martyrdom of Perpetua and [Felicitas](/saints/felicitas-of-carthage), who suffered for Christ together with four other young Christians under the emperor Septimius Severus, are among the fullest and most precious that Christian antiquity has handed down in their original form, in Latin. They were written by an eyewitness, from the account Perpetua left herself and from the vision of the martyr Saturus, who suffered with her; the Romanian page says it seems to have been Tertullian who composed the opening and the close. At the beginning of the year 203 the movement of the fanatics against the Christians was growing in Carthage, in Africa, after Septimius Severus issued an edict forbidding Christian proselytism.

Vibia Perpetua was arrested: a Roman matron of twenty-two, instructed and well educated, for besides Latin she spoke and wrote Greek. She belonged to a well-off family of the town of Thuburbo Minus, today Tebourba, more than forty kilometres from Carthage; she was married and had a small child at the breast. Her father was a fanatical pagan and her mother half a Christian, and she had two brothers, one a catechumen and the other a boy who had died of a cancer of the face. At the moment of her arrest she was herself only a catechumen, not yet baptised.

Arrested with her were two young men of free but modest condition, Saturninus and Secundulus, Felicitas, Perpetua's slave, and the slave Revocatus, all of them catechumens; later their catechist Saturus gave himself up to the authorities of his own accord, so that there were two Christian women and four men. Their names have come down also from the inscription on their tombstone, found in 1907 by the French archaeologist Delattre in the Basilica majorum, which was raised over their graves.

The municipal authorities of Thuburbo, wishing to be rid of any responsibility, sent the two young women and the four young men to Carthage, where they were shut in a dark and horrible prison, probably next to the proconsul's palace on the slope of the hill of Byrsa. The Christian community of Carthage came to their help at once through the deacons Tertius and Pomponius, who paid the head of the prison to ease their condition. So Perpetua could receive her father, who tried in vain to bend her to deny Christ, and her mother and brother, and her child to nurse, and her relatives and friends; and because she could nurse the child the prison seemed to her "like a palace".

In prison she had the first of her visions: she fought the devil in the shape of a dragon, overcame him, and entered paradise in victory, where she saw a tall man with white hair in a shepherd's garment seated on a throne, who gave her communion. After some days they were taken to the forum of Carthage to be judged by the procurator Hilarianus. All of them refused to abjure the Christian faith, and Perpetua did not yield even to her father's tearful pleading. Hilarianus sentenced them to death by the beasts on the birthday of the Caesar — Geta, son of Septimius Severus — which falls on the seventh of March in the year 203.

Waiting for the sentence, she had two more visions: in one her brother Dinocrates, dead as a child, appeared to her beside a basin of water from which he could drink and be refreshed, glad and healed of his disease; in the other she fought an ugly Egyptian, a figure of the devil, and crushed his head with her heel. Saturus too had a vision, of the martyrs carried by four angels into paradise. Secundulus died beforehand in the prison.

On the eve of their suffering, while the martyrs were at their last supper, the agape, the pagans gathered to stare at them, and Saturus rebuked them: "Is tomorrow not enough for you? Today friends, tomorrow enemies! But fix our faces well in your minds, so that you may know us again on that day, the day of judgement." They drew back in fear, and many believed in Christ. The next day the martyrs, two women and three young men, suffered in the amphitheatre of Carthage before a crowded house. Saturninus and Revocatus were set upon by a leopard and a bear, Saturus by a leopard, and Perpetua and Felicitas by a wild cow.

After the beasts the martyrs were brought into the middle of the amphitheatre, where their heads were struck off; and Perpetua, seeing the gladiator hesitate, herself guided his trembling hand to her throat. Later a great basilica, the Basilica majorum, was raised at Carthage over the graves of the five martyrs.
