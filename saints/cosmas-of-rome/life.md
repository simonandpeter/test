# Cosmas of Rome

Cosmas and his brother [Damian](/saints/damian-of-rome) were born of one father and one mother in old Rome and brought up in the Christian faith. They learned the craft of medicine and healed every sickness by their practice, having the grace of the one God to help them in everything; and whatever suffering people or beasts they laid their hands upon were at once made perfectly whole.

They took no payment from anyone for their healing, and so were called the physicians without fee. The only payment they asked of those they healed, and it was a very dear one, was that they should believe in Christ. Not in Rome alone but going through the towns and villages round about, they healed the sick and turned many to Christ. They had much property inherited from their forefathers and their parents; they sold it and shared it out among the poor and the needy, fed the hungry and clothed the naked.

When they healed the sick they would say to them: We only lay our hands upon you, and by our own strength we can do nothing; it is the almighty power of Christ, the one true God, that works it all, and if you believe in Him without doubting you will at once be well. And believing, those people were healed; so that every day many turned from the ungodliness of idol-worship and came near to Christ.

They lived apart in a village within the borders of Rome, where their parents' property was, and from there they gave light to all round about by the holy faith. The devil could not bear a life so bright with good works, and stirred up certain of their own servants to go to the emperor and slander the innocent men. Carinus was then reigning in Rome; he listened to the slanderers and sent soldiers to that village to bring the physicians before his judgement. The faithful gathered and begged the saints to hide until the imperial anger passed, but they would not, and went out to the soldiers of their own will.

At Rome the emperor himself was healed by them, and rising up he lifted his eyes and his hands to heaven and gave thanks to God before all the people, saying: Blessed are You, Christ, the true God, who have brought me out of darkness into light through these Your holy servants. He honoured the two physicians and let them go in peace, and they returned to their village, where the people came out to meet them with joy; and they went about the towns and villages again as they were used to do.

The envious one, unable to destroy them by his first device, found another. There was in those parts a famous physician, the man from whom the saints had first learned their craft, and the enemy stirred him up to hate them, for he could not bear their good name. He called them to him deceitfully and took them up a mountain as though to gather herbs for medicines, with the thought of Cain in his heart; and having led them far off he set each of them to gather apart. Then he fell upon the one and killed him with stones, and afterwards killed the other in the same way, and took their bodies and hid them by a watercourse that was there. The doxologia page names the emperor Carinus and gives no year; saint.gr's heading does give one, 287, and it is recorded here.

The Greek calendar keeps the two unmercenaries on the same 1 July, and saint.gr tells their story in a shorter Greek form. They were brothers and physicians by trade under the Roman emperor Carinus, healing all who had need and taking no money, asking only that the healed should believe in Christ. Well-wishers denounced them to the emperor as working their cures by magic; rather than let others be taken in their place they came before him of their own accord.

When Carinus was interrogating them his face was twisted round towards his own back. They healed it at once by prayer to Christ, everyone standing there believed, and the emperor sent them home to their kinsfolk with great honours. Long afterwards their own teacher in medicine, envious of their fame, took them up a mountain as though to gather herbs and killed them there with stones. Their page's distich turns on the stones: «Βολαῖς ἀδελφοὺς οὐ διέσπων οἱ λίθοι, / Ὡς εἰς ἕν, ἄμφω συμπεπηγότας λίθον» — the stones for all their throwing did not part the brothers, fixed as both were upon one rock.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two brothers on the Julian 1 July, the same menologion day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep them on, in one entry under one heading, and it gives a year three years off the Greek page’s: they suffered honourably for the faith of Christ, it says, in the year 284, where saint.gr’s heading says 287. The folder’s date is widened to hold both books rather than deciding between them. It has them Romans by birth, baptised as children and brought up in the Christian spirit, with the grace of God to heal men and beasts of every sickness and torment, and that usually by the laying on of hands; they asked no reward, only that the sick should believe in Christ the Lord, and the great estate they inherited they gave away in alms to the poor and the distressed.

Two things in that entry are new and one of them is a disagreement. The new thing is what they said to the emperor. They did not merely refuse his order to deny Christ and sacrifice to the idols; they counselled him to turn from dead idols himself and own the one true God. Our God is not made, they told him, but is himself the Maker of all, and your gods are men’s inventions and the work of craftsmen’s hands; and if there were no craftsmen to make you gods, you would have nobody to bow down to.

The disagreement is how they came before him at all. The other two books have them come of their own will, refusing to hide so that nobody should be taken in their place; this book has the persecutors of Christianity bring them fettered in chains, and after a long interrogation and the wonder worked on the emperor himself, whose grievous illness it does not name, he confessed his own faith in Christ and let the holy brothers go in peace. Both readings are written here and neither is chosen. The end agrees with the other books: a physician who had once been their teacher envied their glory, took them into the mountain under pretext of gathering healing herbs, and stoned them to death.

The Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery keeps the two of them on 1 июля ст. ст. — «Бессребреников Космы и Дамиана , в Риме пострадавших (284)» — on the same menologion day as the three calendars this folder already carries, with the earlier of its two years and with Rome named in the line itself. The day page prints a troparion and a kontakion for the pair, both of which address Cosmas and [Damian of Rome](/saints/damian-of-rome) together by name.
