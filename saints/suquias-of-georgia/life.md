# Suquias, with nineteen martyrs

saint.gr gives him one sentence. The holy martyr Suquias and the Christians who were martyred together with him, it says, contended in the second century in Georgia. The day's line numbers his companions — «Άγιος Σούκιος ο Μάρτυρας και οι συν αυτώ δέκα εννέα Μάρτυρες», Suquias the martyr and the nineteen martyrs with him — and the page's own heading sets them down as the holy nineteen martyrs; but it names not one of them, so the nineteen have no folder of their own here.

The page says nothing else: no city or district in Georgia, no persecutor, no manner of death, and no year within the century it gives. It prints no couplet and no hymn, and the only other thing on it is a picture of the saint with the company.

**The Russian calendar is the first source read for this folder to tell the story at all, and the first to name the company.** Sukhiy and his sixteen companions were Georgians, it says, and noblemen at the court of the ruler of Albania — the Caucasian Albania, a state on the territory of present-day Azerbaijan. Escorting Satenik, that ruler's daughter and the wife of the Armenian king Artaxar, whose reign the page dates 88 to 123, they came to Artashat, the old capital of Armenia, which the Romans destroyed later, in 163. A Greek Christian named Chrysos was preaching there, a man the page says had been taught and ordained priest by one of the apostles. The noblemen believed, resolved to give their whole lives to God, and all seventeen followed Chrysos into Mesopotamia; and at their baptism in the waters of the Euphrates they were counted worthy to see the Lord of Glory.

At the place of the baptism they raised a cross and called it the Cross of the Annunciation, and there the priest gave them all new names — to the eldest, Sukhiy in place of Bagadras, and to his companions the sixteen the calendar prints as «Андрей, Анастасий, Талале, Феодорит, Ивхирион, Иордан, Кондрат, Лукиан, Мимненос, Нерангиос, Полиевкт, Иаков, Фока, Доментиан, Виктор и Зосима». They are left in the calendar's own script here, because no folder holds any of them and an English form for a man this corpus does not keep would be a reading nothing rests on. After Chrysos died a martyr Sukhiy became the brotherhood's spiritual guide, and they all moved into wild country on Mount Sukaketi, near the mountain village of Bagrevandi, where the former noblemen lived under the strictest discipline, with the thin mountain plants for food and cold spring water for drink.

The new ruler of pagan Albania, Datianos, heard that his former noblemen had become Christians and settled in prayerful solitude, and sent a man of his household, Barnapas, with a company of soldiers to argue them back to the court and the old religion. Barnapas found them, and they held to their vow and refused everything he offered. At his order they were nailed to the ground in the form of a cross and burned; and as he died the one the calendar names Кондрат began the twenty-first psalm, and the other martyrs took it up after him. Their bodies were then cut to pieces and scattered over the whole of Mount Sukaketi, from which the martyrs got the name Mesukevian — more correctly Sukaketian.

The holy remains lay unburied and incorrupt until the fourth century, when local Christians laid them in coffins and gave them to the earth, the martyrs' names having been found cut into the rock. Afterwards [Gregory the Illuminator](/saints/gregory-the-illuminator), the enlightener of Armenia, raised a church on that place and founded a monastery there, and a healing spring opened afterwards. The sixteen names above are printed as the calendar prints them, in its own script, the way this corpus printed the forty of Sebaste in the calendar's that named them; none of the sixteen has a folder here, because the Greek page this folder was first written from numbers the company at nineteen and names nobody, and sixteen named men cannot be fitted to nineteen unnamed ones by a reader.
