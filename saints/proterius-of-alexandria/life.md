# Proterius of Alexandria

When Dioscorus the heretic took the patriarchal throne at Alexandria after the death of Cyril, and the whole of Egypt was filled with his heresy, there was in that city a right-believing priest, unconquered in the Christian dogmas, named Proterius, holy in his life and full of the wisdom God breathes. Seeing many turning aside to the patriarch's heresy, which alleged one nature in Christ, he set himself hard against it with words nobody could answer.

Dioscorus was vexed at him, and tried to draw him into his own bad communion by flattery, honouring him with the office of protopresbyter; but he gained nothing, for Proterius was firm in the faith, having learned right belief well from the most holy Cyril, who had been patriarch of Alexandria before. Then, in the reign of Marcian, the fourth council of the holy fathers of the whole world met at Chalcedon, where Dioscorus was rebuked for his heresy and condemned to be put out of the patriarchal rank and cut off from the Church. He was banished to Gangra, a city in the country of Paphlagonia, and died there after a time.

At Alexandria the believing bishops, the clergy and the right-believing people chose Proterius for the patriarchal throne, a man truly worthy of such a rank. But the common people of Alexandria, who had drunk Dioscorus's heresy, and some of the clergy who had been harmed by it, would not accept the election. He took the throne and began to glorify the right faith as the council at Chalcedon had confirmed it, and at once there was uproar: certain clerics, chief among them the priest Timothy surnamed Aelurus and the deacon Peter Mongus, both ordained by Dioscorus and full of his heresy, broke away from the assembly of the Church, would not communicate with Proterius, and stirred the people to riot.

So the Church of Alexandria was deeply divided, and the end of it was the killing of the innocent bishop of God. The afflicted bishops and clergy, and all who were right-believing, wrote in tears to the emperor Leo, who had been chosen after Marcian, and to the most holy Anatolius, patriarch of Constantinople, telling them everything that had been done and begging them to deliver the Church of Alexandria from such a tyranny.

The emperor and the patriarch grieved over the murder, and the emperor sent his officers at once with a large force and punished those who had broken the peace — some by cutting off their hands, some their tongues, others with chains and prisons, others with beatings and banishments. Timothy Aelurus, the false patriarch, with his bishops and clergy, was sent to spiritual judgement; and being judged by the right-believing bishops he was paid according to his works, put out not only of the episcopate but of Christendom, and exiled to Gangra, where Dioscorus his teacher had ended. In his place another Timothy, called Salophakiolos, a right-believing and wise man loved for his good ways, was chosen to the patriarchate of Alexandria, and so the Church of Christ in that city received peace and quiet. doxologia gives no year for any of it, and prints no hymn for him.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and sets him in the aftermath of Chalcedon. saint.gr says Proterius lived in the years of the sovereigns Marcian and Pulcheria, was a presbyter of the Church of Alexandria, and took part in the Fourth Ecumenical Council, which met at Constantinople in 451 and condemned the Monophysite patriarch of Alexandria, Dioscorus; after Dioscorus's deposition Proterius was elected patriarch, from 452 to 457, having distinguished himself at the council and stopped the mouths of the impious heretics. When he came back to Alexandria the followers of Eutyches and of Dioscorus made risings and ruptures and blocked the grain from coming down to Alexandria by way of Pelousion, meaning to starve the city and turn it against him; but the emperor Marcian, at the saint's request, ordered the grain through, and the city was saved from famine.

After Marcian's death the heretics grew bold and fell back on devilish contrivances to bring their impious plans off — an account the page runs on with at length, further than this reading followed. What it prints of the end is that they dragged his body through the streets of the city, gave it at last to the animals and burnt what was left; and that the new patriarch dared all of this in Holy Week without its hindering him from serving the services of the Lord's Passion. When Marcian's successor, the emperor Leo the Great the Thracian, learnt what had happened, he ordered Timothy Ailouros tried canonically and exiled to Gangra, and everyone who had taken part in Proterius's murder was punished as well; in the deposed Timothy's place the Orthodox Timothy Salophakiolos was elected, from 460 to 482, and Leo imposed the decisions of the Fourth Ecumenical Council, drove out the Monophysite bishops of Alexandria and Antioch and appointed Orthodox men in their place. So, the page ends, the saint lived and was martyred, and his memory flowers in the life of the saints of the Church. Its couplet has Proterius slaughtered with the reeds, being himself a swift writer's reed against error.
