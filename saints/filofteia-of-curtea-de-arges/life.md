# Filofteia of Curtea de Argeș

Filofteia was born in the great city of Tărnovo, of Christian parents, Bulgarians by descent and ploughmen by trade. Her mother lived only a little while after her birth — long enough, doxologia's life says, to have taught her daughter the working of the virtues and the Christian faith, and to have printed it deep in a heart still soft as wax. Left motherless, the girl would have nothing to do with childish games or worldly songs, with costly clothes or plaited hair or painted faces, but began while still a child to thin her body with fasting and to stand at the church services with reverence.

Hearing the Gospel read — «Fericiți cei milostivi, că aceia se vor milui» — she joined almsgiving to her virginity, and the life is built on the two together: virginity alone, it says at the outset, fills no vessel with oil and lights no lamp. Her father had married again, and the stepmother hated her and carried tales of her to him; so she bore beatings, revilings, blows and torments, and had, the life says, neither a day nor an hour free of them, temptations within and persecutions without.

When the sowing came her father went out to the field with the plough, and told his wife to cook and send his food out to him by Filofteia. The poor, knowing how merciful she was, met her on the road every day and asked alms, and having nothing else she fed them out of what she was carrying; so her father went hungry, and when he complained his wife told him to ask what his daughter did with the food on the way. He said nothing, but went out to the field and watched from a hiding place; and seeing her sharing the food out among the poor he was overcome with anger and threw the ploughman's hatchet he wore at his belt, and it struck her in the foot. At that moment she gave up her soul into the hands of God.

There was no persecution then, the life insists, and no tyrant and no torturer: she was killed in a time of peace, in a time of Christendom, and not by strangers but by the man who had fathered her and by her stepmother, both of them Christians; and by patience she was made a martyr not less than the old ones. When the archbishop and the great men of the city came out to carry her body in, the grave-diggers could not lift it or so much as stir it, and neither could the archbishop and the bishops with him; God had made the girl's small body heavy as an unmoved rock.

So they began to name places to her, and to beg her to say where she would go. They named Constantinople, and Sofia, and the monasteries of those mountains; then places beyond the Danube, Bucharest, Craiova, lesser market towns, monasteries in the towns and in the hills. She did not move until, in passing and without hope, they named the princely church in the town of Curtea de Argeș — and at that she became lighter than her own natural weight. Radu Voievod, who ruled in the Romanian Land and had built that church from its foundations — perhaps, the life says, the one surnamed Negru — was written to, and came out to the Danube with the clergy, the boyars and a multitude of people, with incense and candles, and received her relics and set them in the princely church. They have not been moved from that town since, through all the changes of times and rulers and risings.

The Greek calendar keeps her on 28 May rather than on the Romanian 7 December, and its account begins far to the east of Argeș. saint.gr says she was born at Molyvoto in Pamphylia in Asia Minor to the patrician John and to Irene, who brought her up as a Christian; that she made great progress in the study of the sacred letters and had learned the Scriptures by heart; and that from childhood she gave herself to the ascetic life. At fourteen she was obliged against her will to marry a youth of seventeen named Constantine, but she managed to persuade her husband that they should keep their purity within the marriage. She lost her mother at three years old and her father shortly after her wedding; her husband was ordained presbyter and died after six years of married life. She freed the household's slaves, distributed the family's wealth to the poor, to churches and to monasteries, and withdrew with one servant to an island in the lake near Molyvoto, where she lived by fasting, vigil and prayer, strengthening the Christians when idolatry and persecution came back, and receiving the gift of wonders. Four days before her repose she called the priests of the district and gave them her last charges, and she fell asleep in peace and was buried in the church of the Mother of God that was also known as Hagia Sophia.

The page then follows her relics, and it is by that road that the Greek reading meets this folder. They were carried from Eastern Thrace to Tarnovo, the capital of the second Bulgarian state, at the instance of the king Ioannikios or Kaloyan, whose reign it dates 1197 to 1207, escorted by a guard of honour and received by the archbishop of Tarnovo; many healings were worked on the way, among them the healing of the officer Theodore who led the escort, and the relics were laid in the church of the Mother of God called Temniska inside Tarnovo. After the Ottoman capture of Tarnovo in 1393 the Bulgarians of Vidin moved them to their own capital, two years after the fall, in 1395, and from that hour she became the protectress and intercessor of the faithful of the kingdom of Vidin. In 1396 Vidin fell too, and in the ruin of the conquest the traces of her relics were lost for a long time; the page says they are found again with certainty in the second half of the sixteenth century at Argeș in Ungro-Wallachia.
