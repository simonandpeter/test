# Macarius (Sharov)

Macarius — Matthew Terentievich Sharov — came of a rich family of the
townsman class at Efremov in Tula province; his mother was especially
devout, always went about with a prayer-rope in her hand and brought up
her children in the fear of God, and following his parents’ example
Matthew too was pious and humble and lived temperately, ate nothing of
meat or milk, read spiritual books, kept away from worldly vanity, went
diligently to church and prayed much at home. At twenty, in 1822, he
finally resolved to leave the world and enter the Glinsk hermitage under
the never-forgotten abba [Philaret](/saints/philaret-danilevsky), a man
known for the holiness of his life. He was set first to work on the
apiary, where the abbot often came, watched the newcomer and taught him
the monastic life like a father, and seeing his strong bent for it, his
capacity and his zeal in obedience, tonsured him on 16 December 1833 with
the name Macarius. He had watched over his soul as a novice, and as a
monk did so the more; on 23 October 1837 he was ordained hierodeacon, not
by his own will but by his abbot and guide’s wish, in 1839 hieromonk, and
grew more attentive to himself still. His cell was one small room, its
panes pasted over with paper so that his eye should not wander; a nun who
was his disciple describes it — one icon, of the Mother of God of the
Passion, a crucifix, a few books, a table, a chair, a plain wooden bench
with an old coat lying on it, and on the table one apple; he suffered no
ornament. From it he liked to walk into the forest with a spiritual book,
sit in a lonely place and read, or feed his soul with thought of God and
prayer, kneeling, his pure soul rising to God.

In 1844 he was made dean, though not long before he had been retired for
illness, and gave himself to the office without sparing himself, dividing
his love, as it were, in two equal parts, one for the abbot, whose orders
he held it a sacred duty of obedience to carry out, and one for the
brethren, among whom there were those who out of false shame, self-love,
self-pity or stubbornness refused a given obedience; for God’s sake alone
and for love and the wish of his neighbours’ salvation he bore much and
often got himself into trouble, sometimes doing the absent brother’s task
himself — kneading the bread at night, more than once, and other heavy
work — and covering the weak brethren’s weaknesses before the abbot with
his love, and suffering for it. He suffered much too from some of the
brethren over the visitors he received, women especially, and the offices
of dean and elder drew him from thought of God, so that he resolved to give
up both and work for the Lord alone in the solitude of his cell: in 1849,
in view of his illness and at his request, he was relieved of the deanery,
and two years later of his turn of serving. From then he left his cell
only for church and for rare lonely walks, oftener than not to the
cemetery, where much spoke to his humble soul; his many admirers, deprived
of his teaching, came to his cell and were refused. But soon he received
visitors again at a sign from above — I lay as I am now, weary and weak,
he told the abbot, and an angel of God appeared to me and said, receive
the people; now I cannot refuse and must do God’s will — and had the
abbot’s blessing to receive those who came; and a vision made him receive
women without confusion: he saw himself going to church with a multitude
of swallows flying round the monastery and settling on his hands and
shoulders, which he took in his hands and blew on; then coming back from
church carrying a cross on his head, while rooks on the roofs of the
monks’ cells cawed at him — the swallows, the women seeking his counsel,
whom he must not refuse; the cross, that this was no light labour; the
cawing rooks, the brethren who murmured at it. Wonderful was the elder’s
calm as he taught: on his bright face shone a pure joy, the reflection of
a pure heart, in his eye an angelic meekness, the expression of inner
peace — he was all love, one admirer said. With the same patience he heard
the absurd superstition of an ignorant man and the unbelief and mad
free-thinking of a learned one, the senseless complaint of a peasant
woman and the ingenious inquisitiveness of a lady, the artless story of a
simple man and the intricate phrase of the wise of this world; nothing
could disturb his Christian patience and his full peace of soul; all was
subdued to the deepest humility. He was a true teacher of moral theology
and of the spiritual work; healing the weak, he hardly prayed God to heal
his own weaknesses, never sparing himself for the saving of himself and
others and for God’s glory.

But the time came when the house of his body fell into ever greater
weakness, though the inner man grew without ceasing; receiving a
revelation of his approaching end, he wished, as provision for the journey
to live with the angels in heaven, to take the great angelic habit, and
was given the holy schema on 1 June 1863, keeping his name, renewed his
vows and redoubled his already great labours, standing on a crutch
through all the long services of Glinsk to the end despite grave illness.
Six months before his death, worn out, he shut himself in his cell and
received only monks, and preferred complete solitude, communicating more
often still, firmly believing in the soul’s near release from the bonds of
the flesh; a vision assured him that he would not be held at the fearful
toll-houses — I lay sick and sorrowing, he told the nun Agnia: Lord, I am
not ready, and the end of life draws near! and I see a white pillar, as if
papered, rising to the very sky, and on it a great space; it is good
there, I cannot tell you how; that place was shown me, and I heard a voice
that I would not have to pass the toll-houses — so that he awaited his end
eagerly and without fear, and could say with the apostle, to me to live is
Christ and to die is gain. Shortly before it he was carried at his own
wish to the brethren’s infirmary; his life went out like the quiet flicker
of a dying lamp; he stopped speaking and only by his eyes lifted upward
let it be understood that his praying spirit longed to depart and be with
Christ; and on 21 February 1864, in his sixty-third year, provided with
the Church’s Mysteries and mourned by those who honoured him, he quietly
and peacefully gave his soul to the Lord whom he had served zealously all
his days. On 23 February the abbot Innocent and a council of the
priest-monks sang the funeral and gave his much-laboured body to the
earth. The life is from the Glinsk Paterikon of the schema-archimandrite
John (Maslov); the Council of Bishops of 2017 entered him in the calendar
of the Russian Church, which keeps him on 9 September of the old style, 22
September, in the synaxis of the Glinsk fathers.
