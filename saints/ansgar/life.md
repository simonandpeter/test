# Ansgar

Ansgar, whom the Romanian calendar calls the Apostle of the North and the enlightener of Denmark, and who is also called Anskar or Oscar, was born at Corbie in Picardy, in northern France, in 801, a year after the imperial coronation of Charlemagne, of a family of Saxon emigrants. He lost his mother as a child and was brought up by the monks of the abbey of Corbie, where the learned abbot Adelard, the emperor's cousin, had the task of preparing young Saxons to evangelise their own countrymen.

He did very well at his studies and kept the monastic discipline with great zeal, and the monks reckoned that if he did not become a monk he could make a career among the imperial legates. While he was leaving boyhood the sudden death of Charlemagne shook him: he had seen the emperor in the splendour of his power, and the death of such a lord, his biographer Rimbert says, filled him with fear and dismay. He was only thirteen, but from that moment he began to aspire to give himself wholly to God, and as soon as he was grown he became a monk. Of him it was said that he was "a monk within and an apostle without".

For a time he taught in the monastery school at Corbie, and in 823 he became a preacher at New Corbie, Korvey on the Weser in north-western Germany. In 826 Harald, king of the Danes, came to Louis the Pious to ask help against the feuds that made peaceful government impossible, and his request was granted on condition that he receive Christianity; he was baptised with four hundred other men near Mainz and asked for missionaries to go back with him. Other missionaries had tried Denmark before and given up. The emperor asked abbot Wala of Korvey for "a man consecrated to God, ready to face so dangerous a journey for Christ", and the abbot offered Ansgar; of all the monks only one, Autbert, had the courage to follow him.

They entered Denmark and began to preach Christ, but after a year King Harald died and the two were driven out. Two years later Ansgar tried another road, accepting the invitation of King Björn of Sweden and taking with him Witmar, in the place of Autbert, who had died; and in 830 the first Christian church in those lands was founded at Birka, west of Stockholm. Seeing the work bear fruit, he judged that scattered missionaries were not enough and that a base was needed inside the empire on the border of those countries, and he laid the plan before the imperial court.

It was accepted, and he was named missionary bishop with his see at Hamburg. So as "not to run in vain" he went to Rome for the pope's approval, and Gregory IV not only confirmed the election but made him archbishop and his own legate for all the Scandinavian countries. From Hamburg he organised the Mission for the Christening of the North, and by this he became known as the Apostle of the North. He consecrated another monk, Gautbert, bishop, and sent him into Sweden, keeping for himself the harder Danish mission, and with his brethren he founded a monastery for the training of young men from those new lands.

Then in 845 the invasion of the Northmen destroyed all his works at Hamburg, the Danes revolted and the Swedes expelled Gautbert. Abandoned even by his monks, he did not lose heart: he went on supporting the Danish and Swedish Christians, accepted King Olaf's invitation and went to Sigtuna, where he left his friend and disciple Rimbert as bishop, and then went to Bremen as archbishop of Bremen-Hamburg to reorganise a new missionary base. Rimbert, who knew him closely, says he knew how to move within imperial politics without ever giving up the freedom of a man of the Church, and that he took time over every important decision and settled nothing without long thought.

He died at Bremen on 3 February 865, after a life given wholly to the preaching of the Gospel; he lived sixty-four years, of which thirty-four were spent as a missionary. Rimbert, his successor, who wrote the *Vita Ansgari*, observed that there are two kinds of martyrdom, the hidden one of the Church's peaceful times and the open one of persecution, and that Ansgar was ready for both and attained the first — and that a man consumed by the Lord's service through so many grave bodily sufferings and troubles of soul cannot be denied the honour of martyrdom. Pope Nicholas I, who had made him archbishop of Bremen, had also the joy of canonising him as apostle of the North. A statue stands to him at Hamburg and a stone cross at Birka.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and titles him «Απόστολος της Δανίας και της Σκανδιναβίας», apostle of Denmark and Scandinavia. Its own account differs from doxologia's in two places worth recording: it puts his birth in Picardy at the beginning of the ninth century rather than at Corbie in 801, and it makes him orphaned and brought up in the monastery of Corbie, where he became a monk and was charged to work as a missionary among the peoples who had not come to know Christ. The rest tallies: he preached in the Baltic lands, was elected archbishop of the new countries with his seat at Hamburg, had to leave it when the Normans took the city in 845 and travelled the northern lands for two years, and returned to a see now joined with Bremen by decision of the Synod of Mainz. saint.gr adds that he baptised Horik king of Denmark and his successor, who had been an idolater and had raised a persecution of the Christians.
