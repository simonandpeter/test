# Agatha of Catania

In the evil reign of Decius, when Quintianus had been set by him as governor over Sicily, a heathen command went out through all the lands that every Christian should be killed. At that time there was in the city of Panormus a very beautiful virgin named Agatha, born of well-born and wealthy parents. Hearing of that godless order for the killing of Christians, she was kindled with zeal for Christ her Lord, to whom she had made herself a bride by pure virginity; and passing over her inheritance, the honour of her good family, the wealth left her by her parents and all the glory of this world, she began to prepare herself beforehand for suffering.

Quintianus heard of her beauty, her birth and her riches, and was taken with an unclean desire for her, and thought how he might bring her, and her property with her, to his purpose. Learning that she believed in Christ, he at once sent soldiers from the city of Catania to Panormus to bring her to judgement as a Christian. When they came she asked them to wait a little, went into an inner room of her house, shut herself in, and lifting up her hands prayed that God would not let that wicked man, the servant of devils, defile the body in which she had lived until then with joy and honour. So praying, she went out of the city with the soldiers, some Christians she knew going with her.

At Catania she was given for a time into the hands of a woman named Aphrodisia and her daughters, who dressed her in costly clothes, set gifts and rich food before her and made dances and every worldly folly before her eyes; but she would not so much as look, and said that her mind and her thought were founded on a rock and could never be parted from the love of Christ: their flattering words were like the wind, the joys of the world like rain and their threats like torrents, which may beat on her house but cannot shake it, for it stands on the rock which is Christ. Aphrodisia went to Quintianus and told him it was easier to soften stone and turn iron into molten lead than to turn that girl from her Christ.

Then the governor had her brought before him and asked of what family she was. "I am born of a good family and have honoured and wealthy kindred." — "If you are nobly born, why do you wear a poor garment like a slave?" — "I am the slave of Christ, and therefore I wear the form of a slave." — "How can you be a slave, being free and the daughter of good parents?" — "This is our good birth and our freedom, to serve Christ." He threatened her with torments; she told him that his gods were not gods but devils, whose images he cast in bronze and marble and gilded their faces; and when he struck her on the face she asked him where his understanding was, since she had only wished him to be like his own god, and he was ashamed of them himself.

He had her put in a dark prison, to which she went as to a feast; and the next day, when she had answered him that her health was Christ, he had her hung up naked on a beam and beaten. Under the beating she said that his torments brought her gladness as a man is glad at finding great treasure, for as wheat cannot be gathered into the barn until it is cleansed of the chaff, so her soul could not enter paradise unless her body were first broken with torments. Then he had her breast torn with iron pincers and cut off; and she said to him: "Godless and inhuman persecutor, are you not ashamed to cut off a woman's breast, which you yourself sucked at your mother's? But I have another in my soul, which you cannot touch, for it has been consecrated to God from my youth."

They threw her back into the prison, and at midnight the Apostle Peter appeared to her in the form of an old and honourable man, carrying many medicines in his hands, with a beautiful youth going before him with a shining torch. He told her the tyrant had gained nothing by her wounds and that he had stood watching her in that hour and knew her breast could be healed. She answered that she had never used any medicine on her body and would not break a good custom kept from her youth; and when he said that he too was a Christian and hoped to heal her, she said that she was a virgin and he a man, and she would rather bear the pain of her wounds than uncover herself before a man's eyes — for she had her Lord Jesus Christ, who heals all things. Rejoicing at such faith, the apostle smiled a little and said: "He sent me to you, maiden, for I am His Apostle; be healed now" — and vanished. She looked at her body and saw all the wounds healed, and an unspeakable light filled the prison all that night, so that the guards fled in fear and left it unlocked. The other prisoners told her the doors were open and she should flee, but she answered that she would not be deprived of the martyr's crown nor bring the guards into danger.

On the fifth day the tyrant brought her out again and asked how long she would resist the imperial command. She answered that his words were empty and his emperor's order unjust, and that she offered a sacrifice of praise to Him who had healed her breast and made her body whole. He had her breast uncovered, saw it whole and sound as before, and asked who had healed her; she said, Jesus Christ, the Son of the living God. Then he ordered sharp potsherds and fire-heated nails to be strewn on the ground with burning coals poured over them, and she to be stretched on them and burned. As it was done, not only that place but the whole city was shaken, and the earth split and swallowed Vultia, the governor's favourite, and his friend Theophilus, on whose counsel he had done it. The citizens ran in fear to Quintianus's court crying out that he should torment the innocent maiden no longer, for the earthquake had come for her sake.

Fearing the earthquake and the tumult, he had her carried back to the prison; and there she lifted up her hands and gave thanks that she had been counted worthy to suffer for His holy name, and asked that she might now leave the world and pass to His great and rich mercy. So praying, she gave up her spirit into the hands of God. The citizens came in haste and took her holy body; and afterwards no imperial officer dared to trouble her kindred, her fame began to spread everywhere, and a church was built over her relics, and the garment she used to wear was laid on her tomb in memory of her humility.

A year after her end a great fire broke out of Mount Etna, near Catania, and came down like a river from the mouth of the mountain, roaring terribly, melting the stones like wax and hurling them from the height, so that all the people of Catania were in great fear for their city. Then not only the Christians but the unbelievers too ran to the church of the martyr Agatha, took her garment, and stood with it against the fire that was coming on the city; and the fire, as if ashamed of the garment of the holy martyr, turned back and went out. Seeing it, the people praised God with great joy and glorified Agatha. This wonder, the life says, happened in the month of February, on the fifth day, the day on which she suffered for Jesus Christ our Lord.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and gives a year the Romanian page did not: she gave up her spirit in 251, in prison, after frightful tortures, in the years of the emperor Decius. saint.gr is undecided about her city — «καταγόταν από το Παλέρμο ή την Κατάνη της Σικελίας», from Palermo or from Catania — and its kontakion calls her the boast of Catania. It adds that at fifteen she was left an orphan and sole heir of her parents' great wealth, and gave the whole of it away to works of charity; that the prefect Quintianus tried by various means to get her to marry him; and that she bore the tortures with a wonderful endurance, glorifying God for counting her worthy of the honour of martyrdom. Her synaxis, the page says, used to be held at her martyrion in the seventh region of Byzantium, and her relics were brought to Constantinople under Basil II and Constantine VIII; her head is now at the monastery of St Paul on the Holy Mountain, and fragments at Prousos in Eurytania and Loukou at Astros in Kynouria.

The Serbian church keeps her on the Julian 5 February, and Bishop Nikolai's Ohrid Prologue answers the question saint.gr leaves open: she was born in the Sicilian city of Palermo, of noble and well-off parents, and she died at Catania. It agrees on the year, 251, on Decius, and on the judge Quintianus, who saw how beautiful she was and wanted her for his wife, and was answered that she was the bride of Christ and could not be unfaithful to her betrothed.

Three things stand only here. Pressed between tortures to deny Christ and so escape the rest, she said that the torments were very useful to her: as wheat cannot come into the granary before it is cleaned of the chaff, so her soul could not enter Paradise unless her body were first broken by suffering. When her breasts had been cut away and she had been thrown back into the prison, the Apostle [Peter](/saints/peter-the-apostle) appeared to her there and gave her back her body whole and her health. And after her death Quintianus set out to seize her property, and on the road the horses under him and under his soldiers went mad, bit his face all over, threw him to the ground and trampled him dead.
