# Matthias

Matthias was of Bethlehem by birth, of the tribe of Judah. From childhood he learned in Jerusalem the Scriptures and the understanding of the Law from [Symeon the God-receiver](/saints/symeon-the-god-receiver), who guided him also in every good work. When the Lord, after thirty years from his birth of the Most Holy Virgin, was baptised in the Jordan by John and showed himself to the world, gathering disciples and preaching the kingdom of God, Matthias heard his teaching and saw his wonders, was wounded with love for him, and left the things of the world to follow him.

He was first one of the seventy lesser apostles, of whom the Gospel writes that the Lord appointed another seventy and sent them two by two before his face. After the voluntary passion, the resurrection and the ascension of the Lord, he was numbered with the twelve greater apostles in the place of Judas, who had fallen. Peter, standing in the midst of the church, said that the place of Judas must be filled so that the company of the twelve should be whole; two were put forward, Joseph called Barsabas and Matthias, and they prayed: You, Lord, who know the hearts of all, show which of these two you have chosen. The lot fell on Matthias, and the choosing was confirmed by the Holy Spirit descending upon him in tongues of fire as upon the rest.

When the apostles received by lot the part of the world each should go to, the lot of Matthias was Judea, and he laboured there, going through the cities and villages with the good news of the world's salvation. But he carried the name of Christ among the nations too: it is told of him that he preached the word in Ethiopia also, where he suffered much — dragged, beaten, hung up for torment, his sides torn with iron and burnt with fire from below — and that Christ strengthening him he received it all with joy.

In Macedonia, the account goes on, the Greek pagans caught him and, wishing to test the power of the faith he preached, gave him a poisoned drink that destroyed the sight of the eyes, so that whoever tasted it was at once blind. The apostle drank it in the name of Christ and was unharmed; and laying his hands on those the poison had blinded — more than two hundred and fifty of them — and calling on the name of Christ, he healed them.

At the last he was brought before the high priest in Jerusalem and told to deny what he had found. Far be it from me, he answered, to turn from the truth I have once found: Jesus the Nazarene, whom you rejected and gave over to death, I believe in my heart and confess with my mouth to be the true Son of God, of one nature with the Father and before the ages; and I say that I am the servant of Christ. The high priest stopped his ears, called it blasphemy, had the Law read out where it commands that whoever blasphemes the name of the Lord shall die, and condemned him to be stoned.

They took him to the place called Betlaschila, the house of those stoned, and on the way he said to those leading him: Hypocrites, well did David prophesy of you, they hunt the soul of the righteous and condemn innocent blood. Two witnesses, as the Law ordered, laid their hands on his head and testified that he had blasphemed God, Moses and the Law, and they cast the first stones; and he asked that those first stones be buried with him, in witness of his suffering for Christ. The rest then stoned him, and lifting his hands to heaven he gave up his soul. After he was dead they cut off his head with an axe, in the Roman manner, to make it look as though the preacher of Christ had been an enemy of Caesar; and the faithful took his body and buried it with honour.

The Greek calendar names him again on 30 Ιουνίου, where saint.gr keeps the Synaxis of the Twelve Apostles. The page lists all twelve whom the Lord first chose, Judas Iscariot excepted and the twelfth place filled by the apostle elected in the upper room on the eve of Pentecost, and it says plainly that it sketches each man's life at his own feast and not here. That roll includes him, though the first of the three apolytikia the page prints runs through a different twelve — Peter and Paul with Luke and Mark, and without him — so that hymn is not taken here. What the synaxis is for, the page says, is the reminder of the unity the twelve had among themselves and of the manner of life that did so much for the world's rebirth in Christ; and the duty it draws from that is that Christians should move in their tracks with the same warm zeal for spreading the Gospel.
