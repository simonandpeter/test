# Porphyrius of Gaza

Gaza, doxologia's life begins, is a city of Palestine near the border of Egypt, given long ago to the sons of Israel of the tribe of Judah and afterwards held by the Philistines — the city whose barred gates Samson once carried off on his shoulders. Long after Christ had become man, and after the apostles and the bishops who followed them had lit part of it with the faith, the greater part of Gaza still wandered in the darkness of idolatry, worshipping a carved thing called Marnas, and Venus, and other unclean Greek gods.

Christian blood had been spilt there many times, and worst under Julian the Apostate, when the unbelievers killed priests, monks and consecrated virgins without mercy. The city held so great a people that nobody could cleanse it and light it wholly with the faith — until Porphyrius. He broke all the idols, raised a church in the middle of the city, and won the whole of Gaza for God with great labour, as the story goes on to tell.

He came of Thessalonica, of rich parents, and at twenty-five he left his father's house, his brothers and his wealth and went first into Egypt and then to Scetis, where he put on the monastic habit and took up the desert life. After five years among the holy fathers there he went to Jerusalem, venerated the life-giving Cross and the Tomb of Christ, went round all the holy places, and settled in a great cave in the parts of the Jordan, living to God in fasting and unceasing prayer.

He was five years there before he fell ill, and asked an acquaintance to carry him to Jerusalem, since he could no longer walk. Ill as he was he went round the holy places daily, and when his legs would not carry him he crawled on his knees; he never let a day pass without going to the church of Christ's Resurrection and to the holy Cross. A young monk named Mark, seeing him labour so in his sickness, began to serve him for God's sake — and it was this Mark who afterwards wrote his life.

After the unbelievers of the city had been put down and punished, the rest of his days passed in peace and quiet. He thanked the maiden Salata, who had hidden him from the hands that sought his life, by enlightening her with holy baptism; he healed the sick old woman and baptised her too, and fed them both from the goods of the church. He wanted to give Salata in lawful marriage, but she chose rather to be a bride of Christ, and at this he rejoiced greatly in spirit, consecrated her to Christ's service and numbered her among the holy virgins.

He worked wonders as well. Three children who had fallen into a deep well he kept alive and whole by his prayer; and a woman of the Manichaean heresy, full of devilish craft and heretical fables, who blasphemed the right faith and was drawing many into ungodliness, he punished by prayer, and she fell suddenly and perished. Having pastured the Church of Christ twenty-four years, eleven months and eight days, he went to the chief of shepherds. doxologia gives no year for his birth or his death, and its troparion tab for him carries a Greek recording and no text.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and gives the whole career with its dates. saint.gr says Porphyrius was born at Thessalonica of rich and devout parents, left both parents and riches in the reigns of Arcadius and Honorius, and went to Egypt, then the great monastic centre, where he became a monk in a skete. After five years he came to Jerusalem and preached the Gospel to Jews and Greeks. There he fell seriously ill of a cirrhosis of the liver, and would still not miss a day at the church of the Resurrection and the other holy places, to the wonder of the other pilgrims; among them was Mark, afterwards his biographer, who had come on pilgrimage from Asia, and the two were bound together for life. Mark took on a serious piece of unfinished business, the division of the family property in Thessalonica with Porphyrius's now grown brothers; and while he was away Porphyrius's health was restored miraculously, after a vision of the Lord's crucifixion and of the grateful thief. Mark came back with the share — 4,400 coins and a quantity of silver vessels and costly clothes — and Porphyrius soon sold it all and gave it to the poor and to the very poor monasteries of Jerusalem and of Egypt.

He was ordained presbyter there in 392 by John II, patriarch of Jerusalem; and when Aeneas, bishop of Gaza, died in 395, he was elected bishop of Gaza and consecrated by John, bishop of Caesarea. To protect his flock from the injustices of the pagans and the magistrates he went to Constantinople to ask the help of the emperor Arcadius and of Eudoxia, and there met [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom), who commended him to Amantius the cubicularius and to the sovereigns and backed his petition warmly. The emperor was persuaded and gave him a decree restricting the pagans and the heretics, with an imperial grant for churches where pagan temples had stood; and Porphyrius had the Marneion, the famous temple of the pagans of Gaza founded by Hadrian in 129, pulled down. In its place a beautiful church was raised at the empress Eudoxia's charge — she sent the Antiochene architect Rufinus to Gaza for it — with thirty-two great columns of Karystian marble, named the Eudoxian, and dedicated at Pascha 407.

In the years after that he worked at the ordering of his diocese, and Mark his biographer keeps his charitable and missionary work in vivid colours. In 415 he took part in the Synod of Diospolis under John II of Jerusalem, which dealt with the theologian Pelagius — who had taken refuge with John at Jerusalem after his clash in Africa with [Augustine](/saints/augustine-of-hippo), bishop of Hippo, over original sin and divine grace — and at which Pelagius was acquitted, having accepted the basic teaching that divine grace is necessary to a man's salvation. Porphyrius rested in 420 after a short illness, at seventy-two, «τὸν καλὸν ἀγῶνα τετελεκῶς πρὸς τοὺς εἰδωλομανεῖς ἕως τῆς ἡμέρας τῆς κοιμήσεως αὐτοῦ», having finished the good fight against the idol-mad to the day of his falling asleep. A fragment of his body is at the monastery of Kykkos in Cyprus.
