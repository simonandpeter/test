# Philaret (Danilevsky)

Philaret — Thomas Danilevsky in the world — was born in Ukraine in 1777.
His mother Theodosia gave him his first lessons in Christian virtue; a
widow of an archpriest taught him his letters at eight, and he was soon
helping the church’s readers to read and sing and had the Typikon by
heart. In his youth Providence took him with an archpriest to the Kiev
Caves Lavra, where the elder Tryphillius, head of the Far Caves, kept him
as cell-attendant and canonarch, formed him in obedience, poverty,
patience and humility, and foretold that he would be a monk and a head
over monks; after three years he served his term of military duty as
reader of the army church of the Black Sea Cossack host at Ekaterinodar,
then returned to the Lavra and sang in the Great Church, where he grew
close to the hieromonk Anthony (Smirnitsky), the future archbishop of
Voronezh, who advised him to withdraw to some hermit monastery. Monks of
the Sophroniev hermitage came to the Lavra with tales of its solitude, its
strict rule and its abbot, the elder Theodosius, friend of Paisius
Velichkovsky; that night Theodosius appeared to him in a dream and called
him, and a sermon on the text Show me, O Lord, the way that I should walk
decided him. He entered Sophroniev in 1802; Theodosius, who said this
brother will give my soul rest, set him over the singers as a master of
the Typikon and tonsured him the same year with the name Philaret, lover
of virtue, taught him the fight with temptation and the Jesus prayer, and
before his death that December presented him for the diaconate. Ordained
hierodeacon on 8 February 1803 by Archbishop Theoctistus of Kursk and
made the hermitage’s typikon-keeper, he suffered much from the lovers of
short services and said that where the Church’s rules are touched one must
resist, if need be, to blood; in 1806 he was ordained hieromonk and made
dean, took a lonely cell in the garden, met the enemy’s assaults —
lustful thoughts, despondency, terrors, the devil in the shapes of vermin
— with the words, poor Philaret, if you have come near to suffering, be
ready for the cross, and won the gift of discernment, so that elders older
than himself came to him for counsel. Offered the abbacy of Sophroniev in
1815 he declined, not without a prompting from above.

The brethren of the Glinsk hermitage, who had learned of his gifts and
by their prayers to the Mother of God been shown their man, petitioned for
him, and on 11 May 1817 the Kursk consistory appointed him superior. He
left Sophroniev with its abbot’s blessing and a copy of its wonder-working
Iveron icon, and arrived on 6 June 1817: as he stepped from the carriage a
swarm of bees flew in and settled on his seat, and a monk had seen the
night before a pillar rising to heaven from Sophroniev toward Glinsk. What
he found was a few blackened wooden cells in a clearing of pine forest, a
stone church needing repair, a rotting iconostasis, an empty granary and
a growing brotherhood; the Mother of God gathered us, he told them, and
she will provide, and when there was no bread for three days and the
faint-hearted came for a blessing to leave, he begged them to bear a
little longer, and that night thirty carts of flour arrived from an
unknown landowner. He remade the house within and without. Within, he
introduced the strict common rule of the Holy Mountain and eldership with
the daily revealing of thoughts, put every newcomer under an experienced
elder, demanded of beginners the whole cutting off of their own will,
laid obediences with such skill that the well-born often worked harder
than peasants, corrected the smallest fault — a hand waved, a head held
high, a needless noise — as a want of humility, found for every fault the
mean between a severity that drives to despair and a leniency that breeds
contempt, and let those who would not bear the reform depart. He wrote
the rule down, thirty-six chapters in three parts, on the Athonite model,
and the Holy Synod confirmed it on 24 August 1821; he copied it out
himself in uncial script and bound it in leather, and vowed to the Mother
of God that he would answer at God’s judgement against any successor who
broke it. Archbishop Theoctistus, visiting a month after his arrival,
said, glory to God who works wonders — I see Lazarus risen from the dead,
and gave him the nabedrennik; Bishop Eugene consecrated the warm church
of St Nicholas on 15 August 1819 and preached that a house flourishes by
the inner piety of its brethren rather than its walls; Philaret (Drozdov)
wrote that the new builder had already done much and the house had begun
to flower by grace, which is not usual.

On 5 July 1821 he was received by Alexander I in his private study; the
emperor rose, took his blessing, kissed his hand and asked about his
origins, the hermitage’s buildings and land, the brethren and their rule,
whether he had been at Valaam and whether his rules agreed with Valaam’s,
and he handed over a short note asking for three hundred desyatinas of
forest, a staff of twenty monks, the payment of the house’s debts of
three thousand roubles, the confirmation of his rule and the brethren’s
right to elect their superior; the emperor promised all, and at a second
audience told him the debts were paid and the forest would follow, with
an imperial decree to the Synod on the staff. The brethren kept 4
November 1823 as a day of thanksgiving with the bells rung all day and the
Paschal services sung, and the abbot set an annual feast on the Sunday of
Orthodoxy in its memory. The Empress Elizabeth Alexeevna, passing the
hermitage’s chapel at Zarutskaya on her way to Taganrog on 25 September
1825, took his blessing, a prosphora and a copy of the Glinsk icon, and
sent five hundred roubles for the chapel. Four journeys to Petersburg
between 1821 and 1825 made him a champion of Orthodoxy against the
heretical mysticism then fashionable at court and among the nobility:
with no secular schooling but an extraordinary memory he named not only
the fathers’ books but the chapter and page, his opponents fetched the
books and were confounded, and Labzin, publisher of the Zion Herald, was
beaten, as Philaret wrote in 1833 — Labzin defeated has perished and his
boat is swallowed up, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against the
Church of God; the ban on secret societies and lodges of 1822, Labzin’s
exile and Golitsyn’s dismissal followed. In 1825 the new emperor Nicholas
I had a dispute against monasticism in the higher clergy of Petersburg
settled at once, and Philaret was called to defend it in his presence, and
twice afterwards to Kiev on the same question. Meanwhile he built: from
1823 the superior’s, sacristan’s and priests’ buildings, an infirmary and
a stone joinery, nineteen wooden houses, a steward’s yard, stables, a
brewery, mills, a fulling-mill, ice-houses and barns, two orchards, a
guesthouse with kitchen and summer rooms, and in 1823–1829 a new stone
wall; marshes were drained and a pond dug; the Near Skete was founded in
1828 when the brethren’s confessor, the hieroschemamonk Panteleimon,
withdrew to the chapel on the spot where the wonder-working Glinsk icon
had appeared and others followed him; and over the holy gate he raised
the stone church of the Iveron icon, begun in 1826 in thankful memory of
the Mother of God’s wonders since 1817 and consecrated in October 1831,
with the Sophroniev copy over its royal doors. In his time the
wonder-working icon of the Saviour Not-made-by-hands came to the house,
and grace-filled signs and healings were worked from it. Hospitality he
wrote into the rule as its twenty-third chapter: every pilgrim was lodged,
fed and doctored without charge, and the guestmaster forbidden to take
money.

He taught the brethren the Jesus prayer, strict restraint, solitude and
silence, the shunning of enmity and quick reconciliation, and told them
that the sweat a monk sheds in obedience with attention to his heart
weighs before God as the blood of the martyrs; he warned them against
self-deceit by immoderate fasting and kept the royal middle way; he met a
merchant’s son stung all over by bees on his first day at the apiary with
good, good, my son, a fine beginning of obedience and patience — now the
bees bite you, later the demons will; bear it then as you bear it now, for
only he who endures to the end is saved, and sent him a wire mask; the
fearful, he said, should not go out to battle, for a soldier is ashamed of
wounds taken with his back turned, and a soldier of Christ proves his
courage by wounds taken face to face. Twelve of his disciples became
superiors of other houses, Macarius (Glukharev), the apostle of the Altai,
among them, who ever after remembered the guidance of the unforgettable
abba Philaret; Pimen (Myasnikov) called him one of the best superiors of
his time, respected not only by monks but by the hierarchs themselves;
Seraphim of Sarov loved him in Christ and sent to him those who asked a
blessing to enter a monastery, calling his house a great school of the
monastic life, and elders came to him from Sophroniev, Korennaya, the
Lavra, Solovki, Valaam and Athos, so that the brotherhood grew more than
fourfold. He wrote rules for the convents of Ekaterinburg (1821, confirmed
1822), Borisovka and Ufa, directed the Annunciation convent at Ufa to the
end of his days, and under his guidance six more convents arose, at
Chelyabinsk, Troitsk, Orenburg, Buzuluk, Menzelinsk and Birsk, while the
Glinsk rule was taken up by the Holy Mountains hermitage in Kharkov
province and many other monasteries. He loved bodily work — orchards,
garden, apiary, the draining of marshes — I torment the tormentor, he
said, and came back to his cell scarcely able to walk, his hands always
calloused; his cell was six arshins by three, with a few icons, a plain
table and chairs, a half-cupboard of books and a bench boarded on three
sides like a coffin for his bed; he received the brethren by night, wrote
some seven hundred and fifty letters a year to spiritual children from
Petersburg and all Russia, and at the first stroke of the bell at half
past midnight went to matins. He saw the Mother of God in his cell on the
eve of the Iveron feast; on the night of 2 January 1833, after matins, he
saw from his porch a radiance in the sky and a soul carried up by angels
with singing, called some of the brethren to see it, and said, so the
souls of the righteous depart — today Father Seraphim’s soul is rising to
heaven, which proved the very night Seraphim of Sarov died. He healed a
peasant’s violent headache by taking his head between two fingers, and a
landowner of Akhtyrka of a long illness by his prayers; during the
epidemic at Ufa in 1833 a nun saw him sprinkling the convent walls with
holy water, and no one fell ill after; and he tapped a visitor’s daughter
Elizabeth lightly with his staff — get used to it; when you carry one you
will know how to use it — and she became the abbess Emilia of
Verkho-Kharkov. Nicholas I sent him a gold pectoral cross on 20 January
1829, he received the abbot’s staff on 1 March 1835 and was made igumen
on 14 March 1839, and the greater part of what he thought of it stands in
his own report of 1837: some success in outward order can be seen, but the
inner spiritual building occupies the minds and hearts of the brethren
more and more, and all of it belongs not to us but to Thy name, O Lord.

Two years before his death he foretold it and named his successor, his
disciple Eustratius, superior of Khotmyzhsk; two days before Christmas
1838 he fainted from exhaustion, and his strength visibly failed; he
served his last Liturgy on the Sunday of Orthodoxy in 1841, blessed the
brethren, humbly asked all their forgiveness and prayers, and met his end
in silence and solitude. God gave him the first day of Pascha in spiritual
joy; at one in the morning of Bright Monday he received the Holy
Mysteries, and at half past six, on 31 March 1841, he died in peace, aged
sixty-four, on the feast of the Iveron icon he had brought from
Sophroniev, his arms folded in a cross. He was buried on Bright Thursday
amid a great crowd, two archimandrites and a council of priest-monks
singing the victory over death in bright vestments, so that it seemed a
festival and not a burial, in the vault he had prepared at the southern
door of the cathedral — at the threshold of the church, as he had willed,
that all who enter may remember him — and his last testament was, keep
peace and love among you, brethren, and if I find boldness before the
Lord, I believe our house will not fail. His body gave out a fragrance at
the burial, and none had been put in the coffin; in 1847, when the
cathedral was enlarged and the vault opened, the monk Israel lifted the
lid and found the vestments whole and the body incorrupt and fragrant.
Elders worthy of contemplation saw him more than once singing at the
kliros with the brethren, and his disciple, the schema-archimandrite [Iliodor](/saints/heliodorus-golovanitsky), praying to know how many of the Glinsk brethren were saved, saw
the church and altar set with lamps great and small, some burning, some
waiting to burn, and among winged monks shining with heavenly light his
abbot standing like the sun among stars, his face too bright to look on.
His Instruction to a Newly Tonsured Monk, printed by the Synod in April
1824, was sent by imperial decree that June to every diocese for the
governing of monasteries; it was read in the Glinsk refectory on the day of
each tonsure, and was reprinted three times with his Instruction to a
Beginner before the Tonsure; he wrote an Instruction on the office of a
confessor serving nuns, five Heirmologia and other chant, and his sayings
survive in his disciples’ notes — the true labour of monasticism is in
the work of the mind and in prayer, where the true knowledge of spiritual
things and of God is found; who wholly gives himself to God’s will has no
sorrow, for the enemy has no place there. In the twentieth century the
Glinsk elders still said, the elder Philaret said, Father Philaret
established, and on his name-day, 1 December of the old style, the
monastery kept a memorial vigil and panikhida. The Council of Bishops of
2017 entered him in the calendar of the Russian Church, which keeps him
on 31 March of the old style, 13 April, and on 22 September, 9 September
of the old style, in the synaxis of the Glinsk fathers.
