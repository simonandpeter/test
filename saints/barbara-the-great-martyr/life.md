# Barbara

Barbara lived and suffered under Maximian. Her father Dioscorus was a rich and illustrious pagan of the Syrian city of Heliopolis who, after his wife died, gave himself entirely to his only daughter.

Seeing how beautiful she was, he decided to hide her from strangers and built her a tower, where none but her pagan teachers were let in to see her. From it she could look out over hills stretching into the distance — wooded slopes, swift rivers, meadows under a mottled blanket of flowers by day, and by night the ordered and majestic vault of the heavens. Before long the girl began to ask herself who the first cause and maker of so harmonious a world could be.

She became convinced by degrees that the soulless idols her father and her teachers worshipped were only the work of men's hands and could not have made the world around her, and the desire to know the true God took hold of her so completely that she resolved to give her whole life to it and to remain a virgin. Her father, thinking seclusion had affected her temper, let her out of the tower and gave her freedom in choosing her friends; she met Christian girls in the city who taught her of the Creator, the Trinity and the Divine Word, and a priest who had come from Alexandria disguised as a merchant instructed and baptised her before returning home.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, and saint.gr sets her in a reign and is careful about one thing the story usually glosses. She lived in the years of the emperor Maximian, which it dates 286 to 305, and she was the daughter of the idolater Dioscorus, one of the richest idolaters of Heliopolis, who because of her bodily beauty kept her shut up in a tower. We do not know where she was taught the Christian truths, the page says, her father being a fanatical idolater — which is why she tried to keep her faith in the Triune God hidden. A chance thing betrayed her: her father learnt from the workmen that she had asked them to open three windows in the tower, in the name of the Holy Trinity.

He was so enraged that he chased her through the tower with his sword to kill her. She fled to the mountains, but he caught her and handed her over to the local ruler Marcian, accusing her of her faith; and under examination she confessed Christ boldly and reviled the idols. After dreadful tortures she was paraded naked through the city and at last slaughtered by her own father — who, the moment he had finished his crime, fell dead, struck by lightning in the divine judgement. Her synaxis used to be held at the place of her martyrdom, at the Basiliskos near Saint Zenais.

The page then follows the relics at length, and this folder takes only the shape of it. They were kept at Constantinople until the eleventh century, when part of them went to Venice under the doge Pietro II Orseolo, carried by the princess Maria Argyropoulina; they were laid in Saint Mark's, moved after 1009 to the monastery of Saint John the Theologian at Torcello, and brought back to Saint Mark's in the Napoleonic wars, where they are today, part remaining at Torcello. It is not known when or how her head came to Montecatini in Italy, where it is now.

In the twelfth century part of the remainder went from Constantinople to the monastery of Saint Michael of the Golden Domes at Kyiv, and in 1930 to the cathedral of Saint Vladimir in the same city; and on 1 June 2003 a portion was given to the Church of Greece and laid in the shrine of her name at Agia Varvara in Attica. She is reckoned, in Greece and elsewhere, the patron of the artillery, established as such in Greece in 1828; and in Orthodox iconography she is often painted with a cup in her hand, as the protectress against sudden death, since she does not wish the dying to be deprived of holy communion.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her on the same menologion day and agrees on the year of her suffering, 306; on one thing it does not agree, and that is where she suffered. It makes her father a man of note in position and in wealth in the Egyptian Heliopolis, where the life this folder was written from puts Heliopolis in Syria. Of the tower it adds the bath-house her father built her with two windows in it. Looking out of the tower at the earth below and the starry heaven above, the book says, her mind was opened by the grace of God and she knew the one true God the Creator, though she had no man for a teacher in it.

When her father was once away from the city she came out of the tower and, by God's providence, met some Christian women who told her the truth of the faith of Christ, and her heart was kindled with love for the Lord. Then she gave orders that a third window be cut in the bath-house, so that the three windows should be a symbol of the Holy Trinity; on one of the stones in it she drew a cross with her finger, and the cross sank into the stone as though it had been cut with iron; and from her footprint on the floor a spring of water welled up, which afterwards healed many of their sicknesses.

When her father learnt her faith he beat her cruelly, drove her out of the tower and ran after her to kill her, and a rock parted and hid her from him. Handed over at last to the prefect Marcian, she was stripped and scourged until her whole body was covered with blood and wounds; and in the prison the Lord himself appeared to her with his angels and healed her. A woman named Juliana, seeing it, desired martyrdom too; both were terribly tortured and led through the city in mockery, and their breasts were cut off. Her own father killed his daughter at the place of execution and the soldiers killed Juliana, and that same day lightning struck his house and killed him and the prefect together. Her wonderworking relics, the book ends, are at Kyiv, and glorified in the kingdom of Christ she has appeared many times down to this day, sometimes alone and sometimes in the company of the Most Holy Theotokos.
