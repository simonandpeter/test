# Nikephoros of Constantinople

Nikephoros was born at Constantinople of right-believing and God-fearing parents, Theodore and Eudocia. His father held an office about the emperor's secret papers under Constantine Copronymus, and was accused to him of honouring the icons of the Saviour, of the most pure Mother of God and of all the saints. Called and questioned, he would not deny them; he was beaten and banished to a city called Molina and shut in a cruel prison, and afterwards brought back and pressed again, and chose beatings and death rather than break the Church's laws. He was sent to Nicaea, where he spent six years in much suffering and died.

Eudocia shared all her husband's dangers, following him in his banishments and suffering with him in his chains. After his death she returned to Constantinople and lived as a holy widow should; and her son Nikephoros studied philosophy and was then put in his father's place at the imperial court. She did not die until she had seen him a monk and a bishop, and then she went into a convent of virgins, was tonsured, and departed to God in deep old age.

Such were his parents. Nikephoros grew up in good ways and passed his youth in chastity and great self-restraint, full of the outward wisdom and the inward, knowing perfectly the books of the Greek philosophers and the divine Scriptures, and a famous orator besides. After the death of Leo, son of Copronymus, the Seventh Ecumenical Council was held at Nicaea against the heresy that fights the holy icons, in the reign of Constantine, son of Leo, and of his mother Irene; and Nikephoros, still a layman, helped the holy Fathers greatly, for the emperor's mind and the emperor's words were entrusted to him at that council. So before his episcopate he was already a confessor and teacher of the right faith.

After the council he stayed some years in worldly honour among the imperial counsellors, then judged that serving a world so full of trouble was no use to his soul, despised all vainglory and left the city, settling in a quiet place apart by the Thracian Bosporus. There he served God alone in prayer and fasting; and after a time he built a church, gathered monks and made a monastery, and although he had not the monastic habit he laboured as a monk, trying himself to see whether he could bear the hardness of that life.

He spent many years there, until the reign of the emperor Nikephoros. Then the most holy Tarasios, patriarch of Constantinople, departed to the Lord, and Nikephoros was chosen to the patriarchate, for though a layman in habit he was a perfect monk in life. He was first tonsured, then ordained priest, and then, against his will, the emperor forced him to take the patriarchal throne on the bright day of Holy Pascha. He adorned the Church with teaching and with a good life, setting the rebellious right, strengthening the upright, and driving the heretics from the flock like wolves.

In that time the Greeks were at war with the Bulgars, and the emperor Nikephoros went out to the war and was killed. His son Staurakios reigned two months and died. After him came Michael Rangabe, a right-believing man who had been curopalates; and after two years Leo the Armenian drove him out and seized the throne. Before his coronation the patriarch sent bishops to him with the book of the right faith, asking him to sign it with his own hand as the right-believing emperors before him had done. Leo dissembled, promised to sign after the crown was on his head, and was believed; and secretly he signed instead the book the heretics brought him.

He came to the great church of Holy Wisdom in much pride, and as the patriarch set the crown on his unworthy head God showed him what that emperor would be: the imperial crown became in his hands like a crown of thorns, and pricked them painfully. The patriarch knew that this meant the persecution soon to come upon the Church from that man, and told his clergy of it with sighs. The life then tells at length the emperor's councils and assemblies, the bishops Nikephoros called to him, the all-night vigil in the great church, his address to the people — let us not be fearful and small-souled — and the long dispute before the emperor over the icons, in which he answered the command given to Moses out of the Law itself and out of the cherubim of gold.

The heretical assembly judged the righteous man unjustly, deposed Nikephoros and anathematised him, and with him the right-believing patriarchs before him, Tarasios and Germanos, who had already died in peace. Late in the evening the emperor sent soldiers to turn him out of the patriarchal house and take him into exile, and they burst in with arms, shouting and reviling him and the patriarchs before him. Hearing it, he wept and thanked God that he was counted worthy to hear such insults for the right faith. The patrician Thomas quieted the soldiers, shut the doors, and told the emperor that two men would be enough, since the patriarch was very ill and could not walk.

So it was done. Wishing to pray once more in the great church that was his throne, he went in leaning on two men, had candles lit and incense burned, stretched himself on the ground in the form of a cross and prayed long, watering the ground with tears. Rising, he saw some of the faithful who had run there and were weeping for him; he blessed them and gave them the last kiss and said: my children, I found you right-believing Christians and I leave you right-believing Christians. Then he went out, and at midnight, when everyone slept, they took him by cart to the shore, put him in a ship and carried him to Chrysopolis, to a place called Volus where there was a monastery. He had shepherded the Church nine years.

Not long after he was sent further, to the island of Prokonnesos, to a monastery of the Great Martyr Theodore. As the ship passed the place where Theophanes the Confessor was abbot, the two men saw one another with foreseeing eyes: Theophanes had incense and a lit candle brought, telling his disciple that the patriarch was going into prison for the right faith and must have the honour due him; and Nikephoros, in the ship, knelt and bowed to him and blessed him with his hands stretched out in the air, saying to those with him that Theophanes would soon suffer the same. It happened soon.

Nikephoros spent thirteen years in banishment, in hardship, in want of necessities and in frequent illness, and then went to the Lord. As he died he said with joy the words of David: blessed be the Lord, who has not given us over to be a prey to their teeth; our soul is delivered like a bird out of the snare of the hunters. The faithful mourned him and the heretics rejoiced, and his body was buried in the church of the Great Martyr Theodore. Afterwards, when the leaders of the heresy were dead and the tyranny against the icons ceased, his relics were brought to Constantinople in the reign of Michael, son of Theophilos, and of his mother Theodora, and laid with honour in the cathedral church of Holy Wisdom. The life names many reigns and prints no year, so none is recorded here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 2 June, and saint.gr's account puts years to a life doxologia dated only by reigns. It says he was born at Constantinople in 758; that his parents, Theodore and Eudokia, belonged to a noble and official rank but were devout and virtuous people who nursed their son on the waters of the divine Scriptures; and that his father's adherence to the right faith was the cause of his being persecuted and exiled by the iconoclast emperor Constantine V, the Copronymus, to Nicaea, where he died. Gifted with unusual spiritual abilities, Nikephoros acquired a great theological and secular education, but withdrew early to an estate of his on the Bosphorus, where he gave himself to asceticism and to the study of the Scriptures — until the emperor obliged him to take over the direction of the great poorhouse of Constantinople. When Patriarch Tarasios of Constantinople fell asleep, the emperor Nikephoros I put him forward as the worthy successor, and on the Sunday of Pascha in 806 he went up to the patriarchal throne: a throne, the page says, that was the equivalent of a Golgotha. He gave hard battle for the honour and the veneration of the holy icons; and when in 813 the impious Leo V, the Armenian, came to the throne of the Reigning City, a great and relentless persecution broke out against those who venerated them. Nikephoros's proud stand and his insistence on the protection of Orthodoxy provoked the emperor's anger, and he was removed from the patriarchal throne. He endured many hardships, and at the last, worn out, gave up his spirit to God the just rewarder on 2 June 822.

The Ohrid Prologue tells the same life in little, and dates some of what doxologia dated only by reigns. It has him govern the holy Church wisely and zealously as the greatest of Constantinople's chief shepherds. When Leo the Armenian rose against the icons he set himself against the emperor, counselling him first and rebuking him afterwards, and for that the impious emperor banished him to the island of Prokonnesos. On that island stood a monastery Nikephoros had himself built in honour of the holy Theodore, and there this confessor of the Orthodox faith passed thirteen years before he reposed and went to the Lord. The Prologue puts that repose in 827, where saint.gr puts it in 822; it says he governed the Church of Christ nine years.

When all the iconoclast emperors had perished, and Michael sat on the imperial throne with his mother, and the patriarch Methodius was given back the patriarchal one, the relics of Saint Nikephoros were carried in 846 from Prokonnesos to Constantinople. They were laid first in the church of Holy Wisdom, from which he had been driven out in his lifetime, and afterwards in the church of the Holy Apostles. The chief celebration of this great hierarch, the Prologue says, falls on 2 June, while 13 March keeps the uncovering and translation of his incorrupt relics: he was driven from Constantinople on 13 March, and on 13 March again, nineteen years later, his relics were brought into the capital.
