# Nicholas of Myra

Nicholas was born in the parts of Lycia, in the city called Patara, of honourable and well-born parents, right-believing and rich, whose names doxologia's life gives as Theophanes and Nonna. They had asked God for a child with many prayers and tears and much almsgiving, and gave him back as a gift to the Giver. The life reads his name as «biruitor de popor», conqueror of the people, and says he was a faster from the cradle: on Wednesdays and Fridays he took the breast only once, and then in the evening, and that fast he kept all his life to the end.

Set in his time to the study of the divine Scriptures, he came in a little while to much wisdom, and was as perfect in the manner of his living as in the word of his learning. He turned aside from empty friendships and idle talk, and passed whole days and whole nights together in prayer and in the reading of the divine books. An uncle of his, a bishop of the same name, saw the young man growing in good works and a stranger to the world, and counselled his parents to give him to the service of God; and the bishop raised him to the priesthood.

The life picks one work of mercy out of many to show the rest. A man of the city had fallen from wealth into such want that he thought to sell his three daughters, and Nicholas, told of it by revelation, would not go to him openly: partly to escape the glory of men, remembering the Gospel's word not to do one's alms before men, and partly so as not to shame a man who had been rich. So he carried a great bundle of gold to the house at midnight and threw it in, and did the same a second time and a third, and each of the three was married.

Going to the Holy Land, he foretold a storm at sea; and when one of the sailors fell from the masthead and lay without breath, he raised him by prayer before he had even been called on, and gave him back alive, as a man roused out of sleep. At Jerusalem he went up to Golgotha, and the closed doors of the church opened of themselves to let him in at night to pray. Afterwards he sought the quiet of a monastery, until a voice from above told him to go out to the labour of the people.

When John, the archbishop of Myra, the metropolis of Lycia, died, the bishops gathered, and one of them was shown that the man who came first to the church at matins and answered to the name of Nicholas was the one God chose; so he was found in the porch before dawn and brought into the middle of the church, and enthroned against his own refusing. In the persecution he preached the faith with a free tongue and was taken and shut up with many Christians, bearing hunger and thirst and the narrowness of the prison, and feeding those bound with him on the word of God. When peace returned, Myra received him back; the life calls him a martyr by his own will, crowned without blood.

He was one of the three hundred and eighteen fathers of the council the emperor Constantine summoned, and stood with great courage against the blasphemies of Arius. One of the historians, the page says, tells that he was kindled with zeal like a second Elijah and shamed Arius in the middle of the council not with word only but with deed, striking him across the face, for which the fathers took his episcopal signs from him — and that Christ with the Gospel and his most pure Mother with the omophorion were seen giving them back, so that the fathers fell silent and honoured him.

At Myra he came upon three officers of Constantine already kneeling for the sword on a false accusation, and went through the crowd, took the sword out of the headsman's hand, threw it on the ground and untied them. In deep old age, full of good days, after a short sickness of the body he ended his temporal life well; and the bishops, gathered out of all the cities, laid him with honour in the cathedral church of the metropolis of Myra, on the sixth day of the month of December. From his relics there flowed fragrant myrrh, and the sick anointed with it were made whole, so that men came to his tomb from the ends of the earth.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 6 December, and its page adds a year, the reigns he lived under, and what was found in his tomb at Bari in our own time. saint.gr has him born in the third century at Patara of Lycia, of devout and wealthy parents, and active in the time of the emperors Diocletian, which it dates 284 to 304, Maximian, 286 to 305, and Constantine the Great. Left an orphan young and the heir of a great fortune, he spent it on the destitute, on orphans, on the poor, on widows and on hard-pressed householders; and hearing that a man meant to prostitute his three daughters for money, he secured each girl's dowry on three nights in secret, leaving a hundred gold florins for each.

Then he gave himself to the ascetic life, and for his singular virtue was honoured, without seeking it, first with the priesthood at Patara and afterwards with the archbishopric of Myra; from which seat he was taken by the local rulers and thrown into prison, and to which he returned when Constantine came to the throne and all the Christians were set free. He sat at the First Ecumenical Council, where he was marked out for his wisdom and the completeness of his character, and he reposed in 330.

The page relates at length that his tomb in the basilica at Bari was opened in 1953 and again in 1957, that the bones were examined by the professor of anatomy at the university of Bari, that they were found to belong to one and the same man of about one metre sixty-seven who lived chiefly on plant food and died aged over seventy, and that the state of some of them showed he had suffered long in a damp prison late in life.
