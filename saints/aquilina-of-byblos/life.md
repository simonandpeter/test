# Aquilina of Byblos

Aquilina was born in the city of Byblos in Palestine, among Christians who had learned the faith in Christ from the apostles. Her father was named Eutolmius; when the child was four months old her mother carried her to the bishop Eutalius, who signed her in the name of the Lord and made her a catechumen, and two months later enlightened her with holy baptism. Her father died when she was a year old, and her mother brought her up, teaching her at seven all the ways that belong to Christians.

In the seventh year of Diocletian's reign, when the girl was ten, a certain Volusian was set as proconsul over Palestine and began to persecute the faithful. Aquilina used to reason with the girls of her own age against the honour paid to idols, asking what good there was in gods who were themselves dead and powerless. One of the proconsul's servants, Nicodemus, overheard her and reported her, and she was taken in the second year of Volusian's proconsulship and the twelfth year of her life, and brought before the court.

Volusian tried her first with soft words, offering her life and the emperor's favour, and as many days as she liked to think it over; she asked for not one day and not one hour, saying that from infancy she had learned to worship one God. He then had her head bored through the ears with iron spits heated in the fire, so that the heat reached her brain and her brains ran from her nostrils with the blood, and she fell as though dead. Thinking her dead, he had her dragged outside the city for the dogs, as unworthy of burial. At midnight an angel touched her and raised her up sound, and told her to go and rebuke Volusian; the city gates opened of themselves, and she stood before him as he slept, and he woke in terror.

He condemned her to the sword. Giving thanks that her course had not been run in vain, she heard a voice from heaven calling her to receive what was prepared for her, and fell asleep before the executioner had raised his blade; he struck off the head of the dead girl all the same, and milk ran from the wound instead of blood. The Christians who were there anointed her body with costly spices, wrapped it in fine new linen and buried it with honour in Byblos, and many sick were healed at her grave.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, 13 June, and saint.gr's account gives no year but names the emperor and the judge. She came, it says, of a noble and wealthy family of Palestine and lived in the years of the emperor Diocletian; at the age of only five she was baptised by the bishop Euthalios, helped in this by her father Eutolmios, who also made conditions in which his daughter could stay firm in the Christian faith, strengthening her mind in every way. So while still young Aquilina began to preach and to teach the word of God with ardour, and by keeping company with the daughters of idolaters she managed to draw many of them to the Orthodox faith; and at the same time her devotion to works of kindness and charity became proverbial. This bright activity of hers was denounced to the proconsul Volusianus, who arrested her and tried every way to force her to renounce her faith. She stayed unshaken and upright, and although a young girl she confessed her faith boldly in the one true God. The proconsul in his rage struck her violently in the face, so that she was covered in blood; then she was put to frightful tortures; and at the last, shamed that he could not bend the faith of a weak girl, Volusianus ordered her beheaded, and so set her among «the cloud of martyrs and saints» that surrounds us.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps her on the Julian 13 June, the same menologion day the folder’s other two rows carry, and tells the whole passion. She was born in the Palestinian city of Byblos of honourable Christian parents. In her seventh year she was already fully taught in the true Christian life, and in her tenth she was so full of divine understanding and of the grace of the Holy Spirit that she preached Christ to her companions with great force and zeal. When Diocletian’s persecution began somebody informed against her to the imperial governor Volusian, a man like a beast.

He ordered her first to be scourged, and then a red-hot rod to be driven through her ears and her brain. To the last hour she confessed Christ the Lord freely and openly; and when brain and blood began to run from her head she fell as dead. The governor, thinking her truly dead, had her carried out of the city and thrown on a dunghill for the dogs to eat. But in the night an angel of God appeared to her and said: Arise, be well. And the girl rose up well, and long raised thanksgiving to God, praying him not to deny her the finishing of her martyr’s contest. Go, it shall be as you ask, came a voice from heaven.

She went back into the city. The city gate opened of itself before her, and she came in like a spirit to the governor’s court and stood before him at his own bedside, and he was seized with unspeakable terror to see alive the girl he held for dead. Next day, at his order, the executioners led her out to be put to the sword. Before the stroke the girl knelt, prayed to God and gave up her spirit, and the executioner struck the head from a body already dead. Her relics gave healing to many sick. She was twelve years old, this book says, when she suffered for the Lord, and it sets her crowning in 293.

The Russian calendar's life tells the same passion again, with the detail differently placed: the informer is one of the servants of the governor Волусиан and is not named; the red-hot rods are driven through her ears to bore her head; the angel's word to her is «Встань и будь здорова», arise and be well; and the sentence passed on her the next morning is a sentence on a sorceress who had not obeyed the imperial decrees. It adds one thing the other three books do not: after the Christians had buried her body with honour, her relics were later carried to Constantinople and laid in a church built in her honour.
