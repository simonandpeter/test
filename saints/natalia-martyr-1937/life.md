# Natalia (Kozlova)

Natalia Ivanovna Kozlova was born on 12 September 1895 into a devout
peasant family of the village of Churiki in the Skopin district of Ryazan
province, the youngest of five children; her parents managed to give their
children the traditional peasant love of work, love of God and the
Church, and such education as peasant children could have then — Natalia
finished the three-class village school, was literate and read the
Scriptures and spiritual books with great interest, and tried not to miss
a single service; of the family’s piety it is enough that one of her
sisters, Matrona, became a nun. When she came of age she was married to a
fellow villager, Stephen Kozlov, a hard-working and deeply believing man,
and they had eight children. The Kozlovs lived wholly by their own prayers
and labours, with a share of land, livestock and all a farm needed, the
father trading in the winter months in ready-made clothes in Moscow. But
the clouds gathered over the Russian land, and Russia sank into the
terrible pit of God-fighting, unbelief and godlessness; by 1935 only four
of the eight children were alive, and in that dreadful and troubled time
worse was coming. One of her surviving sons, Alexander Stepanovich, at
seventy-nine, wrote: in those far-off, monstrously tragic years our mother
Natalia Ivanovna Kozlova suffered too, mother of eight children, into whose
upbringing she put her warm heart and her kind, sincere soul; with great
gratitude we keep her motherly warmth, care and tenderness. Deeply, very
deeply and incurably were our children’s souls wounded by the loss of our
parents: in January 1935 they took our dear father from us, and four of
us were left, minors with crippled lives — the local leaders, communists,
shot our father because he had criticised them at a general meeting of the
villagers for the outrages they committed. After the father’s tragic death
came another blow: in 1936 the family’s horse was taken for unpaid “state
payments”, and what a horse meant to a peasant then is not hard to
imagine — she had to take the plough herself while her small children
strained at the shafts. Yet in that time of grief for her family and for
the whole country she was a model of unshakable faith, confession and hope
in God’s mercy, and pouring out an inexhaustible love for her neighbours
tried to comfort her children and her despairing fellow villagers; she was
chosen first treasurer and then warden of the Epiphany church of Churiki,
and on 26 June 1935 the ruling bishop gave her a certificate of blessing
for her zealous labours for the Church. As treasurer and warden she bore
great labours to provide everything needed for the services, going to
Moscow almost every week for candles and cahors wine and baking the
prosphora at home. In December 1936 a mass procession of more than two
hundred believers went to the village soviet at Churiki demanding the
release of the arrested priest Eugene Svetlov, former rector of the
Epiphany church; nor was she indifferent when the collective farm took
half the church for a granary, calling the people to work for the return
of what had been taken unlawfully and groundlessly from God and the
faithful — in May 1937, on the church council’s initiative, a large
meeting of the believers of Churiki was held, and in June she and the
council member Basil Abramkin went to Moscow, to Kalinin’s reception
office, with more than four hundred signatures of their fellow villagers
demanding the return of the seized half of the church. She was not afraid
to keep in touch with the imprisoned hieromartyr Ignatius (Sadkovsky),
bishop of Skopin, who before his arrest had twice received her at Skopin
on the parish’s business; at her interrogation the question why she kept
up ties with “enemies of the people” — the bishop and the imprisoned
priest of Churiki, Fr Eugene — did not trouble her in the least, for to a
true Orthodox Christian such things had no terror even in that time of
universal informing, and she did as the apostle Paul says, bear one
another’s burdens and so fulfil the law of Christ: to Bishop Ignatius and
the protodeacon imprisoned with him she sent money, and with Fr Eugene she
corresponded, telling him all the parish’s affairs. So active a
churchwarden did not fit the destructive plans of Russia’s new masters —
the churchmen of Churiki were too active by their reckoning — and the
resolution of the Moscow regional NKVD of 14 August 1937 (Ryazan being
then a district of the Moscow region) ran: citizen Kozlova Natalia
Ivanovna, born 1895, of the village of Churiki, an active churchwoman and
individual farmer, is sufficiently exposed as being hostile to the Soviet
power and carrying on among the collective farmers active anti-Soviet
agitation aimed at the collapse of the collective farm, together with
religious propaganda among the people, defeatist sentiments and terrorist
statements against the leaders of the party and the members of the Soviet
government; in the autumn of 1936 she organised a counter-revolutionary
procession of a hundred and fifty churchmen who threatened the village
communists and activists with reprisals for the arrest of the priest
Svetlov — to be charged under article 58-10 and held in the Skopin prison.
She flatly denied the charges of anti-Soviet and counter-revolutionary
activity. With her were arrested the church-council members Basil
Abramkin and Basil Orlov; on 16 August 1937 all three were moved to the
Taganka prison, where on 10 September the troika of the Moscow regional
NKVD sentenced them to the highest penalty, and on 14 September 1937 — 1
September of the old style, the day the Russian calendar keeps her — the
sentence was carried out near the settlement of Butovo in the fenced
ground of the NKVD range. By a resolution of the Holy Synod of 17 August
2004 the churchwarden of the Epiphany church of Churiki was entered in the
synaxis of the new martyrs and confessors of Russia; the Russian calendar
keeps her also in the synaxes of the saints of Ryazan and Moscow and of the
new martyrs of Butovo.
