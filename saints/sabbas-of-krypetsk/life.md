# Sabbas of Krypetsk

Sabbas of Krypetsk was tonsured on Athos — a Serb by birth, the Prologue
says — and came from there to Pskov. He began his labours on Snetnaya hill
at the monastery of the Mother of God near Pskov, then moved to a more
secluded place on the little river Tolva, to the monastery of Euphrosynus,
and at last withdrew to complete silence in the Krypetsk wilderness, fifteen
versts from Tolva, settling alone by a small lake in impassable forest; the
hermit’s food was bread and water, and on Wednesday and Friday he ate
nothing, and he suffered much from unclean spirits but always overcame them
by prayer. After some years lovers of the desert life gathered to him and
begged him to found a monastery and build a church of John the Theologian;
he refused to be abbot and gave the government to the monk Cassian, and
many came to the strict elder from Pskov, whom he healed and admonished,
taking gifts from none. Once the prince of Pskov, Yaroslav Vasilyevich
Obolensky, who often visited the monastery, came with his sick wife; Sabbas
sent to say, the sinful elder Sabbas tells you, prince, do not enter the
monastery with the princess — our rule here is that women do not enter; if
you break the fathers’ commandment your princess will not be healed. The
prince asked pardon for having meant, in ignorance, to break the rule;
Sabbas went out of the gates with the brethren and served a moleben, and
the princess was healed, and at the prince’s petition Pskov gave the
monastery a charter for its land in 1487. He taught lay people to keep
chastity, reminding them of the apostle’s judgement on those who defile
the body; the rich and the judges, not to feed off the poor and to keep
justice; and everyone, often, to avoid quarrels and enmity, to keep love
and peace, and to cover other people’s faults with forbearance, so that we
too may be forgiven. The monastery kept a strict common life from the
first, and even when brethren and means enough had gathered there was
nothing in his cell but two icons, a mantle and the bast mat he lay down
on to rest — the same poverty he taught the brethren, whom he told to till
the ground with their own hands. Why do we call the ancient ascetics our
fathers, he said, when we do not live their life, as children should? They
were homeless and poor, they spent their time in caves and deserts, and for
the Lord’s sake with all their firmness subdued the flesh to the spirit,
knowing no rest by day or night. Let us love the good Lord, children, and
show our love for him not only by words and by the habit but by deeds — by
love for one another, by tears, by fasting, by every kind of abstinence, as
the ancient fathers did. The grateful prince built a bridge to the monastery
across the mosses and marshes, fourteen hundred sazhens long. After his
death, on 28 August 1495, Sabbas did not leave his monastery but defended
it many times: one night robbers came up to it and saw a venerable elder
who, staff in hand, sternly bade them repent, and in the morning they
learned that there was no such elder in the monastery and understood that
it was Sabbas himself — and the robbers’ leader repented to the igumen and
stayed to live in the monastery. He was tall, with a beard white as snow,
round, thick and not very long, and in that form he appeared in the middle
of the sixteenth century to the monk Isaiah, showing where his incorrupt
relics lay; in the same year, 1555, at the request of the Krypetsk
brethren, the Pskov priest Basil wrote his life and his feast was
established. Of his successors at Krypetsk the abbot Dositheus is
remembered. The Russian calendar keeps him with two troparia and a
kontakion — a new bright sun, the kontakion says, rose from the desert over
the glorious city of Pskov.
