# Vladimir (Sokolov)

Vladimir was born on 12 October 1883 in the village of Aleksandrovo in the
Zvenigorod district of Moscow province, the son of the priest John
Georgievich Sokolov. In 1898 he finished the Zaikonospassky church school
and in 1904 the Moscow seminary, and for many years served as psalmist at
the church of St Pimen in Sushchevo in Moscow; in 1924 he was ordained
priest to that church and served there until the Renovationists seized it,
being raised meanwhile to archpriest and awarded the jewelled cross. After
the seizure of the church, to the great grief of all the Orthodox, he was
appointed to the church of Peter and Paul by the Yauza gate, and on 26
February 1937 was moved to the church of the Protection of the Mother of
God on the Lyshchikov hill. The parishioners and priests of the Pimen
church did not accept the seizure and petitioned the authorities for its
return; on 11 March 1937 a great crowd of the Orthodox gathered before the
Pimen church demanding it back, which the authorities noted with
irritation, and on 20 April 1937 he was arrested and put in the Butyrka
prison. Your part in the mass demonstration of your parishioners at the
Pimen church on 11 March? asked the investigator. — The demonstration did
take place, but I took no part in it; it was organised to get back the
Pimen church, which had shortly before been handed to the Renovationists.
— Did you talk with your parishioners about the need for a campaign
demanding the return of the church? — I did; when they put the question to
me I told them it would probably be a waste of time, since there had not
been one case of a church taken from us and handed to the Renovationists
being given back, but that if they wished to get it back they should seek
it lawfully, through the Moscow soviet. — The inquiry has evidence that at
your instigation your parishioners spread provocative anti-Soviet rumours
which led to the mass demonstration of churchmen on 11 March; do you
confirm it? — My parishioners did spread various rumours, all to the
effect that the Pimen church must at all costs be taken back from the
Renovationists, and in the end that led to the demonstration, but I
personally incited and instigated no one; when asked what to do to get the
church back I only said they must act in an organised and lawful way
through the Moscow soviet. — How was your directive on an organised demand
carried out? — On my advice two delegations were formed, one of men and
one of women, which went in an orderly way to the Moscow soviet with
petitions demanding the church’s return; what they achieved I do not know
to this day. — Do you admit guilt? — I admit that churchmen did gather
round me, but I do not hold that grouping counter-revolutionary; of
anti-Soviet activity I do not admit myself guilty. A cleric of the Pimen
church, Michael Toluzakov, called as witness, testified that Sokolov was
extremely hostile to the Soviet power, by conviction an inveterate
monarchist, whose most notable anti-Soviet activity was among children of
school age: before each school year he gathered the pupils in church and
served special molebens, went from flat to flat secretly teaching the Law
of God, and had played one of the chief parts in the demonstration of 11
March, having long before urged the churchmen to get the church back from
the Renovationists at any cost, collect signatures and petition the Soviet
organs. The indictment, signed on 26 May 1937 by Radzivilovsky, head of the
Moscow NKVD, said that in his everyday counter-revolutionary practice the
accused had worked on school-age children in a counter-revolutionary
church spirit, intimidating them in every way, held special molebens in
church for the beginning and end of the school year, drawing large numbers
of schoolchildren through their parents, and gone from flat to flat
secretly teaching them the Law of God. On 2 June 1937 the Special Board of
the NKVD sentenced him to five years in a corrective labour camp; he was
sent by stages to the Ukhta-Pechora camp at Chibyu and after a short stay
further north to the Vorkuta camp, where he arrived on 22 June 1937. He
died in the Vorkuta camp on 9 September 1940 — 27 August of the old style,
the day the Russian calendar keeps him — the feast of Pimen the Great,
patronal feast of the church in which he had served almost all his life,
and was buried in an unknown grave at Vorkuta. The Russian Church numbered
him among the saints in 2005; the life is by Igumen Damascene (Orlovsky),
and the calendar keeps him also in the synaxes of the saints of Moscow and
of Komi.
