# Theodotus (Levchenko)

Theodotus — Theodosius Levchenko in the world — was a native of the
village of Chertorich in the Glukhov district of Chernigov province, of
plain Cossack stock, and was never taught to read; his father, an honest
and good Christian, raised him in the fear of God and put into him from
childhood a love of the holy and virtuous life. When by God’s will the
time came for him to leave the world he obtained a paper allowing him to
live freely anywhere in Russia and left his home for ever, so that from
then on he was as if dead to it; he went where Providence led him, and
came to the Glinsk hermitage, then a small brotherhood, and was set to the
brethren’s kitchen, where he began his long ascetic life. He took to the
work with zeal, doing all that was given him — chopping wood, carrying
water and slops, cleaning the kitchen of its dirt, washing the pots, the
heaviest and dirtiest of kitchen work — hearing the cooks’ orders with a
child’s simplicity and doing everything without a word; in all his long
years in the monastery, even when he was head cook, he had no cell of his
own where he could rest his body, worn by labour and ascesis and sickly,
but always lived in the kitchen, with none of the fittings of an ordinary
human dwelling, and lived so for about seventy years. Nor was his ascesis
outward labour only: a Christianly wise simple man who could not read, he
prayed hard and fervently, and novices heard him at night in the kitchen
making countless prostrations and crying now to the Mother of God, most
holy Theotokos, save us, now to Christ, Jesus, Son of God, have mercy on
me, and went away long before he had finished. Seeing his simplicity and
kindness, some people, instead of loving him, treated him roughly and with
contempt and often beat him, as he himself later told and some of the
brethren saw; bearing cruel blows and every kind of ill-usage without
murmur, to mortify the old man in himself, he was freed from the passions
and burned with zeal for God. It was seen afterwards that for his tireless
labours and guileless patience he had reached, by God’s grace, the height
of dispassion and received the gift of not fearing the spirits of evil,
looking with a seer’s eye on the devil’s designs, which he told the
brethren about and tried to break; the gift of clairvoyance showed in him
plainly, and the brethren and all who knew him held him in deep
reverence. Then the abbot [Philaret](/saints/philaret-danilevsky) made
him a rasophore with the name Theodotus.

The abbot, a man of great virtue and grace, treated him with deep respect
and ascribed great power to his prayers for the house, telling the
brethren more than once: see God’s favour to our house, brethren — we live
now without particular want, the Lord sends us all we need, and it is the
prayer of the elder Theodotus that draws that favour on us humble men. And
indeed his prayer rose to God’s throne: the elder
[Macarius](/saints/macarius-sharov), then dean, coming out of church
during the morning service, suddenly saw a pillar of light over the
brethren’s kitchen, understood in the spirit whence it came, went quietly
into the kitchen corridor, and looking through a crack the old latch had
worn in the door saw Theodotus on his knees before the icon of the Saviour
with his hands raised, a shaft of flame-like light going from his mouth to
the icon and lighting the wall where it hung; he stepped back in awe, and
told it afterwards with great reverence to like-minded brethren. Others of
the more reverent brethren also saw him at prayer in wonderful states —
some lifted from the ground, some lit by an unearthly light; he prayed
often behind the kitchen stove, where his big wooden cross with the
Crucified hung on the wall, and when he prayed before it a bright radiance
came from the cross. So his life passed in labour, temptation, patience
and prayer; the brethren trusted and loved him, a childlike purity marked
him, and at times he covered his humility with folly. In May 1859 he felt
an extreme weakness and had to leave the place of his labours, the
kitchen, for the monastery’s apiary, where in a narrow cell, lying on
straw, he ended his long and much-suffering course; ten days before, he
foretold the day of his death. In those last days he was anointed and
several times received the Body and Blood of Christ, and when the days he
had named were out he was clothed in the angelic habit of the mantle and
named Theodosius again. On Thursday 16 July, with a few of the younger
brethren present, he lay quiet and still on the floor, his fading gaze
fixed on the icon of the Saviour, and at four in the morning, when the
hour of parting came, he raised his hand with an effort, signed himself
with the cross, and with a bright face gave his spirit into the hands of
his Lord, falling asleep like an innocent child. At that hour the monk
Dositheus, the sacristan, shutting the church after matins, suddenly heard
a wonderful, sweet singing that rang out loud in the air over the apiary
and then, rising above the forest, grew fainter and fainter until it was
gone. His labours, patience, suffering and warfare with the spirits of
evil, and his deep humility, are fully known only to the all-seeing Lord,
for whom from youth he renounced not only the world and what is in it but
his own body, which he wore down with cruel privations to his last breath,
and his own will. The Council of Bishops of 2017 entered him in the
calendar of the Russian Church, which keeps him on 9 September of the old
style, 22 September, in the synaxis of the Glinsk fathers.
