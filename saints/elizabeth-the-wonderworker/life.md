# Elizabeth the Wonderworker

Elizabeth was chosen for the service of Christ from her mother's womb, the life says: her birth was announced to her mother by a divine revelation, which told her beforehand that the child she would bear was to be a chosen vessel of the Holy Spirit. From childhood she gave herself to the service of God and became a bride of Christ, and she lived in the angelic order, in the company of virgins — that is, of nuns — wearing her body down with fasting and with labour. She was given the gift of healing sickness, and not the body's only.

Bodily illnesses she healed by prayer; human souls she healed with words and with counsels breathed into her by God, leading them towards repentance and towards every good work. Her clothing was a single rough hair garment and her body was numb with cold, but her spirit burned always with the flame of the love of God. Set as abbess over the sisters, she showed great diligence and cared for their salvation.

Her abstinence was without measure. For many years she ate no bread but lived on greens and vegetables, and she never tasted oil or wine in the whole of her life. Many times she kept a fast of forty days, like the great Moses, tasting nothing at all. For three years, following the publican's humility, she did not lift her bodily eyes to heaven, while with the eyes of the soul she looked always towards God on his high and lifted-up throne, surrounded by the Seraphim. When she raised the midnight prayers alone, she was lit up from above with a heavenly light.

She was a wonderworker too. She killed a fearful dragon with prayer; she healed a woman who had bled for many years; she drove unclean spirits out of men, and did many other wonders, in her life and after her death. Her grave worked wonders and gave healing to the sick, and even the dust taken from her relics gave sight to the blind. The page gives no year and names no place.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day, and saint.gr's account gives her a country, a century and a name with a reason behind it. Elizabeth came from Heraclea in Thrace and lived in the fifth century. Her parents, Eunomianos and Euphemia, were famous and of great name, renowned for their wealth and celebrated for their virtue; they lived near Heraclea, at the place long called Thrakokrene and later Abydenoi, taking Job for their model, and open-handedly helped everyone in material need out of a passionate wish to imitate the hospitality of Abraham. But sixteen years had gone by since their marriage and they were still childless.

There was an old custom in that country for the Christians to gather at the memory of the martyr Glyceria and keep a whole week's feast; and there her parents were, among the rest, making processions and all-night doxologies and visiting the churches of the city where the relics of the forty holy women and of the deacon Amos and of many other saints were kept, and carrying Glyceria's own venerable skull in procession. During the liturgy, which the city's bishop Leo was celebrating, Eunomianos saw the holy skull now smile and now grieve; he took it for a sign of his faith in the martyr, and his soul filled with joy and sorrow together. With his wife he begged the victorious saint to loose the bonds of their barrenness; and when sleep took him for a while he saw Glyceria, who said: why do you put me to trouble, man, and ask of me what God alone can give? Yet if you truly give your word that you will get a humble heart and spirit and will never boast at others' cost, I will pray that the bountiful Lord may give you soon a girl, and you shall call her Elizabeth, for she will be shown like in soul to the mother of John the Forerunner and Baptist.

He agreed, and the saint sealed him with the sign of the Cross and went; his wife conceived at once, and after nine months bore a girl. When Elizabeth was twelve her mother died, and three years later her father, and she was left an orphan; but she entrusted herself at once to God and was marked out in the service of the poor and the least of her brethren, giving her property to the poor — laying it up with God by their hands — and freedom to her slaves. Then she left for Constantinople, to the monastery of the great martyr [George](/saints/george-the-trophy-bearer) called the Little Hill, where a paternal aunt of hers was abbess; and there she renounced the world, was tonsured, and lived in hardship, fasting and asceticism, walking barefoot, never letting her body be washed with water but keeping it clean by bathing it daily in the unfailing springs of her tears.

So she came to the heights of holiness, and God counted her worthy of foresight and of wonderworking. Two years later the abbess departed this life, having appointed Elizabeth her successor; and the patriarch Gennadius I installed her. She filled with light those who came to her in faith. Once, while the liturgy was being celebrated in the church, she saw an indescribable light flash out and the All-Holy Spirit come down after the Cherubic hymn into the sanctuary and cover the priest who stood before the holy table; she was flooded with awe and astonishment, and told nobody of it until the time of her own departure to God had come.

As her hour drew near her longing to see her own country grew, as she said herself. So she came to Heraclea and venerated the holy churches of the saints there; and in the church of the Mother of God she saw in a vision the Theotokos, who received her — and she recognised that face in an icon when she reached the church of the hieromartyr Romanos. The voice of the All-Immaculate called her to go back to her monastery, because the time of her falling asleep was close; and so Elizabeth returned, and fell asleep in peace. Her holy relic was buried in the church of St George, and remained whole and untouched.

The Serbian church keeps her on this day, and the Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović gives her the house she entered and the year she left it, where neither of the calendars read for this folder before gave either. In her early youth she entered the monastic life **in the monastery of Saints Cosmas and Damian at Constantinople**, and took on herself a heavy labour for the sake of Christ God and of her own soul. She counted herself the bride of Christ and this world as though it did not exist.

Out of her great love for God came her great mercy towards men, and especially towards the sick and the suffering; and by the gift God gave her she healed various diseases and various torments in people. At her night prayers she was seen all lit with the light of heaven. After her death her relics too were healing, so that through the centuries a multitude of the sick and the afflicted gathered at her grave. She reposed in peace and passed into the eternal joy of her Lord in 540 — **the first year this folder has carried**.

The Russian calendar’s reading of her is a catalogue of abstinence and of wonders. She was chosen for the service of God before she was born, it says: her mother had a revelation that the girl would be a chosen vessel of the Holy Spirit, and her parents gave her to a monastery in childhood. She grew up in fasting and labour and received the gift of healing not only bodily sicknesses but sicknesses of the soul, and the sisters chose her as their abbess. She wore a garment of sharp haircloth; her body froze and her spirit burned with the flame of divine love.

Her self-restraint was beyond measure. For many years she ate nothing but herbs and vegetables without bread, and never took wine or oil at all; many times she passed the whole of the holy Forty Days taking nothing whatever. In imitation of the publican’s humility she did not raise her eyes to heaven for three years, but always looked towards God with the eyes of her spirit, and at her midnight prayers a heavenly light shone round her and lit her. She killed a fierce serpent by prayer, healed a woman who had suffered many years from an issue of blood, and drove unclean spirits away from people. After her death her coffin gave healing from sicknesses, and even the dust taken from her relics gave sight to the blind.

One thing in the calendar does not agree with the year this folder carries. The day’s own line heads her «(VI-VIII)», placing her anywhere between the sixth century and the eighth, where the Ohrid Prologue gave this folder a peaceful repose in 540. The Russian window does not contradict the Serbian year so much as decline to fix it, and neither reading was altered on the strength of the other.
