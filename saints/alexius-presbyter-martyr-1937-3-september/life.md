# Alexius (Zinoviev)

Alexius was born on 1 March 1879 to the priest John Zinoviev. From 1898 to
1904 he studied at the Tula seminary, after which he was ordained priest
for the church of the village of Voskresenskoye in the Chern district of
Tula province; in 1914 he was moved to a church in the town of Efremov,
and from 1917 served at the church of the village of Storozha in Moscow
province, where in thirteen years the Orthodox came to love the kind and
zealous pastor. When in 1930, in another wave of persecution, he was
arrested on the charge of supposedly setting up anti-Soviet groups in the
village, the parishioners rose as one in his defence — almost the whole
village came to the village soviet and demanded the release of the
innocent pastor; the authorities did not free him but took him under guard
to the prison at Efremov, where he was held about five weeks, then
released after all, and he returned and served in the church as before.
In the early thirties, with collectivisation, the ruin of the peasant
farms, the forced founding of collective farms and the taking of all the
peasants’ grain, the peasants began to hide it so as not to starve; the
plan by which they were to hand grain to the state exceeded what they had
and condemned them to death by hunger. Seeing the authorities search the
village house by house for grain, the psalmist Theodore Kananykhin begged
Fr Alexius to let him store his own grain and flour for a time in the
bell-tower, and the priest, though he sensed it might end in their arrest,
could not refuse. In 1932 the OGPU began to receive reports that the grain
plan at Storozha had been met at 8.9 per cent, and that the priest, the
psalmist and three peasants who went often to church — Ivan Popov, Basil
Razenkov and Andrew Beregovsky — were to blame; on 2 December 1932 all
five were arrested and put in the Efremov prison, and searches of the
church on 4 and 6 December found two sacks of rye, a sack of millet and
fifteen poods of flour, which were taken towards the grain levy.
Questioned, the priest said he had met the psalmist and the peasants and
had said that Soviet power had strangled the individual farmer and gave
him no life, and that things had been better before; the psalmist said
part of the grain in the church was his — he had hidden it because he saw
grain being taken under the Soviet power, which had never happened under
the tsar, when no one would have hidden grain, and he had argued to the
peasants that the collective farms were serfdom and ruin; the peasants
said much the same, except Popov, who admitted only a comradely
acquaintance with the “kulaks” and that his father’s farm had been a kulak
one, and said none of the grain was his. On 19 February 1933 the OGPU
troika sentenced the priest to five years in a labour camp, and he was
sent to hard labour on the White Sea–Baltic canal; the psalmist,
Beregovsky and Razenkov got three years on the same canal, and Popov a
year of forced labour and his freedom. The heavy work so broke the
priest’s health that he was certified an invalid and released after six
months, and he went home to Storozha and served in the church again — until
in 1935 the authorities seized it for a granary, after which he served
molebens in the houses of devout parishioners, and so until the new wave
of persecution in 1937. On 16 August the NKVD questioned a false witness,
a peasant of Storozha, who said the priest had supposedly been one of the
most active organisers of a counter-revolutionary rising in the village
and had said that no one should join the collective farm, that collective
farming was contrary to God, that godlessness would flourish in it and God
be forgotten, that on his own farm a man was his own master and there
would be a bondsman and a slave; that back from his sentence he had
renewed his ties with the kulaks and served molebens and vigils in their
houses; that in the autumn of 1935, after the villagers had resolved to
pour grain into the church, he had told the faithful it was a sin — a
church is God’s house where God’s word is preached; grain should have
granaries; the church must be cleared and served in, and the Lord would
punish them for the blasphemy; that in June 1936 he had said the Lord was
punishing them for blaspheming and molebens for rain were needed, so that
the church council went to the village soviet to ask leave for a moleben
in the fields; and that after a service in a house that June there had
been talk of the soviet elections and he had said that God-fearing, good
people should be elected. On 24 August 1937 he was arrested and put in the
Taganka prison in Moscow. The investigator said the inquiry had material
that in 1935 he had systematically held services in believers’ houses; he
confirmed that he had, and that the believers of Storozha had gathered at
them. Had he held counter-revolutionary talk after the services? He had
held the services, but had never held any anti-Soviet talk. Charged with
using the house services to talk about the founding of collective farms
and to show discontent with the life around him, he answered that he had
served in private houses and, staying afterwards, had talked of various
things, and had said in particular: life is hard now, and especially for
me. On 15 September the NKVD troika sentenced him to be shot; he was shot
the next day, 16 September 1937 — 3 September of the old style, the day
the Russian calendar keeps him — and buried in an unmarked common grave at
the Butovo range outside Moscow. The life was compiled by the igumen
Damascene (Orlovsky) for the lives of the new martyrs of the Moscow
diocese.
