# Cyriacus the Anchorite

Cyriacus was a man who cultivated patience and meekness, the Greek synaxarion says, and that is why he succeeded in the ascetic life. He was born at Corinth in the fifth century, the son of a priest, John; his mother was Eudoxia, and his brother was Peter, archbishop of Corinth. Of a priestly family, then, he went young to Jerusalem and from there to the lavra of Euthymius the Great, who made him a monk and sent him to the ascetic Gerasimus. When Gerasimus died, Cyriacus went back to Euthymius’s lavra, until a disturbance there drove him to the lavra of Souka, where at forty he was ordained priest and put in charge of the vessels.

What marked him among his fellow monks was the calm with which he met them, and all of them took him for an example. At seventy he left Souka too, and went patiently round many monasteries and sketes, living in the strictest asceticism. He died at a hundred and seven. The verse makes him an eater of squill, the bitter sea-onion, who shut his eyes on the twenty-ninth. The Greek calendar keeps him with three apolytikia and a kontakion, one of the apolytikia being the desert-dweller’s common «Τῆς ἐρήμου πολίτης».

The Romanian calendar keeps him on the same day with a troparion, which is that common, and two kontakia, and says of him in a line that however old he grew he loved labours and struggles, standing at the services with zeal, and was never found sitting idle, but either praying or working at something.

The Russian calendar keeps him on the same 29 September, as «Прп. Кириака отшельника», and dates his repose 556. Its index of names places him: «Кириак Палестинский, отшельник», Cyriacus of Palestine, the anchorite.

The Serbian church's Ohrid Prologue keeps him first of the four it enumerates on this day and gives him much the longest of its four entries, and it is the fullest account of him any of the four calendars prints. His father John was a presbyter and the local bishop Peter was his kinsman — where the Greek entry makes Peter, archbishop of Corinth, his brother. It was that bishop who made him in early youth a reader of the cathedral church; and reading the Scriptures the young man marvelled at the providence of God, how He glorified all the true servants of the living God and how He ordered the salvation of the human race.

In his eighteenth year the desire of the spiritual life took him to Jerusalem, where he entered the monastery of a man of God named Eustorgius, who gave him his first instruction in the monastic life. Then he went to [Euthymius](/saints/euthymius-the-great), who saw in him a future giant of the spirit, clothed him in the schema and sent him to the Jordan to [Gerasimus](/saints/gerasimus-of-the-jordan), with whom he spent nine years. After Gerasimus's death he returned to Euthymius's monastery and stayed there ten years in silence, and after that changed place for place, fleeing the glory of men.

He struggled also in the monastery of [Chariton](/saints/chariton-the-confessor), where at last he ended his earthly course, having completed a hundred and nine years of life — where the Greek entry makes it a hundred and seven. He was large and strong of body and stayed so into deep old age for all his hard fasting and vigil, and in the desert he sometimes lived for years on raw herbs alone. He was zealous for the Orthodox faith and confuted the heresies, the Prologue says, and the heresy of Origen above all.

Of himself he used to say that since he became a monk the sun had never seen him eat, nor seen him angry with anyone. By Chariton's rule, the entry adds, the monks ate only once a day and that after sunset. He was a great light, a pillar of Orthodoxy, the pride of monks, a mighty healer of the sick and a gentle comforter of the sorrowing; and having lived long to the profit of many he passed into the eternal joy of his Lord in 557 — a year the Russian calendar prints as 556, and the Greek and Romanian not at all.
