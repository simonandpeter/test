# Ilarion Felea

Ilarion Felea was a preacher of the gospel greatly valued by people and
intelligentsia alike in the middle of the last century, professor at the
Theological Academy of Arad, and the author of many theological works, the
chief of them Towards Tabor, which Father Justin Pârvu called the best work
of Romanian Orthodoxy so far. He was born on 21 March 1903 at Valea Bradului
in Hunedoara county, where his father was priest; he went to school in his
village, then to the lyceums of Brad and Arad, studied theology at the
Andreian Academy in Sibiu from 1922 to 1926, and taught a year at Brad
before his ordination on 29 July 1927. He was parish priest at Valea
Bradului until 1930, when he won by competition the parish of Arad-Șega,
newly cut off from the town centre: a new church in raw brick, a modest
parish house, a cultural house that had been the confessional school, and a
people of Orthodox Romanians, most of them craftsmen and workers at the
Astra and UTA factories nearby — and with the toughness of a fighter for
church and nation, a devout server at the altar and a shepherd who lays down
his life for his sheep, joining the altar and the pulpit to the school desk
and the cultural house, he soon won their hearts. In those nine years he
kept up his studies and his writing, begun as a student in 1924: a
licentiate at Bucharest in 1932 on salvation in the Orthodox, Catholic,
Protestant and sectarian conceptions, a doctorate in 1939 on repentance,
and books on conversion, on the Baptist heresy, on God and the soul in
contemporary Romanian poetry, on drunkenness, and on the icons of
Orthodoxy. From 1 January 1939 he was priest at Arad-Centre, the cathedral,
and from 1 October 1938 professor of dogmatics and apologetics at the
Theological Academy of Arad, for a time its rector, teaching dogmatic and
moral theology, ascetics and mystics and homiletics; he edited the diocesan
review Biserica și Școala from 1939 to 1945 and the popular sheet Calea
mântuirii from 1943, and these were the most fruitful years of his life.
Always well documented, bold and penetrating, speaking in a voice of thunder
and the spirit of a prophet, stripped of every sweetness, he drew a great
number of intellectuals to prayer and the church and kept close to them; his
cycle of books of sermons and meditations set out to answer the question
“what is Christianity?” — The Spirit of Truth (1942, a second edition in
1943, crowned by the Romanian Academy, and read from the lectern at Matins
where a parish had no priest), and The Religion of Love (1946) — and he
printed a catechism in four editions, books on the sacraments and on
salvation, the diocesan calendar, and an anthology of church chant.

Then came the years of the Thursday of the Passion, the materialist and
atheist dictatorship. On 3 March 1945 he was taken with a number of men of
culture and religious life and held in the camp at Caracal until July. On
the morning of Epiphany 1949, having finished blessing the houses, he was
taken to a foul, crowded cellar, interrogated, held alone in a cell on the
second floor kept for political prisoners, moved to the Timișoara prison,
and on 28 October 1949 sentenced to a year for “failure to denounce”, which
he served partly in the old cells of Aiud, coming out on 5 January 1950. He
worked in the diocesan library, and on 1 July 1952 was restored to the
cathedral parish, where he served until 25 September 1958, when he was
taken to the Ministry of the Interior at Uranus in Bucharest, put through a
very severe and unjust investigation, moved to Cluj, tried in secret, and on
14 March 1959, on unsupported testimony and with six other priests of Arad,
sentenced by the military tribunal of Cluj to twenty years’ hard labour and
eight years’ loss of civil rights for “plotting against the social order”,
and twenty years’ heavy prison for “intense activity against the working
class and the revolutionary movement”. He served it at Gherla and then at
Aiud, where he died on 18 September 1961, and was buried without a cross
and without anyone knowing where his remains rest — crowned by his death a
martyr for church and nation, as his forebears the moți had been before
him. The Romanian calendar keeps him on 18 September with a troparion and
kontakion — a torch of right belief, the kontakion says, sprung from the
soil of Crișana.
