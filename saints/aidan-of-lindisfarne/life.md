# Aidan of Lindisfarne

Aidan was a native of Ireland — then called Scotland — and a monk of Hy,
Iona, the great monastery his countryman Columba had founded. When the holy
king Oswald of Northumbria asked the bishops of the Scots for a man of
episcopal character to preach the faith to his pagan Anglo-Saxon subjects
and plant the church among them, the first sent was of a rough and austere
temper and could do little good; soon forced home again, he laid the fault
on the rude and indocile dispositions of the English. The Scottish clergy
called a synod to consider what to do, and Aidan, who was present, told the
prelate as he blamed the obstinacy of the English that the fault lay rather
in him, who had been too harsh and severe with an ignorant people, who ought
first to be fed with the milk of milder doctrine until they could digest
solid food. At those words the whole assembly turned its eyes on him as a
man endowed with prudence, the mother of the other virtues, and he was
appointed to the great and arduous mission. Oswald received him most
graciously and gave him for his see the isle of Lindisfarne — Holy Island to
later generations, for the saints who lived and lie buried there; eight
miles round, an island only at high water — where he fixed his see and
founded a monastery in 635, the second year of Oswald’s reign. From it all
the churches of Bernicia, from the Tyne to the Firth of Edinburgh, had their
beginning, and some of those of Deira to the south; and since the see of
York had stood empty for thirty years after Paulinus left it, he governed
all the churches of the Northumbrians for seventeen years. Of his humility
and piety Bede gives an edifying account and proposes him as a pattern for
the bishops and clergy who came after. Those who travelled with him had to
spend the time reading the Scriptures or learning the psalms by heart; he
neither sought nor loved the goods of this world, and the presents the king
and other rich men gave him he distributed among the poor or spent redeeming
captives; he rarely went to the king’s table, never without one or two of
his clergy, and after a short meal always hurried away to read or pray in
the church or his cell; from his example even the laity took up the custom
of fasting until three in the afternoon on Wednesdays and Fridays outside
the fifty days of Pascha. Bede admires his apostolic freedom in reproving
the proud and the great, his love of peace, his charity, continence and
humility, and all the other virtues which he not only practised but by his
spirit and example passed to a rough and barbarous nation, imbuing it with
the meekness of the cross; the discipline of Lindisfarne came from the
eastern monastic rules and was very austere, its monks drinking nothing but
milk and water until wine and beer were allowed them in 762. He died on 31
August 651 at the royal villa near Bamburgh, a little after the death of his
friend the holy king Oswine, Oswald’s successor in Deira. At the moment of
his death, the Greek synaxarion says, an innocent young shepherd on the
hills above — Cuthbert, later bishop of Lindisfarne — saw his blessed soul
rising to heaven in an unearthly light, and the vision moved him to give
himself to God as a monk. He was buried first in the cemetery at
Lindisfarne, and when the new church of St Peter was built there his body
was translated into it and laid at the right of the altar; Colman, the third
bishop, going back to Scotland after the synod of Whitby, carried part of
his bones to Iona. The Roman martyrology names him on this day, and the
Greek calendar keeps him on 31 August among the saints of Britain.
