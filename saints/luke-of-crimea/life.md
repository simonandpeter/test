# Luke of Crimea

Archbishop Luke, who bore in the world the name Valentin, son of Felix, Voino-Yasenetsky, was born on 14/27 April 1877 in the town of Kerch, the old Panticapaeum, at the eastern end of the Crimean peninsula. His father, a man of astonishing purity of soul, was a very devout Roman Catholic who always went to church and prayed long at home; his mother, Orthodox, prayed earnestly at home but seems never to have gone to church, being scandalised by the greed of the priests and the quarrels among them.

As a boy Valentin showed a talent for painting, studying at the gymnasium and at the Academy of Arts in Kiev at the same time; painting drew him like a magnet, and when he had finished the gymnasium he decided to study at the Academy of Arts in Saint Petersburg. In that time his faith began to show itself strongly: every day he visited the Lavra and the other churches of Kiev, and painted people while they prayed or sketched devout pilgrims. Yet the unrest within him did not stop. He himself says that he took a passionate liking to the teaching of Leo Tolstoy and became, one may say, a hardened Tolstoyan, sleeping on the floor on a rug and mowing grass and rye beside the peasants in the summer; but after reading Tolstoy's forbidden work *What I Believe* he concluded that Tolstoy was a heretic, very far from authentic Christianity.

Alongside his studies he searched the holy Scripture with particular diligence. Many places in the Gospel held him and he underlined them in red ink, but nothing could compare with the deep impression made on him by Matthew 9:36–38. "My heart leapt," he said, "and I whispered: O Lord, art thou short of labourers? Later, when I had become one of the labourers of the Lord's vineyard, I was sure that this text of the Gospel was the Lord's first call to me to give myself to his service."

Having entered the medical faculty of the University of Kiev he took an enormous interest in anatomy, and from the second year his fellow students had decided he would become a professor of anatomy, which came about twenty years later. In the third year he was chosen head of his year, passed his examinations with the highest marks and received a diploma of merit; and when the diplomas were given out Valentin was set on becoming a country doctor to help the poor, at which his fellow students exclaimed in astonishment: "What, you a country doctor? You are a scholar by vocation!"

Before he could be posted as a communal doctor the Russo-Japanese war broke out. The young doctor offered to serve as a volunteer in the Red Cross detachment, and on 30 March 1904 set out for the Far East, the journey by train taking a month. At Chita, where he worked as a surgeon in a military hospital, he met Anna Vasilevna Lanskaya, the daughter of the manager of a Ukrainian village, who was working there as a nurse and became his wife. Before the end of the war he was appointed doctor in the little town of Ardatov, near Sarov, the place where Saint Seraphim had laboured.

In 1909 he went alone to Moscow to widen his studies and to prepare his doctoral thesis, leaving his family with relatives. There he worked and served his apprenticeship in the surgical clinic of Professor Diakonov, who let him work on the method of local anaesthesia, which he strove to perfect by various researches; by his method he carried out five hundred and thirty-eight highly successful operations at only thirty-three years of age. In 1916, living then at Pereyaslav, he defended his doctoral thesis on regional anaesthesia at Moscow, for which the University of Warsaw gave him the great Hoinatsky prize, worth nine hundred gold roubles.

Valentin, by then chief physician at Tashkent, was arrested by the Bolsheviks; the painful news reached his family and shook irreversibly the frail health of his wife Anna, who died. Many thought his wife's death was the reason he embraced the faith and soon after decided to be ordained, but it seems the decision had been forming in him a long while and had grown at the pace of his religious understanding. He was a man of perfect purity of soul, as severe and unbending in keeping the commandments of Orthodoxy as in establishing scientific truth.

On 26 January 1921 doctor Valentin was ordained deacon by Archbishop Innocent of Tashkent and Turkestan, and a week later, on the feast of the Meeting of the Lord, was made priest. He received the cross of the priesthood in a very difficult time. He remained director of the General Hospital of Tashkent, operated every day, and taught his courses at the medical school always dressed in a cassock and wearing the cross; on Sundays and feasts he served in the cathedral of Tashkent, and the bishop allowed him to preach and to hold gatherings with the faithful where he discussed the questions of the faith, criticising materialism and atheism in particular.

In the summer of 1922 a schismatic group, the "Living Church", backed by the secret services, chose its own bishop for Tashkent, and Archbishop Innocent had to leave. The exiled bishop of Ufa, Andrew, came to Tashkent, having had the agreement of Patriarch Tikhon of the canonical Church — then sorely tried by the persecution — to choose and consecrate bishops. Father Valentin was tonsured a monk by bishop Andrew and received the name of the holy apostle and evangelist Luke; and on 18/31 May 1923 the new hieromonk Luke was consecrated bishop at Samarkand by two exiled bishops, Daniel of Bolkhov and Basil of Suzdal, which Patriarch Tikhon agreed to and without hesitation declared lawful.

On the evening of Saturday 9 June 1923, at about eleven o'clock, bishop Luke was arrested by two Bolshevik commissars of the secret services. "So began for me," he wrote, "the eleven years of prison and exile." In December 1923, during his stay in the Taganka prison at Moscow, groups of prisoners were formed for deportation to Siberia, and in the middle of the winter of 1924 the exiled hierarchs reached the town of Yeniseisk; then followed a fearful martyrdom on the journey to the Arctic Circle in dreadful weather. At the end of January 1926 he returned at last to Tashkent, to his family and his parishioners, and served in the church of Saint Sergius of Radonezh, without being given any place again at the faculty; in 1927 his resignation from the episcopal rank was accepted and he put in his papers for a pension.

On 23 April 1930 he served for the last time in the church of Saint Sergius, which was burnt down while bishop Luke was under arrest and in prison for the third time. On 13 June 1930 the charge was made known to him — the killing of Professor Mikhailovsky — which he rejected, declaring a hunger strike. At the end of 1933 he was set free and went to Moscow, but every door he knocked at stayed shut, and so he took the advice of one of the hierarchs and went to the Crimea. In the autumn of 1934, after ten years of preparation, his famous study *Essays on the Surgery of Septic Infections* appeared, and produced enthusiasm among his fellow doctors throughout the country; and the years 1935 and 1936 were calm and quiet for him, working hard at the hospital, teaching and often invited to congresses.

In 1937 he was arrested a fourth time and questioned by the N.K.V.D. for thirteen days and nights, from 23 November to 5 December. During the first exhausting interrogation, on 5 December 1937, when he had almost lost his senses, he was forced to sign the record of the questioning, which he afterwards denied, refusing to accept that he was guilty: "I was pressed insistently to admit that I was a spy." He tasted also the bitterness of betrayal by men close to him, such as the archpriest Michael, a close and beloved fellow worker, who did not hold out under the tortures, gave way and brought lying accusations against him. After the torments of prison, the tortures and the interrogations, he was sentenced to three years of exile in Siberia, and so went far from his family for the third time.

In March 1940 the exiled bishop, a famous professor, found himself in Bolshaya Murta, a small and abandoned little town, where he tried to find work at the small hospital in exchange for a plate of food and a bed to rest in. At the end of the summer of 1941 he was put in charge as chief physician of the military hospital 15-15 at Krasnoyarsk and made responsible for all the hospitals of the region, where he remained two years. From the spring of 1942 the behaviour of the authorities towards him changed: his successful operations, his researches and new scientific discoveries, his lectures and his addresses at congresses had increased the medical world's regard for him. In 1946 he received the Stalin prize of the first class for his *Studies in Septic Surgery* and his *Late Resections in Cases of Infected Wounds of the Great Joints*, and between 1945 and 1947 he wrote a theological book in apologetic form, *Spirit — Soul — Body*.

In May 1946 Luke was moved to the Crimea as archbishop of Simferopol and the Crimea. Though he was seventy he came determined to work for his flock without compromising with the Power; by the end of the 1940s sixty-eight churches had been opened, though they lacked much — holy vessels, vestments, service books. He celebrated every service with great reverence and attention, without haste, without shortening or summarising, and instructed his priests by continual circulars to celebrate daily and to pray for their parishioners. When the five hundredth anniversary of the autonomous Russian Church was kept from 9 to 17 July 1948 and all the primates of the Orthodox Churches were invited, Luke was not among those invited, which confirmed his suspicion that "the N.K.V.D. had given orders that he be kept at a distance".

He held it a chief duty to preach Christ everywhere and always, and preaching was the thing he gave himself to from the beginning: in thirty-eight years of priesthood he composed a thousand two hundred and fifty sermons, of which seven hundred and fifty were recorded and gathered into twelve typed volumes of some four thousand five hundred pages. The Theological Academy of Moscow called that collection an extraordinary phenomenon in church life and in contemporary theology, and chose its author an honorary member. In 1957, being blind, he dictated his memoirs to his secretary E. P. Leikfeld.

On 11 June 1961, the feast of All the Saints who have shone out and sanctified Holy Russia, while the faithful were preparing for the Liturgy, "archbishop Luke drew breath twice strongly and twice more weakly, and so his soul took flight to heaven". Honoured as a new unmercenary physician and worker of wonders, the hierarch and doctor Luke was canonised in 1996, and his holy relics were laid in the cathedral of the Holy Trinity at Simferopol, where they work wonderful healings. In 2013 the Holy Synod of the Romanian Orthodox Church decided to enter his name in the calendar of this Church too, with his commemoration on 11 June; and a part of his relics is in Romania, at the chapel of the C.
