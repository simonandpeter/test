# Mary, niece of Abraham the Recluse

Mary was the only daughter of the brother in the flesh of [Abraham the Recluse](/saints/abraham-the-recluse). When her father died she was left an orphan, and those who knew the family brought her, seven years old, to her uncle. He gave her the outer of his two cells and kept the inner for himself, and through the little door between them taught her the Psalter and the other holy books. She lived as a monastic as he did, in fasting and prayer and all the labours of that life, and her father's property, which was ample, he ordered divided among the poor at once.

She would beg her uncle to pray God to deliver her from the devil's many snares, and in everything she came to resemble him in the monastic life, so that he rejoiced to see her labours, her tears, her humble mind, her meekness and her love for God. Twenty years she lived beside him, the life says, like a clean lamb and an undefiled dove. Then the devil set snares for her, thinking that in this way at least he might trouble her uncle and draw his mind away from God.

There was a monk who was a monk in name only and had no works. He came to the saint as if to profit from him, and seeing the girl wished to speak with her, for his heart had caught fire with love of her. He tempted her about a year, until with satan's help he came into her cell and deceived her. When the sin was done she was struck with horror, tore her clothes and beat her face, and in her distress would have strangled herself, saying to herself that she had died in her soul and lost her days, that her monastic labour and abstinence and tears had come to nothing, that she had angered God and destroyed herself.

She said also that she had cast her holy uncle into great shame and grief; and she asked where now was his teaching, and the teaching of his friend Ephrem, who used to tell her to take heed to herself and keep her soul undefiled for the immortal Bridegroom, since her Bridegroom is holy. She dared not look at heaven any more, being dead to God and to men, and could not stay where she was, for how should she, full of uncleanness, speak with her holy father. She got up at once, went to another city, changed her dress and went into an inn.

Two years she stayed there, while her uncle prayed for her day and night; he had seen her fall and her recovery in a vision of a dragon that swallowed a dove and then burst and gave it back alive. After two years he learned where she was, dressed himself as a soldier to hide his face, rode to the inn and asked the host to show him the beautiful girl who was there. When she was brought and he saw her in her harlot's finery he kept back his weeping so that she should not know him and run away.

They sat and ate, and she embraced him and kissed his neck, and smelling the sweetness that came from his body worn with ascetic labour she remembered the first days of her continence, sighed and wept and cried, "Woe is me!" The host said that in two years he had never heard a word of grief from her; she answered that she would have been happy to have died before those years. In the room afterwards her uncle caught her hands and threw off his soldier's cap and wept over her, asking where her angelic look was gone, and why she had not told him when she sinned.

She became like a lifeless stone in his hands, afraid and ashamed. He entreated her until midnight, saying that he would answer for her at the judgement and take her penance upon himself, and she said she could not look at him for shame, and how should one defiled with unclean deeds pray to God. "Your sin be upon me," he said. At last she laid her head on his feet and spent the night asking how she could repay him; and in the morning, told to leave behind the gold and the clothes she had there because they were the enemy's, she left them and went.

He set her on the horse and led her home, and shut her in the inner cell where he had lived, and took for himself the cell she had had. She put on a garment of hair and lived in humility and weeping, in abstinence and meekness, calling on God to help her and repenting with such earnestness that our own repentance, says the life, is a shadow beside hers. God, who wills that none should perish, had mercy on her and forgave her, and as a sign of her forgiveness gave her the gift of healing the sicknesses of those who came to her. Her uncle lived ten years more and was comforted by what he saw in her.

After his repose Mary lived five years in great abstinence, praying to God with tears day and night; those who passed near at night often heard the voice of her measureless weeping and lamentation, and stood, and glorified God. Having repented to the end and pleased God, she passed over in peace.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day as her uncle, 29 October, and tells her story as part of his. Past seventy, saint.gr says, Abramius could give himself to the saving of sinful women, and once he had the good fortune to draw his own brother's daughter out of the mire of sin. He saw her in an inn, loaded with jewellery and in the company of dissolute young men, and she did not know him. The young woman had gone astray but had not quite thrown off her devout memories; and the next day she went to the old ascetic and asked his blessing. He answered that the blessing of men is of no use at all when God is obliged to withhold his own. Those words shook Mary: she repented, confessed, and from then on lived a holy life. The epigram the page sets over her name turns the same thing round: having left the lovers of the flesh, Mary, you are mystically embraced by the lover of souls.
