# Eulalios

saint.gr's calendar for 22 Οκτωβρίου names Eulalios and begins by saying how little it can promise. Information about him comes from the chroniclers Leontios Machairas and Florios Voustronios; of his life and of his work as a bishop nothing at all is recorded; and the years in which he flourished are unknown. What the page sets out, it says plainly, is what two local traditions tell and what it could find in his office, published for the first time by the Byzantinist K. Hatzipsaltis in the ninth volume of the Bulletin of the Society of Cypriot Studies.

One tradition, widespread, makes him bishop of Edessa in Syria, the city where the image of the Holy Mandylion was kept. When a pagan successor of king Abgar resolved to destroy that image and told the bishop so, Eulalios took the Mandylion secretly by night and fled, walked all night, reached the shore and boarded a ship bound for Cyprus. A storm rose as the ship neared the island; the bishop drew his treasure out of his breast, made the sign of the cross, spread the Mandylion on the raging sea and sat down upon it, and the sea grew calm and the waves carried him to Lambousa, where he built the monastery to which he gave the image, and which was therefore called the monastery of the Acheiropoietos, the image not made by hands.

A second Cypriot tradition, the page says, holds that Eulalios the bishop of Lambousa has nothing whatever to do with the Eulalios who was bishop of Edessa, and is an unrelated person born and brought up at Lambousa. By that account he was God's man from childhood, taught by devout parents to love the sacred letters and virtue; he was marked out while still young for his holy and exemplary life, was ordained deacon and then priest, and when the old bishop of the city died clergy and laity with one voice called him to that office, which he took with humility and the fear of God.

He distinguished himself as a bishop, the page says, and died at a good age, and his death was felt through the whole district and mourned over the whole island; people who had been helped by his love and kindness ran from everywhere to see him once more and take his blessing. A church was later built in his name near the sea, a short distance from the monastery of the Acheiropoietos; it was destroyed in the raids the rich city suffered, and in the sixteenth century another was raised on its ruins in a Franco-Byzantine manner by «ὁ Νεόφυτος τῆς Λευκωσίας Ποιμήν», which still stands in good order outside.
