# Paraskeva of Iconium

The Russian church calendar of the Сретенский monastery heads the day with her, under the first of its three service marks — «Вмц. Параскевы, нареченной Пятница (III)», the great martyr Paraskeva, called Friday. The name is the whole of the first thing it says about her. Her parents, both of her life pages agree, kept the day of the Lord's sufferings above the other days of the week, and when a daughter was born to them on a Friday they called her by the Greek word for it, Παρασκευή; so the woman carried the Passion in her name before she came to it in her body.

She was of Iconium in Asia Minor, of a rich and devout house, and was left an orphan early. Grown, she took a vow never to marry, wanting her life spent on God and on bringing the light of Christ to the pagans among whom she lived. The second of the two lives dates what followed: in the year 300 an officer of the emperor Diocletian came to the city with orders to destroy the Christians. She refused to sacrifice to the idols, and was tied to a tree and torn with iron nails until the wounds went to the bone, then thrown half-alive into prison. God healed her there, and the healing taught her tormentor nothing: he had her hung up again and burnt with torches, and at last beheaded with the sword. Christians buried her, and healings were given from her relics.

What the calendar then adds is about Russia rather than about Iconium, and it is the longer half of the first life. She has been loved there with a particular love, and a crowd of customs and observances gathered on her day. The old Russian month-books write her name in exactly the form the calendar still prints: «Святая Параскева, нареченная Пятница». Churches dedicated to her were called Pyatnitsy, and so in time were the small wayside chapels. Common speech called her Pyatnitsa, Pyatina, Petka. Her icons were kept and adorned above others, and the icon painters gave her one fixed likeness — a severe ascetic, tall, with a radiant crown on her head.

She is held in those customs to be the guardian of a household's peace and happiness, the patron of the fields and the herds, and a healer of the heaviest sicknesses of soul and body. On her day fruit is brought to the church to be blessed and then kept through to the next year as a holy thing, and she is prayed to that the cattle be spared the murrain. The calendar prints three hymns under her name, two troparia and a kontakion, and all three are hers: they put in her mouth the words the lives give her — cut my body with the sword and burn it with fire, for I go rejoicing to my Bridegroom.
