# Ninian of Britain

Ninian — Ninyas — the apostle of the southern Picts, was the son of a prince
among the Cumbrian Britons, who lived in Cumberland and Galloway. From his
cradle his only delight seemed to be to visit churches, to talk of heavenly
things and to be busy in devotion; while others took pains in their
education to advance themselves in the world, this noble youth, knowing the
inestimable treasure of the faith he had found, thought no labour great that
could improve his soul in the knowledge and practice of religion, and bade
farewell to the world, cutting off the very root of covetousness, sensuality
and ambition, left court, friends and country and made the long journey to
Rome, where he spent many years in the exercises of heroic Christian virtue
and the study of the sacred sciences. Running that race with a giant’s
strides, his soul daily more inflamed with love and zeal for God’s honour,
and moved with compassion for a native country that had received the faith
more slowly and imperfectly than the south of Britain, he came home at last
to share with his countrymen the blessing in which their happiness lay:
those few who had some tincture of the faith he taught to value it and
cultivate it with all their strength, he brought the idolaters of the
province into the paths of eternal life, softened the fierce temper of
Tudovald, king of the Picts, and built a church of stone at Whithorn in
Galloway — the northern Britons having never seen a building of stone, the
town took from it its name, the White House, Candida Casa, as Bede and
Malmesbury say. He fixed his see there and dedicated the church to St
Martin, whose tomb he had probably visited on his way through Gaul; he
converted the Cumbrians and all the provinces of the southern Picts as far
as the Grampians, the northern Picts being left to Columba in 565, and the
church of Whithorn became a seminary of apostolic men and many glorious
saints. He died on 16 September 432, was famous for many miracles, and his
relics were kept with veneration in the church that bears his name at
Whithorn until the change of religion. Butler’s sources are his acts, Bede’s
history in its third book, the Saxon Chronicle, Alcuin, William of
Malmesbury, Fordun, Ussher and the Bollandists. The Greek synaxarion keeps
him on 16 September with the line that it has no details of his life.
