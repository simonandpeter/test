# Naum of Ohrid

Naum of Ohrid was one of the five disciples of Saints [Cyril](/saints/cyril-the-philosopher) and [Methodius](/saints/methodius-of-moravia), the apostles of the Slavs, whose day is 11 May; the five are known also as the five followers. doxologia.ro calls him a wise teacher, a guide of monks, a hard ascetic, a man of prayer and a spiritual father who worked wonders, and says that he lived from 830 to 23 December 910. About the year 843 he became a disciple of Cyril and Methodius and went with them to the mission in Upper Moravia.

About the years 867 to 868 he was ordained priest at Rome, and afterwards returned to Moravia, where he helped Cyril and Methodius as a priest in their missionary work. In 885 he was exiled from Moravia, after a time spent in prison, through the malice of the German clergy of that region. He is one of the founders of the school of literature at Pliska. In 905, after Saint [Clement](/saints/clement-of-ohrid) had been ordained bishop of Drembica-Velika, Naum withdrew to the shore of Lake Ohrid.

A multitude of monks gathered round him there from all over the Balkans, for he was known as a wonderworker while he was still alive; and there he founded the monastery of Ohrid, which was later to carry his name and to hold his relics. In that monastery he translated the holy Scripture from Greek into Slavonic. He fell asleep in the Lord on 23 December 910, and doxologia.ro says that his wonderworking relics work a multitude of wonders to this day for those who pray with faith, above all healings of grave illnesses, and especially of madness. The troparion the site prints for him is in four rhymed Romanian lines and carries no tone.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and adds a journey to the west. saint.gr makes Naum a fellow worker of [Cyril](/saints/cyril-the-philosopher) and [Methodius](/saints/methodius-of-moravia) in the ninth century, in their struggles for the spreading of the Christian faith in Bulgaria; and in that heavy work, where they met great obstacles, his presence counted for much, because to the force of his teaching he added the impression made by the wonders he worked by the grace of God. Returning from Rome, where he had gone to the Pope Adrian of that time, he passed also through Germany, where there were many and various heresies, and having preached there too for Orthodoxy, he came back again to Bulgaria. There he organised with others a body of inward mission and worked most warmly by his preaching and his teaching tours. Death found him on his feet, labouring to his last breath.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps him twice, and says so itself: his principal feast is 23 December — the day the Romanian and Greek calendars keep him on — and the Julian 20 June is his summer feast. What it prints on the summer day is not a life but an account of the feast. There is a great gathering of the people at the monastery of Saint Naum, it says, and many sick come, or are carried there, to receive healing over the saint’s relics through faith and prayer. And not only the Orthodox come: people of another faith come too, to seek help from him.

Then it gives one instance, with names and a year. A Muslim of Resen brought and gave the monastery a bell, in 1926, out of gratitude that the saint had healed his brother and raised him up again to life from his deathbed. The giver was called Džemail Zizo, the healed brother Sulejman Zizo, and both were respected citizens of Resen. The year is the bell’s and not his, and nothing in his dates is changed by it; what the entry adds is the shape of his veneration a thousand years on, and the two names of the men it happened to.
