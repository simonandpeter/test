# David of Euboea

David of Euboea is one of the great wonderworking saints, doxologia.ro's page begins, and he was called "the Elder" from his youth on account of how far he had gone in the spiritual life. He was also called "the child of the Forerunner", because of the close bond he had with Saint John the Baptist, who appeared to him many times in a wonderful manner from his early childhood and led him up the ladder of perfection.

He was born in 1591 in the village of Gardinitsa in Locris. His father was a priest and took him with him to the services, and the child was often seen standing before the holy icons, and longest of all before the icon of Saint John the Baptist, to whom he felt himself especially drawn. Before he was two years old, asked what he liked best in church, he answered: "The throne of Jesus Christ, the eyes of the Mother of God, and the wings of Saint John the Baptist." His parents took note of the words and grew more certain that the boy had been chosen by God for a high service.

The most striking thing in his childhood happened when he was only three. On a Tuesday morning Saint John the Baptist appeared to him in his sleep and asked whether he would come to his house. "Where is your house? I know you stay in the church, in your icon," said little David. "I have a house too. Follow me and you will see." He took him by the hand and led him out of the village to a little church dedicated to the Baptist, and when he stood before his icon Saint John entered wonderfully into it, and then told him that his father would come on Saturday to fetch him.

The family, not finding him, were beside themselves, and the priest's wife nearly lost her mind. On the Saturday his father came with the faithful to serve the Liturgy in the church of the holy Forerunner, hoping that the church's protector would help them find David; and great was the joy of the priest and of his wife and of the rest when they found the child standing in the church with a face full of light and shining eyes. After this his devotion to Saint John grew, and he would go to his church and stand for hours before the icon, talking with him.

At four he learned to read; at five he began to study the service books, though the services were written in the old Greek; and enlightened by God he could by eight interpret all of them, being drawn most of all to the hymns of Saint John of Damascus. He spent much time on the Epistles of the Apostle Paul and on the Acts of the Apostles. At fifteen, when the call to a life of fasting and struggle was becoming plain to him, God called him wonderfully to the narrow way: Saint John the Baptist appeared and told him to set out the next day after the Liturgy to find his spiritual guide. Leaving his family, David met the father Acacius, a man of prayer and fasting who had come to great spiritual measures.

For some years he lived at Acacius's monastery in Magnesia, where he was tonsured and given the obedience of caring for the church, and he shone by his virtues and his wisdom. Then Acacius took him to the Holy Mountain of Athos, and they went about the monasteries and many of the sketes, visiting many venerable fathers; and afterwards Acacius let David choose a place to live, and he chose the Great Lavra, the Lavra of Saint Athanasius the Athonite.

While David was struggling there, Acacius went to Constantinople, where the ecumenical patriarch asked him to accept the rank of metropolitan, and after nearly two years he was consecrated metropolitan of Nafpaktos and Arta. He then wrote asking the abbot of the Great Lavra to send David to him, and when they met again told him that he wished to consecrate him bishop. David refused firmly, and seeing that, the metropolitan resolved to ordain him priest; after his ordination he became the metropolitan's counsellor and the spiritual father of the young men who wished to become priests.

Being close to the metropolitan, David did not shrink from drawing his attention to certain wrong things that some of the priests were doing, and he did it wishing only that things should be put right, not out of slander. When at one point he complained to the metropolitan of the decayed state of a monastery, the monastery of Vernikova, he was given the obedience of being its abbot, precisely in order to try to set it in order.

While he was struggling with the habits of some stiff-necked monks who would not give up their passions, a very heavy trial came on him. Some Greek children were living in the house of a rich Turk who had the right of life and death over them, and when they managed to escape the Turks searched as hard as they could to learn who had helped them, and were told that David was the guilty man, who was known not only for his love of God but for loving his own people. The Turks came to the monastery, seized him, put him to all kinds of torture, threw him into prison, and then hung him up by the hands, leaving him barely alive.

He was set free by the care of some wealthier Christians, who gave money to the governor there. But weighed down by the thought that perhaps one of his own unworthy monks had given him up to the Turks, he did not go back to Vernikova, and set out for Euboea, the island where he had been born. Coming near the island, he asked a boatman to carry him across, and the boatman, seeing his shabby and torn cassock, refused; so David spread his cassock on the water and went over on it as on a raft, under the boatman's eyes, who understood that he had a man of holy life before him.

On Euboea he stopped near Rovies, beside an old church dedicated to the Transfiguration. He found a cave near by and lived in it two years; and when afterwards a young shepherd asked to be received as his disciple, he thought of raising a monastery. Water was very hard to find in that place, and he prayed God to work a wonder and make water spring up so that the monastery could be built; and where he struck a great stone with his staff, the next day, when the stone was moved, water sprang out.

After that wonder he took his disciple, whom he had tonsured, and went to gather the money needed for the building. They travelled through Wallachia and Moldavia and got as far as Russia, and everywhere they found people who offered to help them. Everything they were given was sent to Euboea in a log hollowed out inside; and by God's wonder the log floated to the shore of Euboea, and all that had been gathered came safely to David.

After some years he raised the monastery and formed a fairly large brotherhood. The monks, taking his example, tried to follow him in his labours, and the fame of the monastery spread everywhere, and many people ran to the holy abbot to ask a word of profit or to confess; he spoke to them much of the importance of confession and of the fight with sins, and men found in him the right physician for the illnesses of their souls.

A great wonder he worked before his passing happened in the village of Distos. Passing through it, the faithful, knowing his boldness before God, asked him to pray for them that they might be rid of the crowds of mosquitoes that troubled them all the time; and after he had prayed on his knees together with them, God not only drove the mosquitoes away but dried up the marshes round the village, to everyone's joy. Three days before the end of his earthly life he told the monks of his brotherhood that God had revealed to him the day he would die; and on the third day, after he had encouraged them to stand firm in their monastic promises, he gave up his last breath. The page gives no year for his death, and says that by his death he did not go away from his brotherhood and from those who had known him, but receiving grace from God protected them and helped them by the many wonders he worked.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 1 November, and gives his family and his teacher. He came from the village of Gardinitsa, near Talanti, a place by the sea opposite Euboea, saint.gr says, and he lived when Jeremias was Patriarch of Constantinople, about 1519. His father was called Christodoulos and was a priest, and his mother Theodora; he had one more brother and two sisters. From small he showed an exceptional form and learned the sacred letters excellently; and at fifteen he put himself under a holy elder, Akakios, who trained him in the virtues of the monastic life. From then on, the page says, the venerable David ran a spiritual course his biographer calls formidable, teaching virtue by practice and working various wonders; he foresaw his own death and departed in peace and in great holiness on 1 November. His life was written by his disciple the monk Christophoros and his office by Neophytos, bishop of Talanti, from Athens; and there is a monastery of the venerable David in Euboea.
