# Paul of Constantinople

When Constantius, the son of Constantine the Great, held the sceptre of the Greek empire, the Arians raised a persecution against the Orthodox, having the emperor to help them, for he was taken in by the same heresy. The Church of Christ was then in great trouble and had few pillars left standing: [Athanasius of Alexandria](/saints/athanasius-of-alexandria), the chief defender of Orthodoxy, had been driven from his see, and Alexander, Patriarch of Constantinople, had exchanged this passing life for the everlasting one.

As Alexander lay dying his flock stood about his bed and asked him to whom he was leaving them. He set two men before them: Paul, a priest, of a Thessalonian family, and Macedonius the deacon; and he said that if they wanted a learned shepherd shining with good works they should choose Paul, and if they wanted one only of fair appearance and outward ornament, Macedonius. Then he departed to the Lord. In the assembly that followed the Orthodox wanted Paul and the Arians, who were many there, wanted Macedonius; the Orthodox side prevailed, and Paul was chosen patriarch in the church of Saint Irene.

Constantius was at Antioch when it was done, and coming back to Constantinople he grew angry that Paul had gone up to the throne without his will. Urged on by the Arians, he gathered an unjust council and deposed him, though he was guiltless and pure of heart and of great use to the Church of Christ, being in wisdom and in life a light to the world and shining in the Church like a morning star among the clouds. In his place the emperor set Eusebius of Nicomedia, and went back to Antioch.

Eusebius, being a heretic, began to trouble the Church with his unjust teaching, and laboured with all his strength to strike the word *homoousion* — of one essence — out of the confession of faith, so that the words *begotten, not made, of one essence with the Father* should not be read; for he did not confess Christ the Son of God to be equal with God the Father.

Paul, after his deposition, went to Rome, whose Church then held the right faith, and found there Athanasius and other bishops whom Eusebius had driven out, and stayed with them. Eusebius wrote to Pope Julius of Rome so that Rome too should give them no peace; but Julius read the letter and saw the lying slander of God's guiltless servants in it, counselled Athanasius, Paul and the other bishops to go back to their thrones, and wrote to the bishops of the East to receive them with love and not to hinder them. The bishops went home with those letters. Eusebius meant to gather a council at Antioch and answer the pope, but he died before he could; and the Orthodox people of Constantinople received Paul with joy and led him into the church.

He was driven out again. Sent under guard to the town of Cucusus in Armenia and shut up in a church there, he was set upon by the Arians while he was serving the divine Liturgy and strangled with his own omophorion, and so gave up his soul to the Lord. Macedonius went up on the patriarchal throne again and did unspeakable harm, driving out and killing the Orthodox and putting heretics of his own in the bishops' places; with the prefect Philip to help him, women who kept the faith had their breasts cut off with knives, others had their mouths forced open with iron and the Arian communion put in by force, others lost their noses and ears or were branded with hot iron. Two of Paul's own notaries, Marcian and Martyrius, defenders of the true faith, were put to the sword.

When Macedonius sent three hundred armed men into Paphlagonia to force the faithful there into Arian communion, the people of the town of Mantineum came out against them with axes and scythes and bars, and a great number fell on both sides; and when he dared, without the emperor's order, to dig the relics of Constantine the Great out of the ground and move them elsewhere, the church was filled with blood. Hearing of it the emperor was angry, and put Macedonius out of the patriarchal throne and Philip out of his office.

The heresy of Arius and of Macedonius still did harm to the Church for forty years more, until the reign of Theodosius, who gathered a council of holy Fathers at Constantinople, undid the heresy, raised up the right faith, and brought the relics of Paul, the blessed confessor of Christ, with great honour from Cucusus of Armenia to Constantinople. The Romanian life gives no year anywhere.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 6 November, and fills in the places. saint.gr makes him a native of Thessalonica, born at the beginning of the fourth century, and secretary to the most holy patriarch of Constantinople Alexander, whose memory it puts on 30 August; when Alexander died Paul was elected in his place. The Arian emperor Constantius, returning from Antioch, put him off the patriarchal throne and proclaimed Eusebius of Nicomedia patriarch on his own authority, and Paul went to Rome, where he found [Athanasius the Great](/saints/athanasius-of-alexandria), whom Constantius had likewise driven from Alexandria. The emperor Constans, hearing of it, wrote to his brother in protest, and both men were restored; but Constans soon died, and Constantius ordered Paul removed again and exiled him to Koukousos in Armenia. There, as he was celebrating the divine Liturgy, Arians rushed upon him and strangled him with his own omophorion. The page adds that his incorrupt relic was brought to Constantinople in 385 under Theodosius the Great, laid first in the church of Saint Irene and then in one built in his honour; that it was carried to Venice in 1226; that it was lost and found again in 1493; and that at the recognition of 1686 the Bollandist C. Jannik described it whole. Part of his skull is at the monastery of Simonopetra on the Holy Mountain, a fragment at the Lavra of Saint Alexander Nevsky in Saint Petersburg, and the body itself in the Roman Catholic church of San Giorgio Maggiore in Venice.

days.pravoslavie.ru keeps him on the same day as the other two calendars, 6 ноября ст. ст., and its line is «Свт. Павла исп., патриарха Константинопольского (350).» — the only one of the three to print a year. Its life agrees with them in outline and differs from them in three places. It dates the death of the patriarch he succeeded, «Александр», to 340, where saint.gr's text says 377; it says he was strangled with his own omophorion while he was serving the Liturgy at Кукуз in Armenia «в 350 году»; and it puts the translation of his relics from there to Constantinople under «Феодосий Великий» in 381, where the Greek page says 385. Both years for his death now stand in this folder's date and neither is chosen.

It also names the exiles one by one, on the authority of a contemporary: it quotes «Афанасий Великий» writing that Paul was sent first to Pontus by Constantine, then by Constantius in iron fetters to Singara in Mesopotamia, then moved from there to Emesa, and a fourth time to Cucusus in Cappadocia by the Taurus wastes — which does not agree with the Armenia of the same page's own narrative, and the page does not reconcile them. On the Arian who was put in his place it adds only that he did not govern the Church long. The last thing it records is that in 1326 the relics were carried to Venice.

The day page prints a troparion and a kontakion under his name, and they are the Church Slavonic of the two hymns this folder already carries in Greek and in Romanian — the same troparion of the third tone, with Abel and the blood of Zacharias crying out beside him, and the same kontakion of the second. Both are kept here in the Slavonic, with the English rendering the site had already made for them.

The Ohrid Prologue opens on his predecessor's deathbed. When the blessed [Alexander](/saints/alexander-patriarch-of-constantinople) lay dying, the grieving faithful asked him whom he was leaving behind as chief shepherd of Christ's rational flock, and the sick patriarch answered: if you want a shepherd who will teach you and shine upon you with the virtues, choose Paul; if you want only a comely man, outwardly adorned, choose Macedonius. The people chose Paul. That did not suit the Arian heretics, and it did not suit the emperor Constantius, who was at Antioch at the time.

He was deposed soon after, and fled to Rome together with [Athanasius the Great](/saints/athanasius-of-alexandria); there Pope Julius and the emperor Constans received them both honourably and upheld them in their Orthodox faith. On the letter of Constans and the pope he was restored to his throne — but when Constans died the Arians raised their heads and drove the Orthodox patriarch out to Cucusus in Armenia. There, serving the Liturgy one day, he was set upon by Arians and strangled with his own omophorion.

The book puts that at 351, which is one side of the interval this folder already holds and not a reason to close it: the Greek page gives 351 and the Russian 350, and the third book agreeing with one of them settles nothing. It carries the relics further than either: translated to Constantinople in 381, in the time of the emperor [Theodosius](/saints/theodosius-the-great), and to Venice in 1236, where it says they are still. It closes on his own clerics and secretaries, [Marcian](/saints/marcian-companion-of-martyrius) and [Martyrius](/saints/martyrius-companion-of-marcian), who suffered soon after their patriarch, and sends the reader to the 25 October for them.
