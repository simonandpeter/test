# Paul of Constantinople

When Constantius, the son of Constantine the Great, held the sceptre of the Greek empire, the Arians raised a persecution against the Orthodox, having the emperor to help them, for he was taken in by the same heresy. The Church of Christ was then in great trouble and had few pillars left standing: [Athanasius of Alexandria](/saints/athanasius-of-alexandria), the chief defender of Orthodoxy, had been driven from his see, and Alexander, Patriarch of Constantinople, had exchanged this passing life for the everlasting one.

As Alexander lay dying his flock stood about his bed and asked him to whom he was leaving them. He set two men before them: Paul, a priest, of a Thessalonian family, and Macedonius the deacon; and he said that if they wanted a learned shepherd shining with good works they should choose Paul, and if they wanted one only of fair appearance and outward ornament, Macedonius. Then he departed to the Lord. In the assembly that followed the Orthodox wanted Paul and the Arians, who were many there, wanted Macedonius; the Orthodox side prevailed, and Paul was chosen patriarch in the church of Saint Irene.

Constantius was at Antioch when it was done, and coming back to Constantinople he grew angry that Paul had gone up to the throne without his will. Urged on by the Arians, he gathered an unjust council and deposed him, though he was guiltless and pure of heart and of great use to the Church of Christ, being in wisdom and in life a light to the world and shining in the Church like a morning star among the clouds. In his place the emperor set Eusebius of Nicomedia, and went back to Antioch.

Eusebius, being a heretic, began to trouble the Church with his unjust teaching, and laboured with all his strength to strike the word *homoousion* — of one essence — out of the confession of faith, so that the words *begotten, not made, of one essence with the Father* should not be read; for he did not confess Christ the Son of God to be equal with God the Father.

Paul, after his deposition, went to Rome, whose Church then held the right faith, and found there Athanasius and other bishops whom Eusebius had driven out, and stayed with them. Eusebius wrote to Pope Julius of Rome so that Rome too should give them no peace; but Julius read the letter and saw the lying slander of God's guiltless servants in it, counselled Athanasius, Paul and the other bishops to go back to their thrones, and wrote to the bishops of the East to receive them with love and not to hinder them. The bishops went home with those letters. Eusebius meant to gather a council at Antioch and answer the pope, but he died before he could; and the Orthodox people of Constantinople received Paul with joy and led him into the church.

He was driven out again. Sent under guard to the town of Cucusus in Armenia and shut up in a church there, he was set upon by the Arians while he was serving the divine Liturgy and strangled with his own omophorion, and so gave up his soul to the Lord. Macedonius went up on the patriarchal throne again and did unspeakable harm, driving out and killing the Orthodox and putting heretics of his own in the bishops' places; with the prefect Philip to help him, women who kept the faith had their breasts cut off with knives, others had their mouths forced open with iron and the Arian communion put in by force, others lost their noses and ears or were branded with hot iron. Two of Paul's own notaries, Marcian and Martyrius, defenders of the true faith, were put to the sword.

When Macedonius sent three hundred armed men into Paphlagonia to force the faithful there into Arian communion, the people of the town of Mantineum came out against them with axes and scythes and bars, and a great number fell on both sides; and when he dared, without the emperor's order, to dig the relics of Constantine the Great out of the ground and move them elsewhere, the church was filled with blood. Hearing of it the emperor was angry, and put Macedonius out of the patriarchal throne and Philip out of his office.

The heresy of Arius and of Macedonius still did harm to the Church for forty years more, until the reign of Theodosius, who gathered a council of holy Fathers at Constantinople, undid the heresy, raised up the right faith, and brought the relics of Paul, the blessed confessor of Christ, with great honour from Cucusus of Armenia to Constantinople. The Romanian life gives no year anywhere.
