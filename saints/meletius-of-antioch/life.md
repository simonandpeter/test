# Meletius of Antioch

Meletius came from Armenia the Great, born of Christian parents of good family and trained in all the outward learning and in the holy dogmas of the Christian faith. He was perfected afterwards in every virtue, and for his amended life, for the burning and pure love he had towards God and his neighbour, and for his meekness, his freedom from malice and his humility, he was loved by everyone; so that the right-believing pressed him, as a worthy man, to take up the shepherding of the rational sheep.

He was made bishop first of the city of Sebaste in Armenia, and was then moved to Beroea in Syria. He came to the throne of Antioch in this way. Eudoxius, archbishop of Antioch, misled like Macedonius by the Arian opinions, coveted the see of Constantinople for its riches, which in the reign of Constantius, son of Constantine the Great, were greater than Antioch's; and when the Antiochenes learned that he despised his own church they were angry and drove him out, and he went and took Constantinople. At the council they held to choose in his place, the Arians could do much and the orthodox little — the latter were called Eustathians, after Eustathius, an earlier bishop of Antioch who had suffered exile for the faith — and the name on everyone's lips was Meletius, the Arians wanting him most of all, because they reckoned him of one mind with themselves.

They confirmed the common election with their signatures and entrusted it to Eusebius, bishop of Samosata, a right-believing man who had been at that council, and with the emperor's consent he was brought to Antioch with honour and a public welcome. Theodoret describes the entry: all who were servants of the church came out to meet him, and the whole multitude of the citizens, and there were even Jews and pagans among them who wished to see the famous Meletius.

Having taken the throne, he taught the people first the amended life and good manners, smoothing the stubborn way in their hearts towards right belief: he waited, the life says, to pull the thorns and thistles out of the fields of their hearts, so that the seed of the faith might be sown in them more easily. There he ordained [Basil](/saints/basil-the-great) the Great, who had come to Antioch from Jerusalem, to the diaconate; and [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom), then still a boy at his lessons, who had gone out with his fellows and all the people to meet him, received holy baptism from his hands, and later wrote a word of praise for him.

Thirty days after he took the see the heretics drove him out of the church. The people, pressing him hard to know for certain which confession their new archbishop held, drew from him a sermon in which he glorified the faith confirmed at Nicaea, at the first ecumenical council, and confessed that the Son is of one essence with the Father and the maker of all creation. The archdeacon of Antioch, taken with the heresy of Arius, came up to his bishop and stopped his mouth with his hand so that he should not confess or teach the right faith; and the saint, his mouth stopped by the archdeacon's palm, stretched his hand out to the people and confessed the Holy Trinity with his fingers better than with his mouth — showing first three fingers, figuring the three divine persons, and then closing them and showing one, figuring the one Godhead.

The archdeacon saw it, let go his mouth and seized his hand to stop that figure of the Trinity; and the saint, his mouth free and his right hand held, blessed the Trinity in unity and the unity in Trinity, and urged the people to hold the confession confirmed by the council of Nicaea, and assured them that whoever casts away its dogmas is wholly astray. The archdeacon stopped his mouth again and would not let him figure the Trinity even with his fingers, and the bishop, pulling free, taught the people now with his mouth and now with his fingers.

The right-believing, whom the heretics called Eustathians, rejoiced with unspeakable joy to see such a bishop on the apostolic throne. The Arians were angry, drove the bishop of God out of the church, slandered him everywhere as a Sabellian heretic, and urged the emperor Constantius to exile him to his own country, Armenia. As the governor's carriage carried him out through the middle of the city, the Christians, unable to bear parting from him, threw stones at the governor from every side, choosing rather to lose this life than to be parted from the saint; and the blessed Meletius covered the governor's head with his own garments, shaming the enemy, and taught his disciples to be patient under evil and to bear no malice against those who wrong them. Euzoius, a defender of Arius's dogmas and a deacon, was chosen in his place.

After the exile the faithful separated altogether from the Arians, taking a church outside the city at the place called Palaea, where they held their services with a priest named Paulinus. Constantius died and Julian the Apostate came after him, who at the beginning of his reign pretended to be right-believing and let all the exiled bishops go back to their sees. Meletius came back from exile and found the orthodox Church split in two: one part had elected Paulinus bishop before he came, and those were called Paulinians, the others Meletians — chiefly those whom Meletius had brought back from Arianism — and the Paulinians would not receive them into communion, both because they had been baptised by Arians and because Meletius himself had been chosen by Arians. Both parts held the true faith and were divided for those reasons alone. He set himself to the peace of the Church, and did not reject Paulinus's episcopate but honoured it, for Paulinus was meek and humble of heart.

When Julian was settled in the empire he denied Christ openly and worshipped the idols, and the saint was driven out of Antioch again, for the persecution then rose against Christians everywhere and most of all there. The emperor, marching against Persia, came to Antioch and offered many sacrifices to the idol of Apollo at the place called Daphne before the city, where the relics of the holy martyr [Babylas](/saints/babylas-of-antioch) had been laid with the three children; and when he asked the unclean god whether he should conquer the Persians, the idol gave him no answer — for since those relics had been brought there the demon had fled and the idol that had spoken much before was silent.

Julian perished, and under the right-believing Jovian Antioch had Meletius for its shepherd again; the emperor honoured and loved him, and the Arians feared him. A local council was held at Antioch by Meletius and Eusebius of Samosata, at which the Arians confessed that the Son is of one essence with the Father, and the faith confirmed at Nicaea — but in pretence only, for when Jovian died soon after and Valens came, who was deceived by the heretics through his wife Domnica, they went back to their own belief.

Then the Church of the orthodox was persecuted again and her shepherds driven out. Valens stayed no little while in Antioch, strengthened the Arian heresy, and at the Arians' urging exiled Meletius, who remained in banishment until Valens perished. After Valens came the right-believing Gratian, and the orthodox bishops were freed from exile by imperial order and took their sees without hindrance.

Meletius returned to Antioch and to his throne a third time, and the division over the two bishops, Paulinus and himself, went on among the faithful, some holding to one and some to the other; he laboured greatly to make a perfect peace between them. Gratian at that time gave an order through his whole dominion that the churches be taken from the Arians and given back to the orthodox, and a nobleman named Sapor came to Antioch with it. Meletius kept the see to which he had been chosen at the first by everyone's goodwill, and governed it well, in peace and quiet, until his blessed end, and many in Antioch grew in virtue and faith by his guidance and by the pattern of his blameless life.

In the days of this Meletius, in the parts of Syria where Antioch lies, [Symeon](/saints/symeon-the-stylite) — who afterwards went up on the pillar — began his struggle; but first he climbed a high hill and shackled himself with an iron chain. Hearing of him, Meletius went to him and, seeing him in irons, said: «A man can master himself without fetters, and be bound to a place not with iron but by his own will and understanding.» Symeon took the good of it, took off the shackles, and bound himself of his own free will, to be willingly bound for Jesus Christ.

Not many years before his end the right-believing Theodosius came to the throne, who had been under Gratian an honoured general, famous for his courage. Before he received the empire he had once seen in a vision the archbishop of Antioch, this Meletius, whom he had never seen in the flesh but only heard of, standing near him and putting the imperial cloak and crown upon him. Not many days later the vision was fulfilled, for Gratian, unable to govern East and West together, made the general Theodosius his partner in the empire and gave him the whole East. Coming to Constantinople, Theodosius wished to see Meletius, and when many bishops came into the palace he looked at them and knew him at once, left the others and ran to him like a beloved son, kissed his hands and his breast and his head, told everybody of the vision, and honoured him above all the other bishops.

At that council Meletius satisfied everyone by these signs. The Arians, not understanding the Holy Trinity, had overturned the world with their pagan teaching; and Meletius rose and showed the people who asked teaching of him three fingers, in the figure of the three persons of the Holy Trinity; then, joining two and bending one, he blessed the people, and fire like lightning came out from him, and he cried: «Three hypostases we understand, but of one essence we speak.» So he astonished them all, shamed the heretics and strengthened the right-believing in the true faith; and in that same council he confirmed [Gregory the Theologian](/saints/gregory-the-theologian) on the patriarchal throne of Constantinople.

A few days later, while the council was still sitting, Meletius fell ill and fell asleep in the Lord, causing much weeping to the emperor, to the bishops and to all the right-believing people, who buried him with honour. Afterwards he was carried to Antioch and laid near the hieromartyr Babylas, for the defence of the city. The page gives no year for his birth or his death, and its «Tropar» tab is a Greek-language audio page carrying no text, so no hymn is recorded here.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and fills in a chronology the Romanian page had not. saint.gr puts his birth about 310 at Melitene in Lesser Armenia; has him elected bishop of Sebaste by a synod in 358 and resign at once before the partisans of his predecessor Eustathius; made patriarch of Antioch in 360, and exiled to Armenia after a single month when the Arians persuaded the emperor Constantius to replace him with Arius's old associate Euzoius — which is how the party called the Meletians came to exist. It quotes [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom) twice: on how unshakeable the teaching he planted at Antioch in so little time proved, and on the scene at his removal, when the crowd began to stone the governor who was carrying him off into exile and Meletius, unable to stop them with words, stood up and shielded his own persecutor with his body. Exiled again in 365 and in 371 to Getasa in Armenia, near the Cappadocian border, he was often in touch there with [Basil the Great](/saints/basil-the-great); he returned to Antioch in 379 and at once held a synod confessing Nicaea and condemning every heresy. Called to preside at the Second Ecumenical Council in 381, he died of illness before its work was done; the emperor attended the funeral, [Gregory of Nyssa](/saints/gregory-of-nyssa) gave the oration, and his relic was later carried in great procession to Antioch and laid in the tomb of [Babylas](/saints/babylas-of-antioch).
