# Antonina (Novikova)

She was born in 1880 at Gorki in the Zaraysk district of the Ryazan province, into the family of Andrew Novikov, who owned a brickworks and land and fourteen summer houses that he let. From the age of eight she lived with an aunt in a monastery at Ryazan, where she learnt to read, and she grew up a religious girl and in time entered the Trinity and Maria monastery in the town of Yegoryevsk. That house had been formed in 1900 by the labour and the gifts of Nikifor Mikhailovich Bardygin, a baker's son who got his knowledge by work and by stubborn, thoughtful reading and became the owner of mills in Yegoryevsk. Brought up in deep piety, he was in church at the first stroke of the bell, stood the liturgy through, read the Epistle and sang in the choir; lovers of the old church singing gathered round him until a fine choir was formed that afterwards sang in the Dormition cathedral. He was elected mayor from 1872 to 1901 and the town owed him almost all its improvement — its water supply, its paved roads, its street lighting, its fire brigade, its barracks, its gymnasium, its girls' school, its public garden and its house of industry — and the monastery was built entirely at his own cost.

Antonina entered the house soon after its founding, as did [Nadezhda (Kruglova)](/saints/nadezhda-kruglova), and both lived in it until the godless authorities closed it in 1918, after which they settled in Yegoryevsk, earned their bread by needlework and helped about the church, keeping the whole monastic rule. That last was what the witnesses testified at their arrest and what the OGPU made the chief charge against them.

They were arrested on 19 May 1931 and shut in the Yegoryevsk prison; thirty nuns and novices were taken in the same case. The charge was that after the closing of the Trinity monastery in 1918 they had stayed on in Yegoryevsk, had gone on living as nuns and keeping the monastic rule to the present day, and that some of the nuns had bought houses of their own which became the regular places of their gatherings and their rule. In 1930, the charge went on, a general meeting of two villages had resolved to close the old cemetery and use the building there as a club; the nuns then agitated among the peasants against the closing, and the same general meeting revoked its resolution; and of late religion had risen among the peasants and the number of believers had grown. The nuns who were questioned did not admit themselves guilty of anti-Soviet agitation. They admitted only that since leaving the monastery they had gone on living as nuns.

On 29 May 1931 an OGPU troika sentenced them to five years of exile in Kazakhstan. They returned to Yegoryevsk in 1935, which is four years and not five, and the life does not reconcile the two; it says both and this folder says both. There they took one room together, and Nadezhda, as the younger, took Antonina onto her keep. They lived wholly given to the service of God and kept the whole rule, but after the exile they were careful and tried not to form ties with unbelievers. In the authorities' understanding the monastic life was itself the crime, and the secretary of the town soviet wrote in Nadezhda's character sheet that she had been deported before for being a nun.

The two were arrested again on 1 March 1938 and held in the Yegoryevsk prison for the questioning. The investigator told Antonina that the organs of the investigation knew she was set against the Soviet power and slandered the power and the collective farms. She answered that she had never said anything against the Soviet power and had not slandered either it or the farms. Asked whether she had committed anti-Soviet acts in 1937 among those who lived in the same house, and whether she had slandered the collective farms in January 1938, she answered each time that she had never engaged in anti-Soviet activity, had not spoken against the power, and had held no conversations and uttered no slander about the farms; and that she did not admit herself guilty of the charge brought against her, because she had not said those things. Nadezhda was questioned in the same days with the same charges and the same questions and rejected everything brought against her too. Then the duty witnesses were questioned — the owners and the neighbours of the house where the two lived — and they signed the false testimony the NKVD had composed. The investigation closed on 8 March, and on 11 March a troika sentenced both to be shot. They were taken to the Taganka prison in Moscow, shot on 20 March 1938 and buried in an unmarked common grave at the Butovo range outside Moscow, which civil day is the Julian 7 March the calendar keeps them on.
