# Chrysostom of Smyrna

Chrysostom Kalafatis was born at Triglia on the Propontis in 1867 to
Nicholas Kalafatis and Calliope Lemonidou, who had eight children, four boys
and four girls; of the boys only the firstborn Eugene, born 1865, and
Chrysostom survived, and Eugene stood by his younger brother through the whole
of his stormy life and followed him at the last to martyrdom. Nicholas knew
Ottoman law and represented his townsmen in the Turkish courts, loved church
music and was elected an elder of the community; Calliope, a devout woman,
vowed Chrysostom to the Mother of God at Epiphany 1868, when the metropolitan
of Prousa came to Triglia. His first teachers were the archimandrite and
later metropolitan Ioannikios for church matters, Gazis for Greek,
Christophoros Moumouzis for Turkish, Nicholas Hatzichrysaphis for French and
Papa-Theodosis for church music. He studied at the theological school of
Halki from 1884 to 1891, was archdeacon to Constantine Valiadis of Mytilene —
the Ecumenical Patriarch Constantine V of 1897 — and protosyncellus of the
Great Church, and in 1902 Patriarch Joachim III consecrated him metropolitan
of Drama.

At Drama his fight against the Bulgarian propaganda and for the national
spirit so troubled the Sublime Porte that it demanded his recall in 1907; he
withdrew to Triglia in bitterness, went back in 1908 when the new Turkish
constitution allowed it, was received with an enthusiasm the Porte called a
danger to public order, and was recalled again on 20 January 1909. On
11 March 1910 he was translated to Smyrna, where he went on as before,
calling a mass protest against the Bulgarian violence in Macedonia and the
Porte’s oppression of the Greeks; the local authorities had him removed in
1914, and he came back only after the armistice of Mudros in 1918. Under the
Greek administration of Smyrna from 1919 to 1922 he was the unquestioned
leader of Asia Minor’s Greeks and the inspiration of the “Asia Minor Defence”
that meant to make an autonomous state if the army failed. When the front
collapsed in August 1922 he condemned the Great Powers’ plans to clear the
Greeks out of Asia Minor, and refused to leave his people, whatever the
British and French consuls pressed on him. On 27 August 1922, after the
Liturgy at St Photini, the Turkish commandant Nureddin Pasha had him seized
and handed to the mob. The French writer René Puaux set down what a French
naval patrol of twenty men saw: Nureddin on his balcony telling the crowd,
if he did you good, repay him, if he did you harm, do him harm; the
metropolitan dragged to a barber’s shop, dressed in a white smock, beaten
with fists and sticks, spat on, stabbed, his beard torn out, his eyes put
out, his nose and ears cut off, and finished a little way further on — while
the sailors shook with rage and their officer, revolver in hand, held them to
their orders. His two volumes On the Church, his articles in Ekklesiastiki
Alitheia and Ieros Polykarpos, and the whole of his preaching show the
spiritual stature of the man.

The Greek calendar keeps him, by a synodal encyclical of 5 July 1993, on the
Sunday before the Exaltation of the Cross, with the hierarchs
[Gregory of Kydonies](/saints/gregory-of-kydonies),
[Ambrose of Moschonisia](/saints/ambrose-of-moschonisia),
[Procopius of Iconium](/saints/procopius-of-iconium) and
[Euthymius of Zela](/saints/euthymius-of-zela); the verse calls him
protomartyr, sacrificed on an ill-omened day, a multitude of fellow-athletes
following his fate.
