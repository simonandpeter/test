# Cyrus of Alexandria

Cyrus was born and brought up in Egypt, in the city of Alexandria, and was famous as a physician; he healed the body without taking payment and the soul with his words and the pattern of his life. Coming in to the sick, the Romanian life says, he spoke to them not out of Galen and Hippocrates but out of the ordinances of the prophets and apostles, telling them to keep clear of the hurt of sins, which are the cause of bodily sickness; for the sickness of the soul is graver than any sickness of the body. So he brought many Greeks to the knowledge of the true God.

When Diocletian raised the persecution, Cyrus was denounced to the governor of Alexandria as a Christian who was teaching many, and the governor ordered him seized. He heard of it and left the city and Egypt for Arabia — not from fear of torments, the life insists, but following the word of Christ, when they drive you out of this city, flee into another — and there he at once took the monastic habit. He gave up the physician's craft, because God had given him the gift of wonderworking, and healed now not with medicines and herbs but with prayer and a word.

[John](/saints/john-of-edessa), a well-believing Christian of Edessa who held rank in the army, heard of him, sought him first at Alexandria and then in Arabia, and attached himself to him with his whole soul. When a woman called Athanasia was taken with her three daughters, Theoctista, Theodosia and Eudoxia — fifteen, thirteen and eleven years old — and held at the city of Canopus, the two went there to strengthen them, fearing that girls so young might be broken by threats or coaxed away.

They were denounced to the governor Syrianus as two men, one in a monk's habit and one in a soldier's, who were telling the captive girls not to sacrifice. Brought before him, they answered that it was their custom to speak little, and that they would not deny Christ however much they suffered for him. They were scourged, broken with staves, burnt with torches, their burnt limbs washed with vinegar and salt, rubbed with rough haircloth and their feet drenched with boiling pitch, partly to break them and partly to frighten the women, who were made to watch.

The mother and her daughters were then tortured in the same way, and, the life says, the weaker sex differed in nothing from the strong and unconquered men; the governor was put to shame, and had the four beheaded. Cyrus and John were brought out once more, offered gifts and threatened, and at last sentenced: that Cyrus the teacher and John who held the same faith, having despised the imperial command, should be beheaded by the imperial law.

The soldiers beheaded them in the same place where the holy women had laid down their lives, on the thirty-first day of January. Christians in hiding took the bodies and buried them with honour in the church of the Apostle and Evangelist Mark, in separate graves — Cyrus and John in one, Athanasia with her daughters in the other — and many years after, under the emperor Theodosius the Younger, the patriarch Cyril of Alexandria had their relics translated.

The Greek calendar keeps the two of them on the same thirty-first of January, and not by themselves: saint.gr's line for the day sets beside them the widow [Athanasia](/saints/athanasia-the-widow) and her three daughters, [Theodote](/saints/theodote-daughter-of-athanasia), [Theoctiste](/saints/theoctiste-daughter-of-athanasia) and [Eudoxia](/saints/eudoxia-daughter-of-athanasia), who were arrested in the same persecution. It was for those five women that the two unmercenaries came out of hiding: fearing that they might lose heart under the tortures, they hurried to them to give them courage, and were taken themselves and brought before the governor, where they declared their faith with boldness and were beheaded with the sword in the year 292. The page adds that it was Sophronius, patriarch of Jerusalem, who wrote down their wonders, because they had healed his eyes, and that their synaxis was kept in the martyrion raised in their honour in the quarter of Phorakion.

The Ohrid Prologue of Bishop Nikolai Velimirović keeps the two of them twice. On the Julian 31 January it tells the passion, and on the Julian 28 June it keeps the carrying of their relics from Canopus to Manuthin, and the wonders that followed. Its 28 June entry sends the reader back to January for the life and gives the summer day its own account instead: the patriarch [Cyril of Alexandria](/saints/cyril-of-alexandria) was praying that the idol-worship at Manuthin be undone, where an idol temple stood and a demonic power ruled.

An angel of God appeared to the patriarch and told him that Manuthin would be cleansed of its uncleanness if he brought the relics of the two martyrs to the place. He did it at once, the book says, and built a church there in their name. From the relics a boy named Ammonius, the son of the governor of Alexandria, was healed of scrofula; a Theodore of blindness; an Isidore of Maiuma of a rotting of the liver; a woman Theodora of poisoning; a Eugenius of dropsy; and many others of other sicknesses and torments. All of it, the entry closes, happened in the year 412.

That year is the year of the translation and of the healings, and not of anything else: the Prologue gives no year for the martyrdom itself on either of its two days, and the interval this folder carries is the one the other books gave it.
