# Olympia of Lesvos

In the thirteenth century, two hundred years before the martyrdom of Raphael, Nicholas and Irene of Lesvos, there was a convent of nuns on the hill of Karyes above the village of Thermi. The house was a large one, built in the eighth century and most likely by the empress Irene, who had been exiled to Lesvos; its imperial origin, the page says, was made known in a vision of 1961 to Kostas Kanellos of Mytilene, who saw the saints of Thermi with a military officer, and the officer, who said he came from Kalloni where he had lived in a monastery, explained how an empress had built the house at Karyes.

Olympia's mother came of a priest's family of Constantinople, four daughters and a son; the son became an archimandrite, three of the sisters married, and one of them, Dorothea, became abbess at Karyes on Lesvos. Olympia's mother married a priest of the Peloponnese, and there Olympia was born and brought up in the piety of the Orthodox faith. When she was ten both her parents died and she was sent to her aunt Dorothea at Karyes. There she resolved to give her whole life to the Lord; at nineteen she was tonsured, and at twenty, after her aunt's repose, she became abbess of the house and the spiritual guide of thirty nuns.

On 11 May in the year 1235 the monastery was attacked, torn down and burned — the page calls the attackers Turks in one place and pirates in another. Some of the nuns escaped into the mountains and many were killed. One of the older nuns, mother Euphrosyne, who was ill and could not walk or hide, was hung from a tree and burned alive, and nothing was left of her but ash. Olympia too chose to endure. They burned her flesh; they heated an iron rod and drove it into one ear and out the other; they tortured her with the flames of candles. Then they stripped her, stretched her on an iron door and fastened her there to a wooden plank with twenty great nails, and drove three larger nails into her head, two into her ears and the third through her skull. At the last they set fire to the monastery. She was buried with the door to which she had been nailed.

For about a hundred and fifty years nothing remained but ruins, until a pious and wealthy woman named Melpomene undertook to rebuild the house — this time as a monastery for monks. It was there that Raphael, Nicholas and Irene suffered at the hands of the Turks in the year 1463. It was the Lord's will that the holy relics should be found after nearly five hundred years, and found under the guidance of the martyrs themselves, who appeared in dreams and visions to believing people and showed them both their martyrdom and where they were to dig for the traces of the holy monastery.

When Olympia's grave was found, the nails with which she had been fastened were found with her. One of them was taken by a pious woman and kept with great care, and the thing that had once been an instrument of murder was now covered with sweet myrrh that never ran out. A monk who learned of it asked her for it, saying: «Dă-mi cuiul să însemnez cu el în chipul crucii ochii surorii mele care este în primejdie să orbească și se află la spital pentru operație» — give me the nail, that I may sign with it in the form of a cross the eyes of my sister, who is in danger of going blind and is in hospital for an operation. The wonder was done and the sick woman's eyes were healed; and so, the page says, were the spiritual eyes of many who looked and did not see.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day and tells her whole story. The venerable Olympia and the venerable [Euphrosyne](/saints/euphrosyne-of-lesvos) lived in the thirteenth century, saint.gr says, and gave up their souls by a martyr's death on the eleventh of May in the year 1235. Olympia was born of pious parents who came from Constantinople; her father was a priest and her mother a priest's daughter. From Constantinople, for a reason the page says is unknown, they left and settled in the Peloponnese. At the age of ten Olympia lost her parents, and her relatives sent her to the monastery of Karyai at Thermi — the monastery that is now Saint Raphael's — where the abbess of the time, Dorothea, was her aunt. At nineteen she became a nun, and at twenty-five, when her aunt died, she became abbess.

Ten years after that, on the eleventh of May 1235, pirates came to Mytilene. They went up to the monastery, scattered the thirty nuns, and those who could not get away in time they abused; the abbess and an old nun, Euphrosyne, they tortured terribly. Euphrosyne they hung on a tree and burned; Olympia they burned over her whole body. Long afterwards, when the venerable relics of the saints of Thermi were found and their story became known by divine revelations, together with the graves that held them, there were found in Olympia's grave the twenty nails with which they had nailed her. Part of her relics is at the monastery of Saint Raphael at Thermi on Lesvos, and a fragment at the monastery of Mega Spilaio of Kalavryta. Not one of the thirty nuns is named.
