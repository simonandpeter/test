# Damascene

Damascene — Dmitry Dmitrievich Tsedrik in the world — bishop of Starodub
and vicar of the Chernihiv diocese, was born in 1877 in the town of Mayaki
in the Odessa district of Kherson province, the son of a postal official. He
finished the seminary, then the agricultural institute at Vladivostok as an
agronomist and the Kazan institute of oriental languages; soon he was
tonsured Damascene and went as a missionary to the Peking ecclesiastical
mission. In 1914 he went to the front with a Red Cross detachment in the
Caucasus. In 1918 he was arrested in Orel province and sentenced to death,
and escaped the shooting; his brother Nicholas, a priest, was shot at the
same time for fearlessly denouncing the Bolsheviks. He lived for a while at
the St Michael monastery in Kyiv as diocesan missionary and a student of the
Kyiv academy, was made hieromonk in 1919 by Archbishop Dimitri (Abashidze)
of Simferopol, and in 1920 archimandrite and superior of the St George
monastery at Balaklava — and was arrested with Dimitri that same year, freed
after some months, and banished from the Crimea. He fought the Renovationist
schism with success, and on 14 September 1923 Patriarch Tikhon himself
consecrated him bishop of Starodub, with the government of the Chernihiv
diocese and the Hlukhiv vicariate. Arrested again and again in those years,
he was so worn by prison and interrogation that he had to anoint the people
at the vigil sitting down, and had heart attacks in the sanctuary, and
served again the next day, since the services were his one joy. Banished to
Kharkiv in 1924 and confined to the Danilov monastery in Moscow from
September 1925, he was arrested that November in the case of Metropolitan
Peter (Polyansky), sentenced by the GPU to three years’ exile in the
Turukhansk region, and after the Butyrka lived at Krasnoyarsk and at Poloy
beyond the Arctic Circle, where the agronomy of his youth served him: he
grew a vegetable garden and kept off scurvy with his own greens, and mended
a tiny half-ruined house with his cell-attendant. There the news of
Metropolitan Sergius’s Declaration of 1927 reached him, and shook him so
that he wrote some hundred and fifty letters about it and sent his attendant
out with them; in the winter of 1928 Metropolitan Kirill (Smirnov) was
brought past Poloy, and the two future martyrs met and became friends. Of
his prisons he would only say: the people there are good, I am ready to go
back this minute — holding, as many bishops did, that freedom in those years
was morally worse than confinement.

Freed in November 1928, he was received by Sergius in Moscow and at once
separated from him, joining at first the Danilov group, and wrote him a sharp
letter against his call to thank the Soviet government for its attention to
the spiritual needs of the Orthodox: thank it for what — for the
uncountable sufferings of these years, for the churches trampled by
apostates, for the lamp of St Sergius put out, for the relics of St
Seraphim and before them of Theodosius, Mitrophan, Tikhon and Joasaph
subjected to unheard-of blasphemy, for the silenced bells of the Kremlin,
for the blood of Metropolitan Benjamin and the other murdered — for what?
He settled at Starodub, accepted no appointment, and in the summer of 1929
sent a trusted man to Metropolitan Peter in exile at Khe, with the letters
of Kirill and other bishops to Sergius, asking the lawful head of the church
to rule on the questions of church life; Peter answered by word of mouth
only — you bishops must remove Metropolitan Sergius, and I do not bless
commemorating him at the services. To Kirill he wrote that God’s judgement
was being passed on the church and the Russian people, that the true
soldiers of Christ were being picked out who alone could stand against the
Beast itself, and that it was no longer possible to save the broad masses:
the little flock must be saved — without idle words and loud phrases, build
first a small nucleus of the few who thirst for Christ and are ready to
live the gospel, gather round worthy pastors, and a few people joined in
such a life are already a little church, the body of Christ in which his
Spirit and love dwell. He was arrested in November 1929 on the denunciation
of the dean of Starodub and sentenced to ten years in the camps, sent to
Solovki in June 1930 and freed in 1933, after which he led a number of
Josephite communities in Ukraine and in Russia itself, appointing deans in
Kyiv and the Vyatka diocese and gathering the little flock — underground,
but never taking off the cassock or cutting his hair or beard. Arrested
again at Kherson in September 1934 and sentenced to three years in the
Northern region, he cared there for the spiritual children of the lately
dead bishop Victor (Ostrovidov), and wrote to his own that “grandfather
Peter” had proposed to Sergius to dissolve his unlawful synod and repent
before the church; when an interrogator asked which church orientation he
held and he said he did not recognise Sergius as the lawful primate, the
chekist told him that until he stopped reasoning that way,
counter-revolutionary cases would go on being made against him. In June
1935 he wrote to the Josephite priests that the church must go wholly
underground; he was arrested at Arkhangelsk at the beginning of 1936 and
sentenced to the camps, and worked as a book-keeper in the Karaganda camp in
Kazakhstan, when parcels and letters to prisoners were already forbidden.
Moved from place to place with the transports, where those who lagged were
shot on the road, he carried his friend and fellow-prisoner the priest John
on his own back to the halt to save him from it. By decision of the
Karaganda NKVD troika of 10 September 1937 he was sentenced to be shot, and
was shot at Karaganda on 15 September 1937 — 2 September of the old style,
the day the Russian calendar keeps him, with eight presbyters shot the same
year: Euthymius, John, John, Vladimir, Victor, Theodotus, Peter and Stephen.
The Jubilee Council of Bishops of August 2000 numbered him among the new
martyrs and confessors of Russia.
