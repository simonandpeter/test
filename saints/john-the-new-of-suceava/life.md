# John the New of Suceava

John was born at Trebizond, a great and famous city in the parts of Cappadocia that border on Lesser Armenia, standing by the sea, where ships put in from everywhere. His parents were Christians, right-believing and adorned with good works, and they brought him up perfect in the Christian virtues. The citizens of that city were used to going about the sea in ships and trading for their living, and so John often went aboard ship and travelled to many cities as a merchant.

Once he took passage in the ship of a man of another country with a large trade, a Frank by birth and Latin in religion, savage in temper, unmerciful and without humanity. The enemy began to envy John's manner of life, for he saw him praying often, fasting, gentle and easy of approach to everyone, giving alms to all the needy on the ship, supplying their wants out of his own goods and comforting the sick. His eyes were always full of tears, and he would say: if you have mercy on the brother who suffers, God will have mercy on you.

So the enemy set the ship's captain against him, who was opposed to the right faith, and there were great disputes about the faith on the voyage; and John, being very skilled in books and wise, always overcame the Frank and shamed his reasoning, which made the man hate him the more. When they came to the shore of the Black Sea on the side of the Moldavian land, near Cetatea Albă, the captain went ashore to the governor of the city, who was a Turk by birth and religion and a hot defender of his own faith.

He laid this accusation before him: there is a man in my ship who wishes to leave his Christian faith and take yours; he has sworn it to me many times at sea; take charge of him and bring him over, and you will have great honour from the emperor, for he is a wise man and a famous orator, and of no small family in Trebizond. The official was glad, sat in his place of judgement, and had John called to him with honour, telling him gently that he had heard he loved the Mahometan faith, and bidding him cast off the Christian faith and curse it aloud before the people, who had gathered with their wives and children to hear him confess.

John raised his eyes to heaven while the man was speaking, and then looked at him and answered boldly that he would not be won over to a lie, and that the judge would rather learn the truth from him. When he had prayed aloud, the soldiers were ordered to strip him, and he stood naked, clothed in Christ. Never, he said, will I deny Christ my God and Maker; here is my naked body before you, ready for your torments: beat it with rods, burn it with fire, drown it in water, do what you will.

The governor, kindled with anger, had him stretched on the ground and beaten without mercy with knotted rods, until his body was broken in many places and the whole ground where he lay was red with his blood. Thanks be to Thee, Master, the martyr said, that Thou hast made me worthy to be washed in my own blood; and hearing him pray they beat him until his voice failed. In the evening he was bound with two chains and dragged like a dead man to prison, to be kept for greater torments the next day.

The next morning he came before the judge with a bright face and a cheerful soul, and the man wondered that there was any life left in him and that he seemed to have suffered nothing. He asked whether John did not see the shame his obstinacy had brought him to, and John answered that he cared nothing at all for that. Then the tyrant ordered a wild horse brought, the martyr's feet tied to its tail, and a soldier to mount and gallop through the streets of the city as fast as he could, dragging him through the whole of it.

So he was dragged through all the streets, the ground reddening with his blood and pieces of his flesh falling from him and his head striking the ground and the stones. When the horse came to the Jewish quarter of the city, the life says, he was mocked and pelted as he was dragged past; and one of that quarter ran into his house, caught up a drawn sword, came up with the saint and cut off his head. So the good soldier of Jesus Christ finished his contest. The rider untied the body from the horse's tail and left it where it lay with the severed head, unburied, and no Christian dared come near it for fear.

When night fell, many burning lamps appeared above the body, and three men in white garments sang and censed around it, and a pillar of fire stood over the relics, reaching to heaven; and many saw it. A man whose house was near, thinking Christian priests had come to take the body away, brought a bow and arrow to shoot at one of them, and the arrow and the string stuck fast to the fingers of his right hand and the bow to his left, so that he could neither loose it nor free his hands, and he stood so all night. In the morning the lights and the men vanished, people ran to see him, and he told everyone in detail what he had seen; and when he had told it many times his hands were loosed. The governor, hearing of the miracle, was afraid, and gave the Christians leave to take the body and bury it, and they buried it with honour beside their church.

Some days later the Frank who had sold him to martyrdom tried to steal the body and carry it to his own country, whether from remorse or to say afterwards that the saint had been of the Western faith. He came at night with his men and began to dig the grave open; but the saint appeared in sleep to the priest of that church and told him to run, for the Frank was taking his body. The priest ran, found the grave dug open and the body nearly taken, drove them off, and called the Christians; and they carried the relics into the church and laid them in the sanctuary near the holy table.

There they stayed more than seventy years, with lights and a pillar of fire and a sweet fragrance seen and smelled about them and many healings done, so that the report of them spread everywhere. In that time the right-believing prince Alexander, called the Good, reigned in the land of Moldavia, and hearing of the miracles he desired to have the relics with him. They were brought to Suceava, then the princely seat and capital of Moldavia, and Alexander came out to meet them with all his great boyars, with the archbishop Joseph and all his clergy and all the people, with candles and incense; and the prince fell on the coffin, kissed the martyr's hands and wept, and set him as guardian of himself, of his rule and of the people of Moldavia. The relics were laid in the metropolitan church of Suceava.

They stayed there many years, and then, when the princely seat and the metropolitan see moved to Iași, and the times were troubled, they were brought to Iași and laid in the metropolitan church there. In the year 1686, under Constantine Cantemir, prince of Moldavia, the metropolitan Dositei, fearing the country would be plundered, took the relics and the treasures of the see with him into the Polish land with the army of the Polish king John Sobieski, meaning to bring them back when there was peace. The troubles lasted, and he never did: the relics remained in the market town of Żółkiew for ninety-four years.

In 1783 they were brought back to Suceava. The life tells the whole journey: the bishop sent the hieromonk Ioasaf, once abbot of Putna, and others for them; at Żółkiew they found an imperial commissar and some thirty officers standing about the reliquary; a written certificate was drawn up and signed; the coffin travelled by carriage through the Lwów valley with an escort of hussars, and at the border sixty guns were fired in the saint's honour. Then the bishop Dositei of Bukovina, with a great many priests and the whole people, brought the relics into the metropolitan church of Suceava and set them in the middle of it, where they have remained. Doxologia's life prints no year for the martyrdom itself, only for the journeys of the relics, and none is recorded here.
