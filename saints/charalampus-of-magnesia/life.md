# Charalampus of Magnesia

In the days of Severus, the pagan emperor of Rome, the holy bishop Charalampus lived in the city of Magnesia and taught the peoples the word of God, guiding them in the way of salvation. His emperor, Jesus Christ, he told them, had sent prophets and apostles in the power of the Holy Spirit so that all men might be made wise by their holy preaching and walk straight in the way of righteousness, while their own emperor Severus had devised cruel persecutions to make men sacrifice to lifeless idols and give their souls up to death.

For those words he was seized and brought to trial before the governor Lucian and before Lucius. The governor advised him to take up habits fitting such old age as his and to approach the gods with sacrifice; Charalampus answered that he, who had grown old and finished this temporal life, would not overlook the unseen good things that were now near. They stripped the priestly vestments from him, hung him up and tore him with iron claws until the skin was torn from his head to his feet; and he thanked his tormentors, for by scraping his old body they had renewed his spirit.

The two servants who did the tearing were seized with terror, began to confess and glorify the power of Christ, and were beheaded for his name; their names, the life says, were Porphyrius and Baptus. Three women who watched the martyrdom believed in Christ and were beheaded at once as well. Then Lucius rose from his seat, took the instruments himself and tore the saint's body; and his hands broke off at the elbows as though cut with a sword and hung from the martyr's body. The governor ran up and spat in the martyr's face, and his head was twisted backwards.

The Magnesians in their fear begged the saint to turn the punishment away, and he answered that there was no malice in his heart nor guile on his tongue: Christ God had punished the lawless rulers. A great multitude believed. At his prayer a voice came out of the clouds — «Rejoice, Charalampus, light of the earth» — and Lucius and Lucian the governor were both healed; Lucius fell at the martyr's feet and asked for baptism, and the governor left off persecuting the Christians until he should inform the emperor.

Lucian went to Severus, who was then in Antioch of Pisidia, and reported that a man of the Galilean assembly was turning everyone from the gods and giving health to the sick, and that all Magnesia had received his faith. The emperor sent three hundred soldiers, who drove sharp iron nails into the whole of the saint's body, plaited his long beard like a rope, put it about his neck and dragged him by it along the road. Fifteen stadia out from Magnesia a horse on the right turned to the soldiers and spoke with a human voice, telling them to loose the man they had no power to bind. They were afraid, but carried out the imperial order.

Before the emperor three long spikes were driven into his breast, and a small fire was lit to burn him slowly so that he should not die at once. A woman standing by poured hot ashes over his head and face and beard, bidding the old man die; she was the emperor's wife, and her own sister rebuked her and turned to the martyr saying that his old age was honourable and that she herself wished to believe. The fire did not harm him. Severus asked how many years he had, and he answered that he had spent many years in this vain life, for he had lived a hundred and thirteen.

Galina, the emperor's daughter, is the other person of this life. When her father and the eparch Crispus were bound and hung in the air by invisible bonds, she begged the saint to pray, and at his prayer they were loosed. She told him a dream: a walled meadow full of sweet-smelling trees, a vine in the middle, a tall cedar, a spring at the cedar's root, and a watchman with a rod of fire who drove her father and Crispus away but carried her in on his shoulders. He read it to her — the water is the grace of the Holy Spirit, the enclosure is Paradise, the trees the orders of angels, the cedar the glory of the Cross, the spring eternal life, and the watchman is Christ, who left the ninety-nine and went after the one that was lost.

Thirty days after that punishment the emperor turned back to the idols and had a hook put in the saint's mouth to lead him by it through the whole city. Galina reproached her father, and when he ordered her to sacrifice she went into the temple of Zeus and Apollo and threw the idols down, breaking Zeus into three pieces. Fifty craftsmen made new ones in the night, and the servants told her the gods had risen from the dead as the Galileans say of their Christ; she broke those too, saying that new gods were easier to overturn than old. Charalampus was handed over to a widow for mockery, and the dry pillar he leaned against in her house budded and grew into a great tree that shaded the whole house.

When the neighbours found him there, teaching the widow, and asked whether he was the Christ, he said he was their servant and Christ's slave, and that these things were done in Christ's name; and all of them fell at his feet and were baptised. The eparch Crispus warned Severus that if the wonderworker were not quickly put to the sword everyone would follow him, and the emperor passed sentence. Charalampus sang the psalm of David — *Mercy and judgement will I sing unto Thee, O Lord* — as he went, and came to the place with gladness.

There the heavens opened and the Lord came down with a multitude of holy angels on a throne of emerald, and said: «Come, Charalampus, my friend, who hast endured much for my name. Ask of me what thou wilt.» He asked that wherever his relics should be laid and his memory kept there be no famine and no corrupt air to destroy the crops, but rather peace and bodily health, salvation of soul, plenty of wheat and wine and increase of cattle for men's need, forgiveness of their sins and deliverance from the plague. The Lord said: «Be it according to thy asking, my brave soldier.»

So the Lord went up to heaven with his angels and the soul of the martyr followed, and he died without the stroke of the sword. The soldiers told the emperor how the Lord had appeared and how they had seen the soul ascending, and he was filled with wonder and fear. The blessed Galina asked her father for the body, wrapped it in clean fine linen, anointed it with spices and costly myrrh and laid it in a coffin of gold, glorifying God; and the emperor was afraid to judge his daughter and let her live in the Christian faith as she wished. All this was done while Severus was in Antioch, and Charalampus suffered on the tenth day of February. The page gives no year.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day and gives the years the Romanian page did not: saint.gr heads him «(85 - 198)», names the emperor Septimius Severus and the prefect of Magnesia, Lucian, and says he took the unfading crown at the age of a hundred and thirteen. It tells the miracle that the day's other entries hang on — Lucian, taking his own sword to wound the saint's body, had his hands cut off and left hanging on the hieromartyr's body, and they were rejoined and the governor made whole only after the saint's prayer, at which many of the executioners believed in the true God. Two of those soldiers, [Baptos](/saints/baptos-companion-of-porphyrius) and [Porphyrius](/saints/porphyrius-companion-of-baptos), and [three women](/saints/three-women-of-magnesia) who were present, are kept on this same day for it. The page also lists his relics across some forty houses, and says that his head was given to the monastery of Saint Stephen at Meteora by Vladislav, prince of Wallachia, in 1412–1413.
