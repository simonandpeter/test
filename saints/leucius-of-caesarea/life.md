# Leucius of Caesarea

Leucius suffered with [Thyrsus](/saints/thyrsus-of-caesarea) and Callinicus under Decius (249–251) at Caesarea in Bithynia. He reproached the governor Cumbricius for his unjust persecution of the Christians, and after torture was beheaded. Thyrsus came through his torments unharmed and died in peace, and the pagan priest Callinicus, won by Thyrsus’s courage, confessed Christ and was beheaded too. The Russian calendar keeps Thyrsus, Leucius and [Coronatus](/saints/cornutus-of-iconium) “and their company” on 17 August (Julian).

The Romanian calendar keeps him on 14 decembrie, where doxologia.ro prints the long life the day's martyrs share. It opens by saying that the country of Bithynia bore the holy martyrs Thyrsus, Leucius and Callinicus and the city of Caesarea brought them up, and that they suffered under Decius when a governor named Cumbricius came from Nicomedia by way of Nicaea, a hard worker for the idols who drove everyone to his own godless communion, coaxing some and frightening others. Leucius was among the first men of the city, an honoured man known for his learning, his understanding and his good family.

Seeing what was being done, the life says, his heart hurt him and he burned with zeal for God, and he could hide it no longer: he stood before the governor and asked why he armed himself in wretchedness against his own soul, honouring heathen idols that neither feel nor hear, and drawing many into that error until men grew less feeling than the stones. Cumbricius, hating the man who spoke the truth, had him beaten without so much as a question; he took the wounds willingly and gave thanks, until those who beat him were worn out and his body was weak with wounds. He prayed to be loosed from the body, and the tyrant served that wish: he was taken far outside the city and beheaded, going, the life says, without fear and without trouble, showing no pain in his face but only joy and gladness, as though he were going not to the sword but to a crowning. The idol-priest [Callinicus](/saints/callinicus-of-caesarea), won over later by the wonders done through [Thyrsus](/saints/thyrsus-of-caesarea), is kept on the same day.

The Greek calendar keeps the three of them on the same menologion day, with their company, and adds two things and no narrative. The first is a doubt of its own: perhaps, saint.gr says, they are confused with those of 14 December, and the name Callinicus of the third martyr became Coronatus. Nothing here is merged on the strength of that; it is set down as the page's own hesitation. The second is a place: of these three, it says, it is recorded that «τελείται αυτών η σύναξις πλησίον των Ελενιανών», their synaxis is held near the Helenianai, in Constantinople. He is named on the page beside [Thyrsus](/saints/thyrsus-of-caesarea), [Coronatus](/saints/cornutus-of-iconium).

The distich the page prints is for the three together: «Οἱ τρεῖς ἀθληταὶ νῦν παρίστανται πόθῳ, / Τριάδι θείᾳ, ἧς ἐνήθλησαν χάριν» — the three contenders now stand with longing before the divine Trinity, for whose sake they contended. saint.gr gives no year, no emperor and no hymn.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the three of them on the Julian 14 December, which is the Romanian calendar's day for him and not the 17 August the Russian and Greek keep, and it gives him the first confession of the day. He and [Thyrsus](/saints/thyrsus-of-caesarea) were honourable citizens of Caesarea in Bithynia; he was baptised and Thyrsus was still a catechumen.

When the emperor Koumvrikios, the successor of Decius, began to torture and kill Christians without sparing, he went before him fearlessly and convicted him with a question: why have you made war on your own soul, Koumvrikios? The enraged judge ordered him beaten and tortured at once, and at last beheaded with the sword; and he went to that beheading, the book says, as gladly as to a wedding. It was the sight of his death that kindled Thyrsus with divine zeal and brought him before the same judge.
