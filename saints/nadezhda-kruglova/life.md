# Nadezhda (Kruglova)

She was born in 1891 at Denisovo in the Pochinki parish of the Yegoryevsk district of the Moscow province, the daughter of the peasant George Kruglov. At nine her parents put her into the parish school, where she studied three years, and afterwards she worked with them on the family holding. When she was twenty she entered as a novice the Trinity and Maria monastery in Yegoryevsk, the house a mill-owner of the town had built entirely at his own cost ten years before. There she found [Antonina (Novikova)](/saints/antonina-novikova), eleven years older, who had entered soon after its founding, and the two lives the calendar has for them are one life.

Both lived in the monastery until the godless authorities closed it in 1918, after which they settled in Yegoryevsk, earned their bread by needlework and helped about the church, keeping the whole monastic rule — which is what the witnesses testified at their arrest and what the OGPU made the chief charge against them. They were arrested on 19 May 1931 and shut in the Yegoryevsk prison; thirty nuns and novices were taken in the same case, charged with having stayed on in the town after 1918, gone on living as nuns and keeping the monastic rule, and with having agitated among the peasants against the closing of a village cemetery, so that the meeting which had resolved to close it revoked its own resolution. Those questioned did not admit themselves guilty of anti-Soviet agitation. They admitted only that since leaving the monastery they had gone on living as nuns.

On 29 May 1931 an OGPU troika sentenced them to five years of exile in Kazakhstan; they came back in 1935, four years later, and the life does not reconcile the two figures. The two took one room together, and Nadezhda, as the younger, took Antonina onto her keep; she found work as a cleaner in a school. They lived wholly given to the service of God and kept the whole rule, but after the exile they were careful and tried not to form ties with unbelievers. In her duties Nadezhda was conscientious in the Christian way, and when the NKVD afterwards required the headmaster to write a character sheet on her he wrote that she had a good attitude to her work, worked conscientiously and had had no reprimand for it, and that in the school she behaved quietly and warily. In the authorities' understanding the monastic life was itself the crime, and the secretary of the town soviet wrote in her character sheet that she had been deported before for being a nun.

The two were arrested again on 1 March 1938 and held in the Yegoryevsk prison. Antonina was told that the organs of the investigation knew she was set against the Soviet power and slandered the power and the collective farms, and she denied it; Nadezhda was questioned in the same days with the same charges and the same questions, and she also rejected everything brought against her. Then the duty witnesses were questioned — the owners and the neighbours of the house where the two lived — and they signed the false testimony the NKVD had composed. The investigation closed on 8 March, and on 11 March a troika sentenced both to be shot. They were taken to the Taganka prison in Moscow, shot on 20 March 1938 and buried in an unmarked common grave at the Butovo range outside Moscow, which civil day is the Julian 7 March the calendar keeps them on.
