# Gheorghe of Cernica

Gheorghe the archimandrite, or Gheorghe the Transylvanian as some records call him, was a Romanian of Transylvania who shepherded the community of monks at the monastery of Cernica from 1 September 1781 until 3 December 1806, when the Lord called him to the things that are eternal. From the commemoration list he left, and from the notes of those who have studied his life, he was born in 1730 to believing parents of good family at Săliște near Sibiu, an old Romanian settlement of Transylvania first named in a document of 1354.

Born with the bent of soul that serves God and men by a high spiritual life, and with that bent cultivated in his own family, at nineteen he left his father's house for Bucharest, carrying in his heart the Saviour's word to the rich young man: if you would be perfect, go, sell what you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; then come and follow me. From there his road led to the Holy Mountain, where the day and night labours of this zealous Romanian monk, and his struggle with himself and with the temptations of so hard a life, can scarcely be told.

On Athos he resolved to enter the little community of the elder Paisius, beside the Moldavian and Slav monks whom that humble and enlightened man was guiding on the way of salvation; it was about the year 1752. After ten more years of praiseworthy labours on Athos, the hieroschemamonk Gheorghe moved to Moldavia together with the elder Paisius and sixty-four of his disciples, who settled at the monastery of Dragomirna in northern Moldavia and stayed there from 1763 to 1775.

Passing later through Bucharest he met at the metropolitanate an old friend, the hieromonk Macarie, also a disciple of Paisius, who took him in and after a few days presented him to Metropolitan Gregory II. Though he was then fifty-one, being full of modesty he thought himself unequal to so responsible a charge; but having taken counsel, he accepted, and chose for his community the abandoned and deserted monastery of Cernica, to the great astonishment of the metropolitan, who had expected him to ask for one of the largest and most famous houses. In his charter the metropolitan confessed that since taking the see in 1760 he had greatly desired to have coenobitic monasteries in his diocese and had been able to do nothing until God sent him the elder Gheorghe, and that he had given him Cernica so as to bring in fathers of the common life and set there the rules and orders of the Holy Mountain.

In very hard conditions, in the isolation of the forest, helped by two old disciples, Atanasie and Gherasim, he cleared the ground of thorns and drove out the wild beasts. There were no cells left except the ruined houses of the boyar Cernica beside the church, levelled to the ground; only a vaulted cellar stood, and in winter the brethren sheltered there at night. Yet in four years, from 1781 to 1785, the reopened monastery numbered fifty-four disciples; and the faithful, stirred by the teacher Macarie and by their reverence for Saint Nicholas, to whom the house was dedicated, began to come and to help it with what it needed. Gheorghe then began to build two ranges of cells, and served the Divine Liturgy himself on the feasts of the Lord, the seven hours being kept on ordinary weekdays.

Guided by the practical rule of monastic life summed up in the maxim "pray and work", these fathers turned a wilderness into a house of example. Word of the holiness and the industry of Cernica soon reached the prince Nicolae Mavrogheni, who came himself to see the tireless labourer and, prizing his exemplary life, wrote in a charter that he had himself seen the elder's life and the ordering of the fathers who lived the hermit's life with him. The prince entrusted him with the monastery of Curtea de Argeș beside Cernica and recognised his rank of archimandrite in 1788; two years later he received the charge of restoring the spiritual and material life of Căldărușani as well, and under his wise government both houses were wholly renewed. At Cocioc, in the place of the thorns and the reeds, he planted trees, raised a church that stands to this day, and built cells for the fathers who wanted more stillness.

He was bound in soul to all his spiritual children, guiding with the same fatherly love the monks of the two houses he had reorganised and the good Christians who sought salvation. Weakened by suffering, by fasting and by prayer, at the beginning of December 1806 he called all the monks into the church, and there, in the midst of his brethren and disciples, he took his leave. Saint [Calinic](/saints/calinic-of-cernica) set down in a commemoration list of the monastery, with great esteem: "1806, on 3 December: there reposed Father Gheorghe, archimandrite of this community, a most venerable man, of middle stature, a Romanian of Ardeal."

His passing brought much grief and weeping to the communities of Căldărușani and Cernica. He was buried in the cemetery he had himself made about the church of Saint Lazarus which he had built, and he left a testament in which are gathered the principles by which he had strengthened the two communities; he meant it to remain a rule of the monastic life, and in it the monastic struggle is divided into seven steps — obedience, so that nothing be done without the guide's blessing; confession three times a week to the fathers the council had chosen; watchfulness against the devil's whisper that one is better before God than another; frequent communion, no month passing unpartaken of the divine Mysteries; and further counsels given, as he said, with the tongue of a dying man.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, 3 December, and tells his story the way a Greek source would. He was born in 1730 at Săliște near Sibiu in Transylvania, saint.gr says, and the persecutions of the Orthodox and of the Carpathian monasteries by the Uniates forced him over the Carpathians into Wallachia. His great wish was for the Holy Mountain, but he was poor and had no means; and at Bucharest he met a metropolitan who took him to Constantinople, where he stayed three years, and who afterwards took him with him to the monastery of Vatopedi, where he was clothed and ordained deacon.

When that metropolitan died he became the disciple of the abbot [Paisius](/saints/paisius-of-neamt), then labouring at the skete of the Prophet Elias near Pantokrator, and he followed him with the whole brotherhood into the Romanian lands — to Dragomirna, then Secu, then Neamț. Wanting afterwards to return to Athos he stopped at Bucharest with an old friend, the hieromonk Macarius, another of Paisius's disciples, who presented him to Gregory II, metropolitan of Ungro-Wallachia; and the metropolitan, seeing so spiritual a man, thought him sent from God and begged him to stay in whatever monastery he chose and make a brotherhood.

After much fasting and labour, the page says, Saint Nicholas appeared to him and told him to stay and to clean his house of the wild beasts. The next day he asked father Macarius whether he knew of some abandoned skete of Saint Nicholas, and Macarius remembered with difficulty the skete of Cernica — built on a little island in 1608 and abandoned for an unknown reason, the place entirely deserted. They found the ruined church through a thick wood, and going in to venerate the icons they saw a fearful snake in the sanctuary; and the venerable father said to it mildly, «Μικρούλη μου, μέχρι τώρα έμεινες εσυ εδώ, τώρα είναι η σειρά μας να μείνουμε» — little one, until now you have stayed here, now it is our turn to stay — and to everyone's astonishment the snake left. By 1784 the monastery had a hundred and thirty monks. He fell asleep on 3 December 1806, at the seventh hour of the morning.
