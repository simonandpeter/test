# Tatiana of Rome

Tatiana was born in old Rome of parents of great family. Her father had been three times proconsul, and was a believing Christian who kept the holy faith in secret and raised his daughter in it, in reverence and the fear of God, teaching her the divine books. Come of age and unwilling to take a husband, she spent her life in virginity and purity, for she was made a bride of Christ and wounded with love of him, and served him day and night, wearing down the body with fasting and prayer. For that life she was counted worthy of a service in the Church, and served God as the angels do, though she was in the body: she was a deaconess of the Church of Rome.

The lawless emperor Antoninus Elagabalus (218-222) was killed by the Romans, his body dragged through the city in mockery and thrown into the Tiber, and after him the boy Alexander Severus (222-235) was raised to the throne, being only sixteen. His mother Julia Mamaea was a Christian and from her he had learned to honour Christ, but not in right faith, for he did not put the idols away: he kept in his palace the image of Christ beside that of Apollo, and Abraham beside Orpheus. He did not persecute; but the proconsuls and prefects who governed the empire in his name did, and first among them was Ulpian, prefect of the city, harsh by nature and a great enemy of the Christians.

Tatiana was seized and brought into the temple of Apollo to bow to it. She prayed to Christ instead, and at once an earthquake threw the idol down and broke it to pieces; part of the temple fell and killed many of the unbelievers and their priests, and the demon that lived in the idol fled howling through the air, so that all heard it go. They took her to judgment and to torture: they struck her face, tore at her eyes with hooks, and beat her so long that the torturers failed before she did, for angels standing unseen beside her struck the men who struck her, and they begged the judge to be allowed to stop. She prayed for them; a heavenly light showed them four angels about her, and eight of them fell down asking her forgiveness, confessed Christ, and were beheaded.

On the days that followed they cut her body with razors, and milk ran from the wounds instead of blood, with a sweet smell as from a vessel of spices; they stretched her on the ground in the form of a cross and beat her with staves until nine of the servants died under blows no one could see. They hung her up and tore her with iron claws. Taken to the temple of Artemis, she made the sign of the cross and prayed, and thunder and fire from heaven burnt the temple, the idol and its priests together. A lion loosed on her in the arena fawned at her feet. The fire they threw her into would not burn her. All of it her judges called sorcery, and they cut off her hair, thinking her power lay in it, and shut her in the temple of Jupiter; on the third day they opened it and found their idol fallen and crumbled to dust.

The judge, at the end of what he could think to do, sentenced her to death, and her head was cut off; and her father's with hers, for they had proved him a Christian, stripped him of his office, taken his goods and condemned him to the sword. Father and daughter took the crowns of martyrdom together.

The Greek calendar keeps her on the same day. saint.gr sets her at Rome in the time of the emperor Alexander Severus, whom it dates 222 to 235, and says her father had served as consul; that she held the church office of deaconess, and that in the hymnography she is presented as a disciple of the apostles Peter and Paul. Neither her birth nor the plainness of her faith could be borne by the pagans, who could not accept that such a woman despised worldly ambition and scorned the idols. They cast her on a fire and she suffered nothing; they threw her to starving wild beasts and the beasts did not dare to harm her; at the last they cut off her honourable head. The page adds under its heading for relics that her skull is at the monastery of Curtea de Argeș in Romania.
