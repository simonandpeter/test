# Demetrius (Vdovin)

Demetrius was born on 3 February 1883 in the town of Kolomna in the Moscow province, to Michael and Lyubov Vdovin; his father was a small trader. He finished the town school at Kolomna and went to work as a shop assistant. In 1910 he married Nadezhda, a merchant's daughter, and from 1912 he kept a petty trade of his own, in which he was both the owner and the man behind the counter.

After the revolution, which made unbelief the faith the state asked of its citizens, and after the breaking up of the country's economy, he took work as a supply agent and a clerk. When small trade was allowed again he became owner of a quarter share in a shop dealing in crockery and wooden ware, and from 1927 he made and sold sieves and riddles. In 1930 he had to give up the craft altogether and take a post in a state organisation.

Slight as his own means were, he was stripped of the vote as a trader. On 15 April 1935 the authorities of Kolomna ordered everyone so disenfranchised to leave the district within ten days, and from 25 April he lived in the town of Ozyory. He pressed hard to have the decision set aside, and on 9 March 1936 his petition was granted and he came home. In 1937 he was a supply agent for the district board of local industry; in 1928 and 1929 he had been a warden of one of the churches of Kolomna.

In 1937 the country's leadership required of the NKVD a set number of arrests in every town, district and region, and the Kolomna office set about it. Every priest and church servant still at liberty in the district was taken, and some of the laity with them; Demetrius was arrested on 22 August 1937. In the Kolomna prison the investigator charged him with being discontented with the Soviet power. He had nothing to be discontented with, he answered: he lived not badly, he worked, he had three good children and they worked. The investigator pressed him — he had traded, his business had been broken up, how could he be content?

The next day the charge was agitation against the Soviet power. He asked when and where such agitation was supposed to have happened. Well, where, said the investigator; possibly in Moscow — he was a supply agent, and being in Moscow on business he could have agitated. Two nights later he was called up again and told to give evidence about his church activity. He had been a member of the church council in 1928 and 1929, he said, and had not been one since 1929, but had remained an adherent of the Tikhonite allegiance to that day.

He was then told he was arrested as a member of a counter-revolutionary church-monarchist organisation. He denied it categorically; he was told he was lying and that the investigation held evidence against him; he said a second time that he had never been a member of such an organisation, and a third time that he had never carried on counter-revolutionary activity anywhere. Having read the record and signed it, he demanded to be brought face to face with the false witness, and on 28 August that was done; the witness repeated everything, and Demetrius said he held it false and asked the man how he could do such a thing, or whether he did not think of dying — to which the man answered that Demetrius would have done the same in his place.

On 1 September 1937 he was taken to the Taganka prison in Moscow, where he again refused to give the evidence demanded of him, and on 9 October an NKVD troika sentenced him to ten years in a corrective labour camp. He reached Bamlag on 16 November. He was set to felling timber, then fell gravely ill, and after the camp hospital was certified an invalid unfit for work and sent to the central workshops of the railway construction. By January 1939 he was in the Far Eastern Krai, at Izvestkovaya station in the settlement of Kuldur, sewing in the workshop of the fifty-third column.

His letters home are what the life has of his own voice, and they are almost all consolation sent the wrong way. In December 1939 he thanked them for their care and said he was walking without crutches now, with a stick; he wrote little of his legs, he said, not because he was sparing of words but because he would rather not write of what was bad, though there was no good to write of either. In January 1940 he answered his wife's plea not to give way to despondency: he knew well enough that grief mends nothing, his spirit was cheerful if the flesh was somewhat weak, the men on his column were good and those over him kindly and more attentive to him than he thought he deserved, and she was to send neither parcels nor money, for the cost was great and his need was not.

He was glad, he wrote in the same letter, that his children were friends with one another, for that is the best thing in a life, and a family at one with itself bears its joys more cheerfully and its troubles more lightly. He petitioned the Supreme Prosecutor of the RSFSR in January 1940 to review the case, and was given the standard answer that there were no grounds; he petitioned the NKVD in September 1940 to call up his file and quash the troika's decision, and nothing came of it. In November 1940 he wrote that he was alive and comparatively well, that winter was taking its rights and the frosts were sharp, and that the sun shone brightly on the good and the evil alike.

The war began on 22 June 1941 and all contact between prisoners and their families stopped; the last letter they had of his was sent on 28 May. Demetrius Vdovin died on 23 April 1942 in the Sredne-Belsky corrective labour camp and was buried in a grave nobody marked. The Sretensky calendar's own line for the day gives none of it — «Мч. Димитрия (1942).», a baptismal name and a year — and the index entry behind it adds the surname and one other day, the synaxis of the new martyrs. The day page prints three hymns and not one of them under his name.
