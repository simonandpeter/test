# Kiaran (Ciarán) of Ireland

Kiaran — Ciarán, called a lamp shining with the light of knowledge — was
born in 512 and grew up in Connacht in Ireland, the son of a builder of
chariots, one of eight children of whom at least two others took the
religious life. He had a love of animals, and kept a fox for a pet. He left
home as a boy driving a cow before him to pay for his keep, and went to
study under Finnian of Clonard, becoming one of the “twelve apostles of
Ireland” with Columba of Iona, Ninnidh of Lough Erne and Brendan the Voyager
among the rest. The story is told that while the students were on the Gospel
of Matthew, Ninnidh came to class without his book and asked Kiaran for his,
and got it; so when Finnian tested the class Kiaran knew only the first half
of the Gospel, and the others laughed and called him Ciarán half-Matthew —
not Ciarán half-Matthew, said Finnian, silencing them, but Ciarán
half-Ireland, for he will have half the country and the rest of us the other
half. After Clonard he visited other monasteries, among them Enda’s on Aran,
where he was ordained priest and which he left on a vision that Enda
interpreted for him; then Scattery Island under Senan; then his brothers
Luachaill and Odhran at Isel, whose charity was not equal to his — brother,
leave us, they said, for we cannot live in one place with you and feed and
keep our brethren for God, because of your unbounded lavishness. He went off
with his books in a bag, met a stag on the road, laid the bag on its back
and followed it to Lough Ree opposite Hare Island, where he founded a
monastery, left his brother Donnan as its abbot, and went to live in the
wilderness. With nine companions he founded another monastery at
Clonmacnoise on the Shannon, and within seven months fell ill; he asked to
be carried outside and laid on the ground, looked up to heaven, said
something of the way being steep and hard, and departed to the Lord at
thirty-three. Clonmacnoise was a thousand years old when Henry VIII
suppressed it and the armies of the Reformation destroyed it in 1552; its
ruins stand — a cathedral, seven other churches, three high crosses and the
stumps of two round towers — with fifty kings said to lie there among the
abbots and monks, and his crozier survives. The Greek synaxarion keeps him
on 9 September with the one line that he was born in 512 in Connacht, and
the Orthodox Church in America keeps him the same day with this life.
