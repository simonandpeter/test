# Tikhon of Moscow

Tikhon was Vasily Ivanovich Bellavin at his baptism, born on 19 January 1865. His father, John Bellavin, was a country priest in the Toropets district of the Pskov diocese, and his childhood and youth were spent in direct contact with peasants and their work. From his first years the boy showed a particular bent for religion, a love of the Church, and an uncommon gentleness and humility.

While he was still a child his father was given a revelation about each of his sons. One night, when he and the boys were sleeping in the hayloft, he woke suddenly and woke his three sons as well: his own mother, long dead, had appeared to him in a dream and foretold that his death was near, and what would become of the children — one would be unhappy all his life, one would die young, and the third, Vasily, would become a great man. The prophecy proved exact in every part.

Between 1878 and 1883 he studied at the theological seminary at Pskov, where his fellow students liked and respected him for his piety, for his progress in study and for his constant readiness to help, which usually took the form of explaining lessons and of drafting and correcting their many compositions; they called him, among themselves, the bishop and the patriarch. In 1888, at twenty-three, he finished the theological academy at Saint Petersburg, and returned to the Pskov seminary as a teacher of moral and dogmatic theology.

He lived austerely and chastely, and in 1891, at twenty-six, took the monastic vows. The whole neighbourhood came to the tonsure to show its love for his work, and the gentle and humble young man received the name Tikhon in honour of Saint Tikhon of Zadonsk. In 1892 he moved from the Pskov seminary to the seminary at Kholm and was raised to archimandrite; on 19 October 1897 he was consecrated bishop of Lublin and returned to Kholm for a year as vicar bishop of that diocese, giving all his time to the ordering of the new see and winning the affection not only of the Russians but of the Lithuanians and the Poles.

On 14 September 1898 he was appointed bishop of the Aleutians — the chain of islands in the frozen north of the Pacific, between Alaska and Kamchatka — and of Alaska. Working to spread the Orthodox faith in North America, he reorganised the diocese and in 1900 changed its name to the diocese of the Aleutians and North America; and he became so loved among clergy and laity that the Americans made him an honorary citizen of the United States.

On 22 May 1901 he laid the foundation stone of the cathedral of Saint Nicholas in New York, and had a hand in the building of other churches; on 9 November 1902 he consecrated the church of Saint Nicholas in Brooklyn for the Orthodox immigrants from Syria, and two weeks later the cathedral in New York. The parishes grew from fifteen to seventy, all of them self-supporting, and with his blessing the Orthodox prayer books were translated into English.

In 1905 the American mission was raised to an archdiocese and Tikhon was made archbishop, with two vicar bishops to help him: Innocent (Pustynsky) in Alaska and Saint Raphael (Hawaweeny) in Brooklyn. A particular achievement of his was the bringing of all the groups — Russian, Greek, Syrian, Bulgarian, Aleut — under one governance, and the sign of that unity was the first council of the Orthodox Church in America, which he called in February 1907 at Mayfield, New York. In June 1905 he blessed the founding of the monastery in Pennsylvania named for Saint Tikhon of Zadonsk.

In 1907 he returned to Russia as archbishop of Yaroslavl, where his humility and loving affection quickly won the trust of thousands; and when he was preparing to leave for the mission in Lithuania at the end of 1913, the Yaroslavl authorities made him an honorary citizen of the city on 22 December. After the transfer to Vilnius he carried on the same missionary work, and the outbreak of the First World War found him in the Lithuanian capital, where he spared no effort for those in need, and his charity moved many of his countrymen.

After the February revolution and the forming of a new synod of the Russian Church he became one of its members; on 21 June 1917 the bishops' synod elected him archbishop, and on 15 August 1917 he was raised to metropolitan of Moscow. A few months later he was chosen patriarch from among three candidates — Antony of Kharkov, the wise; Arseny of Novgorod, the just; and Tikhon, the gentle, as the members of the council described them. On 5 November, after the Liturgy in the cathedral of Christ the Saviour, he was enthroned.

When the atheist Bolshevik regime took control of the country, the Church's property was confiscated and the Russian Church suffered many persecutions. Tikhon openly condemned the killing of the tsar's family in 1918 and protested against the violent Bolshevik attacks on the Church; he called the Orthodox of Russia to hold together and strengthen their traditions, and, to avoid further persecution, issued a message forbidding the clergy to make any political declaration. On 19 January 1918 he anathematised all those who, being baptised, had killed the innocent with the communist horde, and three days later, on 22 January, the council confirmed the anathema.

In the summer of 1921 famine took the Volga region, and in August he appealed to the Russian people and to the people of the world to help its victims, blessing the priests to give away valuables the Church held, on the condition that only things not used directly in the services should go. On 23 February 1922 the Central Executive Committee decreed that all the valuables in the churches would be confiscated; by the seventy-third apostolic canon that was sacrilege, and the patriarch protested publicly against a total confiscation, the more so since many doubted the valuables would be used against the famine at all. The forced seizure raised indignation across Russia.

His message was treated as sabotage, and for it he was arrested in 1922 and spent the last years of his life under house arrest in the Donskoy monastery in Moscow; on 16 May 1922 all the patriarchal duties were handed to Metropolitan Agathangel of Yaroslavl. As the persecutions went on he began to feel overwhelmed, and his strength and health gave way. On Sunday 5 April 1925 he served his last Liturgy, and died two days later.

On 7 April he received Metropolitan Peter, and towards evening, after resting, at a quarter to midnight he departed to the Lord, making the sign of the cross and saying: Glory to thee, O Lord, glory to thee. More than a million of the faithful took part in the funeral at the cathedral of the Donskoy monastery, and from that time he was held to be a martyr. He was canonised by the bishops' council of the Russian Orthodox Church on 9 October 1989; for nearly seventy years his relics were thought lost, and in February 1992 they were found in a hidden place in the Donskoy monastery, where they are now held in great honour.
