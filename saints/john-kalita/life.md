# John Kalita

John, called Kalita, was the son of [Daniel](/saints/daniel-of-moscow), prince of Moscow, whose memory saint.gr puts on 4 March, and he was born about the year 1290. His name appears first in the liturgical Menaia of Novgorod between 1296 and 1297, where we read that when the people of that city called Daniel of Moscow to take its throne he sent them his son John; probably no later than 1299 John left Novgorod and came back to Moscow. After his father's death in 1303 he was obliged at first to support his brother George, whom he later succeeded as prince of Moscow, in the future capital's quarrel with its rival city of Tver; and the favourable outcome of that quarrel, which the page ascribes to John's political skill, was to settle Moscow's final supremacy.

With a first victory over the forces of Tver, which had taken the city of Pereyaslavl, he recovered it in 1304 or 1305. Between 1320 and 1326, on the occasion of the marriages of the rulers' daughters, he made alliances with the princes of Rostov, Belozersk and Yaroslavl, banding them together against Tver. On 15 August 1327 the khan Uzbek's representative Kolkhan was killed at Tver in a popular rising led by the holy prince Alexander Mikhailovich; Alexander fled to Pskov, and Tver was taken and given to Constantine Mikhailovich, the husband of a niece of John's. In 1329 John sent his army against Pskov, the metropolitan Theognostus anathematised its people for sheltering prince Alexander, and Alexander was forced to settle first in Livonia and later in Lithuania. In 1331 John obtained from the khan the title of grand prince. Matters did not rest: in 1338 Alexander of Tver won the khan Uzbek's pardon and came back to his throne, and in 1339 John went to the Golden Horde and accused him of plotting against the khan, so that Alexander and his son Theodore were brought there under accusation and tried; and to humble Tver, John took the bells from the cathedral of the Saviour and carried them to Moscow.

During his rule, the page says, a precious manuscript was issued, known as the Sijskoe Evangelie, in which a panegyric was written on justice and peace in the Russian land, and the whole fabric of the Kremlin was built in stone. On 4 August 1326, following the counsel of the aged metropolitan Peter, John ventured on the building of the cathedral of the Dormition of the Theotokos, so that Moscow should carry on the tradition of the former capital, Vladimir, where devotion and honour to the person of the Mother of God had been particularly cultivated. He kept close ties with metropolitan Peter and, immediately after his death, set the process of his canonisation going by sending the Synod of Vladimir, in 1327, a letter recording the wonders worked at the metropolitan's tomb.

He fell asleep in peace on 31 March 1340, or else 1341, having already become a monk with the name Ananias, and his holy body was buried in the cathedral of the Archangel in the Kremlin. A religious reverence had grown up around him already in his lifetime, the page says, and he is presented as a defender of Orthodoxy and as a just and philanthropic ruler. The Paterikon of the monastery of Volokolamsk, of the sixteenth century, carries the account of the abbot of Borovsk, according to whom a nun saw in a vision the figure of St John in the glory of Paradise, taking treasures out of his purse — his kalita — and distributing them to the poor.
