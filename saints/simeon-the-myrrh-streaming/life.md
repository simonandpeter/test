# Simeon the Myrrh-streaming

Simeon the Myrrh-streaming, called before that Stefan Nemanja, is a saint of Serbian birth who lived, doxologia says, between the years 1113 and 1199. His life divides into two great periods: the first princely, in which he was grand župan of the Serbs, and the second monastic, in which he founded the monastery of Hilandar and was tonsured under the name Simeon. The Orthodox Church keeps him on 13/26 February.

At the beginning of the twelfth century the Serbs lived in several separate states. His father, prince Zavida of Zahumlje, came of the ruling house of Raška, the chief of the Serbian states; after a quarrel with his brother he was sent to the principality of Duklja, a Serbian state of small importance. Stefan Nemanja was born about 1113 at Ribnica in the region of Zeta, now Podgorica in Montenegro; and because the place of his father's exile lay under the jurisdiction of the Western Church, the child was baptised by a Roman Catholic priest. When Zavida returned to Raška he took him to Kallinikos, bishop of Ras, who received him into the Orthodox Church by canonical order, in the church of the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul at Ras.

In 1163 the great prince of Raška was replaced by Stefan Tihomir, Nemanja's eldest brother, at the will of the Byzantine emperor Manuel I Komnenos, and the other brothers, Nemanja among them, became secondary princes in smaller regions. Nemanja was appointed over Ibar, Toplica, Rasina and Reke, and the emperor later entrusted him with Dubočica as well and a higher imperial rank; he had met the emperor several times before that, and his wife Ana is believed to have been of Byzantine origin, for which the emperor treated them with much sympathy. Towards the end of her life Ana too embraced the monastic life under the name Anastasia, and the Church keeps her on 22 June.

After his death God gave his relics the great gift of streaming myrrh; in the last three centuries, the page says, for our sins they have not streamed myrrh but only a wonderful fragrance. Meanwhile in Serbia war had broken out between Stefan and Vukan, the two sons of Nemanja left in the government, and for this reason their brother, the youngest son, [Sava](/saints/sava-of-serbia), by then archbishop of Serbia, brought the honoured relics of Simeon into the country and laid them in the monastery of Studenica; and by God's mercy he succeeded in reconciling his brothers beside their father's tomb.

When the relics were moved to Serbia, out of his grave at the monastery of Hilandar there grew, wonderfully and without seed, a vine which is kept to this day; and by tradition its grapes have the gift of healing barren wombs, for the bearing of children. Later the bodies of his two sons, [Stefan](/saints/stefan-the-first-crowned) and Vukan, were brought to Studenica as well, and the body of king Radoslav, Simeon's grandson; and after her canonisation the honoured relics of Saint Anastasia, his wife, first called Ana, were brought there too. Sava himself passed to eternity on 13/26 January 1236 at Tŭrnovo, and in 1237 his relics were taken to the monastery of Mileševa, from which in 1594 the Turks stole and burned them on the hill of Vračar in Belgrade.

The Greek calendar keeps him on the same day, as the founder of the monastery of Chilandari on the Holy Mountain. saint.gr names him in the world Stephen I Nemanja — Neeman in the Byzantine sources — king of the Serbs and father of [Sava](/saints/sava-of-serbia), the first archbishop of Serbia; it has him resign the throne in 1196 and go to the Holy Mountain, where he built Chilandari with his son, and fall asleep in peace in 1200, buried on the south side of the monastery's katholikon. In 1208 Sava decided to take up his father's relics and carry them home, and on the day of the translation abundant fragrant myrrh poured from them and went on flowing for some days afterwards from the emptied tomb — which is how he came to be called the Myrrh-streaming. Sava laid the relics in his father's monastery of repentance, Studenica, where they are kept still. The tomb at Chilandari has a silver cover with reliefs today, and out of it, after the translation, a vine sprang of itself without being planted, for the consolation of the monastery's fathers; its grapes, the page says, heal the barrenness of childless women.
