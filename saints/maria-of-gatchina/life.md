# Maria of Gatchina

Maria was a nun at Gatchina, a small town some fifty kilometres from Petrograd, known to everyone there for its gardens, its parks and its palaces. The account doxologia.ro prints is Ivan Andreev's, from his *Saints of the Russian Catacombs*, and it says the revolution of 1917 found her already on her bed of suffering. She had had encephalitis and then Parkinson's disease: her body was wholly still, as though shackled, her bloodless face like a mask, and she could speak only slowly and half under her breath, through her teeth.

Andreev, who was a physician, sets down that the disease commonly brings irritability, a wearying stubbornness, an exaggerated self-regard, and that such patients often end in asylums. Mother Maria, completely disabled in body, did not decline in mind at all. She grew submissive, humble and obedient, asking nothing, gathered into herself and absorbed in unceasing prayer, and carried the hard illness without the least murmur.

He visited her in March 1927, in the wooden house on the edge of the town. Among the photographs in her waiting room he noticed two: Metropolitan Veniamin of Petrograd, the new martyr, who had written to her only that she had consoled him, a sinner, among many who were afflicted; and Metropolitan Iosif, soon to lead the “Josephite” movement, who had inscribed his at length.

Andreev watched people come out of her room. A young man who had fallen into despair at his priest father's arrest and exile left her smiling and resolved to be ordained deacon; a suffering young woman left radiant and resolved to become a nun; an elderly man grieving for his son left with his head up; an old woman who had come in tears went away calm and firm.

He told her of a depression that fell on him for weeks at a time and that he could find no way out of. Despondency is a spiritual cross, she answered, sent to the penitent who does not know how to repent, and there are only two medicines for it: either to learn to repent and to offer the fruits of repentance, or else to carry the cross with humility, obedience, patience and great thanksgiving, for the Lord reckons the carrying of it as the fruit. He wrote that her words were like an operation on his soul, and that he went away another man.

About 1930 she was arrested, charged under paragraphs 10 and 11 of article 58 with counter-revolutionary propaganda and with belonging to a counter-revolutionary organisation. The organisation, Andreev says, was two people, and the propaganda was her gift of consoling those in suffering; the charge was aggravated by her refusal to recognise Metropolitan Sergius after the Declaration of 1927. Two Chekists dragged the paralysed woman from her bed by her arms, twisted behind her, across the floor and the ground to a lorry, and threw her in. Her brother was taken away in another car.

Parcels were accepted at the prison for a month. Then they were refused, with the words that she had died in hospital; the body was never given back. Her brother, a slight young man who had nursed her and received her visitors, was given five years in a Siberian camp after nine months of investigation. doxologia.ro's short notice adds that her relics are venerated in the cathedral of the Apostle Paul at Gatchina, in the Leningrad region, and prints her year of death with a query mark.

The Православный церковный календарь of the Сретенский monastery keeps her on 4 апреля ст. ст., in a line she shares with a bishop she has nothing to do with: «Сщмч. Николая , еп. Вельского, прмц. Марии (1932).» The name link behind her part of it, page 14417, gives «Мария (Лелянова), инокиня, прмц.», and azbyka.ru’s line for the same day names her as this folder does, «прмц. Марии Гатчинской, монахини (1932)», which is what identifies the two as one woman.

azbyka’s life fills in the beginning the Romanian notice did not have. She was born in 1874 in St Petersburg, the daughter of a sealing-wax manufacturer, a merchant, and was baptised Lidia; her father died when she was three and a half and she and her younger sister were left to their mother and elder brothers. She was at a girls’ gymnasium and shortly before finishing it fell ill of encephalitis, from which came Parkinson’s disease and then rheumatism and gout; she was carried to her final examinations in a wheelchair. Her family took her to famous doctors at home and abroad and nobody could help her.

In 1909, on the doctors’ advice, the family moved to Gatchina, and for twenty years she lay motionless on her back, any movement or touch bringing unbearable pain. She did not complain; she took the illness as the will of God and submitted to it entirely, and by those sufferings, the page says, she learnt to pray without ceasing. Her body and even her face were wholly still, but speech was left her: she spoke with her mouth half closed, as though through her teeth, slowly and separately so as to be understood.

The gifts of discernment and of consoling came with the patience, and people came in numbers for advice and for her prayers — laymen, and priests, and bishops. The metropolitan of Petrograd blessed her tonsure, and in 1922, in the Protection church of a women’s monastery’s metochion at Gatchina and before a great crowd, an archimandrite of the Александро-Невская Lavra clothed her in the mantle with the name Maria. Every day a crowd waited in the hall; many brought her food and money, and she gave it away to those in need.

A professor who knew her set down what he saw: a young man who had sunk into dejection after his priest father’s arrest and exile went out from her with a glad smile, having resolved to be ordained deacon; a young woman came in sad and went out in bright joy, resolved on the monastic life; an older man deep in grief for a dead son went out straightened and heartened; an older woman came in weeping and went out calm and firm. He asked her himself about a melancholy that would last him weeks, and her answer is kept: that melancholy is a spiritual cross, sent to help those who repent and do not know how; that there are two medicines only, to learn to repent and bring forth the fruits of repentance, or to carry the cross with humility, meekness, patience and great thankfulness, the carrying of it being reckoned by the Lord in place of the fruit.

The metropolitan who had blessed her tonsure came to her shortly before her arrest and gave her his photograph, writing on it: «Глубокочтимой страдалице матушке Марии, утешившей, среди многих скорбящих, и меня грешного». In the February of 1932 the arrests of monks and nuns of the closed monasteries began — seventy-six people in Leningrad in that operation — and the OGPU’s own papers say why she was in it: that the nuns of the liquidated metochion «группировались около так называемой матери Марии», who had lain on her back twenty years and was visited in great numbers by townspeople, peasants and people from other districts.

She was arrested on 19 February 1932. Two OGPU men came to the nun’s bed, took her by her twisted arms, dragged her across the floor and the ground, then swung her by the arms and legs into the back of a lorry. Her illness made prison impossible and she was put in the prison hospital, and questioned there. She said she held with the metropolitan who had led the opposition, and that she thought the deputy had been wrong to order prayer for the Soviet power: «ей это не нужно. И вообще, пусть за нее молится кто сам хочет… Я считаю, что за власть молиться нужно в том случае, если это власть.» The witnesses the OGPU questioned said with one voice that in the town and round about everyone held her a holy woman with the gift of discernment from God.

On 22 March 1932 she was sentenced to lose the right of living in a long list of regions and cities for three years, tied to whatever place she chose. **She died on 17 April 1932 in the prison hospital** and was buried in the Smolensk cemetery. 17 April 1932 is the civil date of the Julian 4 April, so this calendar keeps her on the day she died; its line closes «(1932)» and azbyka’s says the same. The Romanian notice this folder was written from gave 1930 with a query mark against it, and both years are now held.

azbyka adds what came after: that the Church Abroad numbered her among the New Martyrs in 1981 as «новопреподобномученица Мария Гатчинская»; that the Russian Church canonised her on 17 July 2006 at the petition of the St Petersburg diocese; and that her relics were uncovered in the Smolensk cemetery on 26 March 2007 and placed in the Paul cathedral at Gatchina — which is the cathedral the Romanian notice already named. It prints a troparion and a kontakion for her, both in глас 4 and both naming her, where neither of the other two books printed any hymn at all. Its further day beside this one, the moveable synaxis of the saints of the St Petersburg metropolia on 11 July, is a synaxis and is not written into the feast.
