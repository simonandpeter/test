# Isidore of Pelusium

Isidore was an Egyptian, the son of well-born and God-loving parents, and a kinsman of Theophilus, archbishop of Alexandria, and of [Saint Cyril](/saints/cyril-of-alexandria), who took the see after Theophilus. Having learned both the philosophy of the world outside and the divine wisdom, he left the glory of this world, its wealth and the brightness of a name, counting them all as refuse, and went to the mountain of Pelusium. There he received the monastic life and struggled well, in the days of the reign of Theodosius the Younger; he was a man perfect in goodness, a priest and an abbot, glorious in his life and his wisdom and honoured by all.

Evagrius the church historian speaks of him: "In the reign of Theodosius, Isidore of Pelusium was held in great honour, and the fame of his deeds and words had been heard far off and was praised by every mouth. He so wearied his body with labours that all could see he had an angelic life. Living in the manner of the monastic life and of the thought of God, he was always and before all an example to be followed, and he wrote many most useful words."

Nicephorus the historian bears witness too, saying that from his youth he sweated so much in monastic labours and so mortified his body, while warming his soul with hidden and high teachings, that all could see he led a life truly Christian; that he was a living and breathing pillar of the monastic rules and of the divine vision, a living example of the imitation and the teaching of the Spirit; and that many profitable works were written by him, but above all his letters to all sorts of people, full of divine grace and human wisdom, near ten thousand of them, in which he interprets the whole of divine Scripture and sets right the manners of all men.

From such testimonies, the life says, one sees how pleasing to God the venerable Isidore was, even though his life has not been found written out in detail; and it is enough that from these short words his great sanctity and wisdom are known, for he was to all a pattern of a bettered life and filled the whole world with his God-taught letters. He was a great supporter of [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom) when he was unjustly driven from his throne, and wrote much to Theophilus, archbishop of Alexandria, and to the emperor Arcadius on his behalf.

Much of what he taught is preserved in his counsel to the bishop Palladius on chastity: that if you go to women on some errand you should keep your eyes cast down, and teach those you have gone to to look with a full purity; that having said the few words that can strengthen and enlighten their souls you should leave at once, lest long talk soften and weaken your strength; and that if you would be honoured, as is fitting above all for a spiritual man, you should have no friendship at all with them. Some say that talking with them does no harm; but, he answers, stones are broken by the raindrops that keep falling on them — and what is harder than stone, or softer than water? If nature itself is worn down by such a thing, how shall the human will, which is easily moved, not be overturned?

So, the page says, in counselling the bishop Palladius he counsels us all to a life of whole wisdom, keeping not only the body from falling into sin but the soul whole from the thoughts that ruin it. He taught many good works to all, by his writings and by his speech; and having come to a great age and pleased God perfectly, he ended in peace. The page gives no year for his birth and none for his repose.
