# Copris (17 December)

saint.gr's calendar for 17 December keeps three venerable-martyrs together, Patermuthius, Copris and Alexander, and Copris is the second of them. The page says that they were Christians full of piety and zeal, that they were tortured and at last beheaded by order of Julian the Transgressor, whose years it gives as 361 to 363, and that this happened while Julian was on campaign against the Persians.

What it says of Copris is that he was a young man and that he lost his nerve a little at the start — and that [Patermuthius](/saints/patermuthius-17-december) strengthened him with his warm words, so that all three underwent the martyr's death chanting hymns to Christ; [Alexander](/saints/alexander-17-december) is the third. The page's epigram puts the thanks in Copris's own mouth: father Patermuthius, I have been made sharer with you of your life and of your sword.

That is the whole of the entry. The page names no city and no province, gives no year of birth, dates the martyrdom only by the reign, lists no relic and prints no hymn. The corpus keeps a venerable Copris on 24 September, of Palestine, who is not this young martyr.

The Ohrid Prologue keeps the two of them on the Julian 9 July, where the Greek calendar keeps them on 17 December — two calendars and two days, and both are recorded. Both were Egyptians, it says, tortured by the emperor Julian the Apostate; the first was seventy-five years old and this one forty-five, which are ages and not dates and are recorded nowhere as years. And of this one it tells a fall and a recovery no other book has. The emperor **succeeded** in turning him from the faith of Christ and winning him over to the idols, and he cried out: I am Julian’s and not Christ’s.

But when the elder Patermuthius rebuked him and warned him of the eternal torments, he started up and cried out before the emperor: I am Christ’s and not Julian’s. Both of them were then beheaded with the sword. With them suffered also a soldier of the emperor’s who, seeing their courage in suffering, became a Christian himself; his own folder stands on the Greek 17 December with theirs, and this reading writes him no Serbian row, because the heading of the entry names the two of them and not him. They suffered honourably for Christ and went to Christ, this book ends, in the year 361 — a year inside the interval the Greek page gives by Julian’s reign and his Persian campaign, so the folder’s recorded interval is left standing rather than narrowed to it.

This day prints the same two names twice. The entry that follows this one opens by saying that though of the same names, those were different men from these — two venerables and wonderworkers of the end of the fourth century, where these two are martyrs of Julian’s reign. The book makes the distinction itself, and this folder is the first pair’s.
